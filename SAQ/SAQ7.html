Problem 1 Symmetry of the derivative

Suppose \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) is differentiable at every point, so that we have \(f':\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\). Prove:

(a) If \(f\) is even then \(f'\) is odd.

(b) If \(f\) is odd then \(f'\) is even.

Key idea

“\(f\) is even” and “\(f\) is odd” are identities between two functions: \(f(-x)=f(x)\) and \(f(-x)=-f(x)\). Since both sides are differentiable functions of \(x\), we may differentiate both sides and equate. The only tool needed is the chain rule, applied to the composite \(x\mapsto f(-x)\).

Chain rule reminder. If \(u(x)=-x\), then \(u'(x)=-1\), and \[ \frac{d}{dx}\,f(-x)=\frac{d}{dx}\,(f\circ u)(x)=f'\bigl(u(x)\bigr)\,u'(x)=f'(-x)\cdot(-1)=-f'(-x). \]

(a) \(f\) even \(\Longrightarrow\) \(f'\) odd

Step 1 — Write down the hypothesis. \(f\) even means

\[ f(-x)=f(x)\qquad\text{for every }x\in\mathbb{R}. \]

Step 2 — Differentiate the left-hand side. Using the chain rule with \(u(x)=-x\),

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\,f(-x)=f'(-x)\cdot(-1)=-f'(-x). \]

Step 3 — Differentiate the right-hand side.

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\,f(x)=f'(x). \]

Step 4 — Equate. Two functions that agree for every real \(x\) have the same derivative at every real \(x\). Hence

\[ -f'(-x)=f'(x) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\,f'(-x)=-f'(x)\ \ \text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}\,} \]

and this is precisely the definition of \(f'\) being odd. \(\blacksquare\)

(b) \(f\) odd \(\Longrightarrow\) \(f'\) even

Step 1 — Hypothesis. \(f\) odd means

\[ f(-x)=-f(x)\qquad\text{for every }x\in\mathbb{R}. \]

Step 2 — Differentiate the left-hand side (chain rule, as above):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\,f(-x)=-f'(-x). \]

Step 3 — Differentiate the right-hand side. The derivative of \(-f(x)\) is \(-f'(x)\):

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\bigl(-f(x)\bigr)=-f'(x). \]

Step 4 — Equate.

\[ -f'(-x)=-f'(x) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\,f'(-x)=f'(x)\ \ \text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}\,} \]

That is exactly the statement that \(f'\) is even. \(\blacksquare\)

Why this is believable — the geometry

If \(f\) is even, its graph is a mirror image across the \(y\)-axis. The tangent line at \(x\) and the tangent line at \(-x\) are mirror images too, and mirroring across the \(y\)-axis flips the sign of a slope. So the slope at \(-x\) is the negative of the slope at \(x\): \(f'(-x)=-f'(x)\).

If \(f\) is odd, its graph is symmetric about the origin (a \(180^\circ\) rotation). Rotating by \(180^\circ\) about the origin preserves a slope, so \(f'(-x)=f'(x)\): the derivative is even.

Quick sanity checks

  • \(f(x)=x^2\) (even) \(\Rightarrow\) \(f'(x)=2x\), which is odd. ✓
  • \(f(x)=\cos x\) (even) \(\Rightarrow\) \(f'(x)=-\sin x\), which is odd. ✓
  • \(f(x)=x^3\) (odd) \(\Rightarrow\) \(f'(x)=3x^2\), which is even. ✓
  • \(f(x)=\sin x\) (odd) \(\Rightarrow\) \(f'(x)=\cos x\), which is even. ✓
Where the hypothesis is used. “Differentiable at every point” lets us differentiate the identity \(f(-x)=\pm f(x)\) at every \(x\), and it lets us use the chain rule at every \(x\). In Problem 2 we will only assume differentiability at the single point \(0\), and there the argument must be redone more carefully.
Problem 2 Even function at the origin

Suppose \(f\) is an even function which is differentiable at \(0\). Show that

\[ f'(0)=0. \]

Proof 1 — directly from the definition of the derivative

Step 1 — Write the derivative at \(0\). By definition,

\[ f'(0)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}. \]

Step 2 — Let \(h\to 0\) through negative values. As \(h\) runs over all nonzero numbers tending to \(0\), so does \(-h\); substituting \(h\mapsto -h\) does not change the limit:

\[ f'(0)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(-h)-f(0)}{-h}. \]

Step 3 — Use evenness. Since \(f(-h)=f(h)\),

\[ f'(0)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{-h} =-\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h} =-f'(0). \]

Step 4 — Conclude. From \(f'(0)=-f'(0)\),

\[ 2f'(0)=0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\,f'(0)=0\,} \]

\(\blacksquare\)

Proof 2 — as a corollary of Problem 1(a)

If \(f\) happens to be differentiable on all of \(\mathbb{R}\), Problem 1(a) says \(f'\) is odd. An odd function \(g\) satisfies \(g(-x)=-g(x)\); taking \(x=0\) gives

\[ g(0)=-g(0)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2g(0)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad g(0)=0. \]

With \(g=f'\) this is \(f'(0)=0\). This is the cleanest way to see it — but note that it uses differentiability everywhere, whereas the statement only assumes differentiability at \(0\). Proof 1 is the one that matches the hypothesis exactly.

Geometric meaning

An even function has a graph symmetric about the \(y\)-axis. At \(x=0\), the left-hand slope and the right-hand slope must therefore be negatives of each other. But differentiability at \(0\) says these two one-sided slopes are actually equal. The only number equal to its own negative is \(0\). Hence the tangent line at \(x=0\) is horizontal.

Contrast with odd functions. An odd differentiable function satisfies \(f(0)=0\), but nothing forces \(f'(0)=0\). Indeed \(f(x)=x\) is odd and \(f'(0)=1\). Evenness is what kills the slope at the origin.
Problem 3 Logarithmic differentiation

Let \(f\) take only positive values. Show that \(f\) is differentiable if and only if \(\log\circ f\) is differentiable.

(Precisely: \(f:I\to(0,\infty)\) on an interval \(I\), and the equivalence is meant pointwise / on all of \(I\).)

The two ingredients

(i) \(\log\) is differentiable on \((0,\infty)\), with \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\log x=\dfrac{1}{x}\), and \(\dfrac{1}{x}\neq 0\) there.

(ii) \(\exp\) is differentiable on all of \(\mathbb{R}\), with \(\dfrac{d}{dx}e^{x}=e^{x}\), and \(e^{x}\neq 0\) everywhere.

Also, \(\log\) and \(\exp\) are inverse to each other: \(\exp(\log y)=y\) for \(y>0\), and \(\log(e^{x})=x\).

Both directions are just the chain rule applied to a composition. The hypothesis “\(f\) takes only positive values” guarantees that \(\log\circ f\) is even defined, and that we never divide by zero.

Direction 1: \(f\) differentiable \(\Longrightarrow\) \(\log\circ f\) differentiable

Fix \(c\in I\). The function \(\log\) is differentiable at the point \(f(c)\), because \(f(c)>0\). The function \(f\) is differentiable at \(c\) by hypothesis. A composition of differentiable functions is differentiable, so \(\log\circ f\) is differentiable at \(c\), and the chain rule gives

\[ \boxed{\;(\log\circ f)'(c)=\frac{1}{f(c)}\,f'(c)=\frac{f'(c)}{f(c)}\;} \]

Since \(c\in I\) was arbitrary, \(\log\circ f\) is differentiable on \(I\). \(\blacksquare\)

Direction 2: \(\log\circ f\) differentiable \(\Longrightarrow\) \(f\) differentiable

Fix \(c\in I\). Because \(\log\circ f\) is differentiable at \(c\), it is in particular defined at \(c\), so \(f(c)>0\). Now use the inverse relationship

\[ f(x)=\exp\bigl(\log f(x)\bigr)=\bigl(\exp\circ(\log\circ f)\bigr)(x). \]

The exponential is differentiable at the point \(\log f(c)\in\mathbb{R}\) (indeed everywhere), and \(\log\circ f\) is differentiable at \(c\). Hence the composition \(f=\exp\circ(\log\circ f)\) is differentiable at \(c\), and the chain rule gives

\[ f'(c)=\exp\bigl(\log f(c)\bigr)\cdot(\log\circ f)'(c) =f(c)\cdot(\log\circ f)'(c). \]

Since \(c\in I\) was arbitrary, \(f\) is differentiable on \(I\). \(\blacksquare\)

Summary of the two formulas

\[ (\log\circ f)'=\frac{f'}{f}, \qquad\qquad f'=f\cdot(\log\circ f)'. \]

They are literally the same identity, read in two directions.

Why positivity is essential

  • If \(f(c)\le 0\) for some \(c\), then \(\log f(c)\) is undefined (for \(f(c)=0\)) or not real (for \(f(c)<0\)), so \(\log\circ f\) is not even a function near \(c\).
  • If \(f\) were allowed to vanish, the formula \((\log\circ f)'=f'/f\) would break down by division by zero.
  • Positivity also makes \(f\) nowhere zero, so the composition with \(\exp\) recovers \(f\) exactly.
Application (logarithmic differentiation). For a positive function \(f\), the derivative of \(\log f\) is \(f'/f\), which is called the logarithmic derivative. It is often far easier to compute than \(f'\) directly, and then one recovers \(f'=f\cdot(\log f)'\). Example: for \(f(x)=x^{x}\) (on \(x>0\)), \(\log f=x\log x\), so \((\log f)'=\log x+1\), and therefore \(f'(x)=x^{x}(\log x+1)\).
Problem 4 Polynomial vs. sine

Can there be a polynomial \(p(x)\) such that \(p(x)=\sin x\) on the interval \((-\pi,\pi)\)?

Answer

No. No polynomial agrees with \(\sin x\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\).

The key principle

If two differentiable functions agree on an open interval, then they have the same derivatives of every order on that interval. In particular, if a polynomial \(p\) agrees with \(\sin\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\), then for every \(k\ge 0\), \[ p^{(k)}(x)=(\sin)^{(k)}(x)\qquad\text{for all }x\in(-\pi,\pi). \]

Proof 1 — derivatives eventually vanish for a polynomial, but not for sine

Step 1 — Assume, for contradiction, that such a \(p\) exists.

\[ p(x)=\sin x\qquad\text{for all }x\in(-\pi,\pi). \]

Step 2 — Differentiate \(k\) times. Since \(p\) is a polynomial it is infinitely differentiable, and \(\sin\) is infinitely differentiable, so for every \(k\ge 0\),

\[ p^{(k)}(x)=(\sin)^{(k)}(x)\qquad\text{for all }x\in(-\pi,\pi). \]

Step 3 — Choose \(k>\deg p\). The \((k)\)-th derivative of a polynomial of degree \(n\) is identically zero whenever \(k>n\). So

\[ p^{(k)}(x)=0\qquad\text{for all }x\in(-\pi,\pi). \]

Combining with Step 2,

\[ (\sin)^{(k)}(x)=0\qquad\text{for all }x\in(-\pi,\pi). \]

Step 4 — Contradiction. The derivatives of sine cycle with period \(4\):

\[ \sin,\ \cos,\ -\sin,\ -\cos,\ \sin,\ \cos,\ -\sin,\ -\cos,\ \dots \] So \((\sin)^{(k)}\) is always one of \(\pm\sin\) or \(\pm\cos\). None of these is identically zero on \((-\pi,\pi)\): for instance \(\cos 0=1\neq 0\) and \(\sin\frac{\pi}{2}=1\neq 0\). Hence \((\sin)^{(k)}\not\equiv 0\), a contradiction. \(\blacksquare\)

Proof 2 — a differential-equation argument

Assume \(p(x)=\sin x\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\). Differentiate twice:

\[ p'(x)=\cos x,\qquad p''(x)=-\sin x=-p(x) \qquad\text{on }(-\pi,\pi). \]

Therefore the polynomial \(q(x):=p''(x)+p(x)\) satisfies

\[ q(x)=0\qquad\text{for all }x\in(-\pi,\pi). \]

A polynomial with infinitely many roots must be the zero polynomial. So \(q\equiv 0\) as a polynomial.

But if \(p(x)=a_nx^{n}+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0\) with \(n\ge 0\) and \(a_n\neq 0\), then \(p''\) has degree \(n-2\) (or is \(0\) if \(n\le 1\)), so the highest-degree term of \(p''+p\) is again \(a_nx^{n}\). Hence \(p''+p\) has degree \(n\ge 0\) with leading coefficient \(a_n\neq 0\) — it cannot be the zero polynomial. Contradiction. \(\blacksquare\)

Remarks and intuition

  • Why restricting to \((-\pi,\pi)\) does not rescue us. The obstruction is local: agreement on any nonempty open interval already forces all derivatives to match there. A polynomial simply cannot mimic the endless oscillatory derivative pattern of sine.
  • Agreement on a single point is fine. For example, the constant polynomial \(p(x)=0\) equals \(\sin x\) at \(x=0\). Agreement on an open set is what is impossible.
  • Comparison with Taylor polynomials. The Taylor polynomial \(T_{2n+1}(x)=x-\frac{x^3}{3!}+\cdots+\frac{(-1)^nx^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}\) agrees with \(\sin x\) in value and in the first \(2n+1\) derivatives at the single point \(0\) — but never on the whole interval. As \(n\to\infty\) the approximations converge to \(\sin\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\), but no finite one equals it.
  • Deeper reason. \(\sin\) has infinitely many real zeros (\(x=k\pi\), \(k\in\mathbb{Z}\)), while a nonzero polynomial has only finitely many. That argument needs a slightly larger domain, which is why the derivative argument above is the sharper one for the interval \((-\pi,\pi)\).
Problem 5 Squeezing an integral to zero

Let \(f,g:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}\) with \(0\le f(x)\le g(x)\) for every \(x\in[0,1]\), and \(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1} g(x)\,dx=0\). Then \(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1} f(x)\,dx=0\).

Setup

Assume \(f\) and \(g\) are Riemann integrable on \([0,1]\) (the statement speaks of the integrals, so both integrals exist). The proof is a two-line squeeze using monotonicity of the integral.

The two properties of the integral we use

(Monotonicity.) If \(u,v\) are Riemann integrable on \([0,1]\) and \(u(x)\le v(x)\) for all \(x\in[0,1]\), then \[ \int_{0}^{1}u(x)\,dx\;\le\;\int_{0}^{1}v(x)\,dx . \]

Proof

Step 1 — The integral of \(f\) is nonnegative.

Since \(f(x)\ge 0\) on \([0,1]\), every Riemann sum \(\sum_{i} f(t_i)\,\Delta x_i\) is a sum of nonnegative terms, hence is \(\ge 0\). Passing to the limit (or using that the lower sums are \(\ge 0\) and the integral is their supremum),

\[ \int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,dx\;\ge\;0 . \]

Step 2 — The integral of \(f\) is at most the integral of \(g\).

Since \(f(x)\le g(x)\) on \([0,1]\), monotonicity of the integral gives

\[ \int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,dx\;\le\;\int_{0}^{1}g(x)\,dx . \]

Step 3 — Use the hypothesis. The hypothesis says \(\int_{0}^{1}g=0\). Combining Steps 1 and 2,

\[ 0\;\le\;\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,dx\;\le\;\int_{0}^{1}g(x)\,dx\;=\;0 . \]

Step 4 — Conclude. A real number squeezed between \(0\) and \(0\) is \(0\):

\[ \boxed{\;\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,dx=0\;} \]

\(\blacksquare\)

Understanding what just happened

The hypothesis \(0\le f\le g\) makes \(f\) a “sandwich filling.” Since \(g\) is nonnegative and has zero integral, \(g\) carries no area at all; and \(f\), being trapped between \(0\) and \(g\), cannot carry any area either.

Finer consequences. Let \(g\ge 0\) be Riemann integrable with \(\int_{0}^{1}g=0\).
  • \(g\) must equal \(0\) at every point where \(g\) is continuous. (If \(g(c)>0\) at a continuity point \(c\), then \(g>g(c)/2\) on a small interval around \(c\), giving a positive integral — a contradiction.)
  • Consequently \(g=0\) “almost everywhere,” and since \(0\le f\le g\), the same holds for \(f\). In particular \(\int_{0}^{1}f=0\), as proved.
  • If in addition \(f\) is continuous on \([0,1]\), then \(f\equiv 0\) everywhere on \([0,1]\), not merely almost everywhere — because a continuous function that vanishes on a dense set vanishes identically.
Common mistake. Do not try to conclude \(g\equiv 0\) from \(\int g=0\) without extra assumptions (such as continuity). A nonnegative function can be nonzero at infinitely many points and still have zero integral; only continuity (or positivity at a continuity point) rules this out.
Problem 6 Nonzero derivative ⇒ local injectivity

Let \(f:(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}\) be differentiable at \(c\in(a,b)\). Assume that \(f'(c)\neq 0\). Show that there exists \(\delta>0\) such that for \(x\in(c-\delta,c+\delta)\cap(a,b)\) with \(x\neq c\), we have

\[ f(x)\neq f(c). \]

What the statement really says

It says that \(f\) is locally injective at \(c\): sufficiently close to \(c\), the function \(f\) never repeats the value \(f(c)\). Equivalently, the horizontal line \(y=f(c)\) meets the graph of \(f\) only at the point \((c,f(c))\), at least near \(c\).

(Note: at \(x=c\) we always have \(f(x)=f(c)\), so the condition must be read for \(x\neq c\).)

Proof — an \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) argument

Step 1 — Name the slope. Put

\[ L:=f'(c)\neq 0 . \]

Step 2 — Choose a radius smaller than \(|L|\). Since \(|L|>0\), the number

\[ \varepsilon:=\frac{|L|}{2}>0 \] is a legitimate choice of \(\varepsilon\).

Step 3 — Apply the definition of the limit. By definition of \(f'(c)=L\),

\[ L=\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}, \]

so for our chosen \(\varepsilon=|L|/2\) there exists \(\delta_1>0\) such that

\[ 0<|x-c|<\delta_1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left|\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}-L\right|<\frac{|L|}{2}. \]

Step 4 — Shrink \(\delta\) so the whole neighbourhood lies in \((a,b)\). Since \(c\in(a,b)\) is an interior point, there is \(\delta_2>0\) with \((c-\delta_2,c+\delta_2)\subseteq(a,b)\). Put

\[ \delta:=\min\{\delta_1,\delta_2\}>0 . \]

Step 5 — Use the reverse triangle inequality. For \(0<|x-c|<\delta\),

\[ \left|\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}\right| =\left|L+\left(\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}-L\right)\right| \ge |L|-\left|\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}-L\right| >|L|-\frac{|L|}{2}=\frac{|L|}{2}>0 . \]

Step 6 — Conclude. The quotient \(\dfrac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}\) has nonzero absolute value, so it is not zero. Since \(x-c\neq 0\), the numerator must be nonzero:

\[ f(x)-f(c)\neq 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\,f(x)\neq f(c)\,} \]

for every \(x\in(c-\delta,c+\delta)\cap(a,b)\) with \(x\neq c\). \(\blacksquare\)

The idea in one sentence

A nonzero derivative means the difference quotient is bounded away from zero near \(c\) — it stays at least \(|L|/2\) in absolute value — so the numerator \(f(x)-f(c)\) can never vanish while \(x\neq c\).

Why \(f'(c)\neq 0\) is necessary

If \(f'(c)=0\), the conclusion can fail badly:

  • \(f(x)=x^{2}\) at \(c=0\): \(f'(0)=0\), and \(f(x)=f(0)\) only at \(x=0\) — here the conclusion happens to hold, but not for the reason above.
  • \(f(x)=x^{3}\) at \(c=0\): \(f'(0)=0\), \(f(x)=f(0)\) only at \(x=0\) — again it holds accidentally.
  • \(f(x)\equiv \text{const}\): \(f'(c)=0\) and \(f(x)=f(c)\) for all \(x\) — the conclusion fails completely.
  • \(f(x)=x^{2}\sin(1/x)\) (with \(f(0)=0\)) has \(f'(0)=0\) and \(f\) takes the value \(0\) infinitely often in every neighbourhood of \(0\) — failure.

So \(f'(c)\neq 0\) is a genuine sufficient condition for local injectivity; it is not necessary, as \(x^{3}\) shows.

Geometric picture. If \(f'(c)\neq 0\), the tangent line at \(c\) is not horizontal. Near \(c\) the graph is “transverse” to the horizontal line \(y=f(c)\), crossing it exactly once in a small window. If \(f'(c)=0\), the tangent is horizontal and the graph can hug, touch, or even run along the level line \(y=f(c)\).
Careful. The conclusion is local: it only guarantees that \(f\) does not repeat the particular value \(f(c)\) near \(c\). It does not say \(f\) is injective on \((a,b)\), and it does not say \(f\) is injective on \((c-\delta,c+\delta)\) as a whole — only that \(f(x)\neq f(c)\) there.
