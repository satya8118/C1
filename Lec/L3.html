1. Understanding Upper Bounds

Definition: Upper Bound
Let $S$ be a non-empty set of real numbers. A number $\alpha\in \mathbb{R}$ is called an upper bound for $S$ if: $$\forall\, x \in S,\quad x \leq \alpha.$$

Examples

  • For $S = \{1, 2, 3\}$, the numbers $3,\, 4,\, 100$ are all upper bounds.
  • For $(0, 1)$, the number $1$ is an upper bound even though $1 \notin (0,1)$.
What does it mean for $\alpha\in \mathbb{R}$ to not be an upper bound for $S$?
$\exists\, x \in S$ such that $x > \alpha.$
Let $S$ be a nonempty set. What you mean by $S$ has a maximum.
$\alpha\in \mathbb{R}$ is maxixum for $S$ means that $\alpha$ satisfies two properties.
  1. $\alpha\in S.$
  2. $\alpha$ is an upper bound for $S.$
Let $S$ be a nonempty set and $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}.$ What you mean by $\alpha$ is not maximum of $S.$
Either $\alpha \notin S$ or $\alpha$ is not an upperbound of $S.$
Why $5$ is not maximum of $S=(1,5)?$
Why $5$ is not maximum of $S=(1,6)?$

Is $3.5$ an upper bound for $S = \{1,2,3,4,5\}$?
No, because $4 \in S$ and $4 > 3.5.$ Hence $3.5$ is not maximum of $S.$
Proposition: If $\alpha$ is an upper bound for $S$ and $\beta > \alpha$, then $\beta$ is also an upper bound for $S.$
Proof. Let $x \in S.$ Since $\alpha$ is an upper bound, $x \leq \alpha.$ Since $\beta > \alpha,$ we get $x \leq \alpha < \beta,$ so $x < \beta.$ As $x$ was arbitrary, $\beta$ is an upper bound for $S.$ $\square$

Consequence:
If $U$ is the set of all upper bounds of $S$ and $\alpha \in U,$ then $[\alpha, \infty) \subseteq U.$
Definition: Bounded Above
Let $A$ be a nonempty subset of $\mathbb{R}.$ Then $A$ is bounded above in $\mathbb{R}$ if $A$ has an upper bound, i.e., $\exists \alpha in \mathbb{R}$ such that $\alpha$ is an upper bound of $A.$
$$\exists\, \alpha \in \mathbb{R} \text{ such that } \forall\, x \in A,\quad x \leq \alpha.$$
Which of the following sets are bounded above: $A=[1,2], B=(2,5), C=[1,\infty), D=\{1,2,3\}, \mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^+.$
  • $A$ is bouded above by $2$,
    $B$ is bounded above by $5,$
    $D$ is bounded above by $3,$
    Remaining sets are not bounded above.
  • What do you mean a set $S$ is not bounded above?
    • It means $S$ does not have an upper bound.
    That is whatever $\alpha\in \mathbb{R}$ you take, there is an $x\in S$ such that $x>\alpha.$
  • For example, $\alpha\in \mathbb{R}$ then $\alpha<\alpha+1\in \mathbb{R}.$
  • But note, we cannot apply same procedure for $\mathbb{R}^{+}=(0,\infty)$ as $-99\in \mathbb{R}$ but $-99+1=-98\not \in \mathbb{R}^{+}.$ We prove in two different ways.
    By cases: If $\alpha \le 0,$ then $1\in\mathbb{R}^{+},$ will work. If $\alpha>0,$ then $\alpha+1\in \mathbb{R}^{+}.$
    Without cases: If $\alpha\in \mathbb{R},$ then $|\alpha|+1\in \mathbb{R}^{+}.$
Proposition: Subsets of bounded-above sets are bounded above.
Let $\emptyset \neq A \subseteq B \subseteq \mathbb{R}.$ If $B$ is bounded above, then $A$ is bounded above.

Proof. Since $B$ is bounded above, $\exists\, \alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $\forall\, x \in B,\; x \leq \alpha.$ Let $y \in A.$ Then $y \in B$ (since $A \subseteq B$), so $y \leq \alpha.$ Hence $\alpha$ is an upper bound for $A.$ $\square$
Are you able to prove that if $\emptyset \neq A \subseteq B \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and $A$ is not bounded above, then $B$ is not bounded above.

Theorem: $\text{lub}\{|x-y|:x,y\in A\} = \text{lub}(A)-\text{glb}(A)$

Statement
Let $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ be a nonempty bounded set. Then the set $\{|x-y|:x,y\in A\}$ is bounded above, and $$\text{lub}\{|x-y|:x,y\in A\}=\text{lub}(A)-\text{glb}(A).$$ The quantity $\text{lub}(A)-\text{glb}(A)$ is called the diameter of $A.$
Rough Work — Two Inequalities to Prove Write $M:=\text{lub}(A)$ and $m:=\text{glb}(A).$ We need to show two things:
  1. ($\le$) $M-m$ is an upper bound for $\{|x-y|\},$ i.e., $|x-y|\le M-m$ for all $x,y\in A.$
  2. ($\ge$) $M-m$ is the least upper bound, i.e., no number smaller than $M-m$ is an upper bound. Equivalently, for every $\varepsilon>0,$ there exist $x,y\in A$ with $|x-y|>M-m-\varepsilon.$
For (1): since $m\le x\le M$ and $m\le y\le M,$ we get $x-y\le M-m$ and $y-x\le M-m,$ so $|x-y|\le M-m.$
For (2): use the definitions of lub and glb to find $x$ within $\varepsilon/2$ of $M$ and $y$ within $\varepsilon/2$ of $m,$ so $x-y>M-m-\varepsilon.$
Every $|x-y|$ is at most $M-m$ (upper bound). By choosing $x$ near $M$ and $y$ near $m,$ we can make $|x-y|$ arbitrarily close to $M-m$ (least upper bound).
Proof. Since $A$ is bounded, $M:=\text{lub}(A)$ and $m:=\text{glb}(A)$ both exist in $\mathbb{R},$ and $m\le M.$ Let $D:=\{|x-y|:x,y\in A\}.$

Step 1. $M-m$ is an upper bound for $D.$
Let $x,y\in A.$ Since $m$ is a lower bound and $M$ is an upper bound for $A,$ $$m\le x\le M\quad\text{and}\quad m\le y\le M.$$ Therefore $x-y\le M-m$ and $y-x\le M-m,$ so $$|x-y|=\max\{x-y,\,y-x\}\le M-m.$$ Since $x,y\in A$ were arbitrary, $M-m$ is an upper bound for $D,$ and hence $\text{lub}(D)\le M-m.$

Step 2. $M-m\le\text{lub}(D).$
Let $\varepsilon>0.$
  • Since $M-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}M-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}.$
  • Since $m+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}>m=\text{glb}(A),$ there exists $y\in A$ with $y
Then $$|x-y|=x-y>\left(M-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)-\left(m+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\right) =M-m-\varepsilon.$$ (Here $x-y>0$ since $x>M-\varepsilon/2\ge m+\varepsilon/2>y$ for small enough $\varepsilon,$ and in any case $|x-y|\ge x-y>M-m-\varepsilon.$)

So $M-m-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound for $D.$ Since $\varepsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $M-m\le\text{lub}(D).$

Conclusion. From Steps 1 and 2, $\text{lub}(D)=M-m,$ i.e., $$\text{lub}\{|x-y|:x,y\in A\}=\text{lub}(A)-\text{glb}(A).\qquad\blacksquare$$
Examples
Set $A$ $\text{glb}(A)$ $\text{lub}(A)$ Diameter $=\text{lub}-\text{glb}$
$[2,7]$ $2$ $7$ $5$
$(2,7)$ $2$ $7$ $5$  (endpoints not in $A,$ yet diam $=5$)
$\{1,3,5,9\}$ $1$ $9$ $8$  (attained by $|9-1|$)
$\bigl\{\tfrac{1}{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\bigr\}$ $0$ (not in $A$) $1$ $1$  (sup of $|1/n-1/k|$ is $1$)
Remarks
  1. The diameter equals $M-m$ regardless of whether $m$ and $M$ actually belong to $A.$ The open interval $(2,7)$ has the same diameter as $[2,7].$
  2. $\text{lub}\{|x-y|\}=0$ if and only if $A$ is a single point (since $M-m=0\Leftrightarrow M=m$).
  3. The diameter of $A$ equals the diameter of its closure $\bar{A},$ since $\text{lub}$ and $\text{glb}$ are unchanged by taking the closure.
