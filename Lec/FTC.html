The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

We abbreviate the name of this theorem as FTC1. In words, it says that the derivative of a definite integral with respect to its upper limit is the integrand evaluated at the upper limit.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1

If \( f \) is continuous on \( [a, b] \), then the function \( g \) defined by

\[ g(x) = \int_a^x f(t) \, dt \quad\quad a \leq x \leq b \]

is continuous on \( [a, b] \) and differentiable on \( (a, b) \), and \( g'(x) = f(x) \).

PROOF   If \( x \) and \( x + h \) are in \( (a, b) \), then

\( g(x + h) - g(x) = \int_a^{x+h} f(t) \, dt - \int_a^x f(t) \, dt \)
\( = \left( \int_a^x f(t) \, dt + \int_x^{x+h} f(t) \, dt \right) - \int_a^x f(t) \, dt \)
\( = \int_x^{x+h} f(t) \, dt \)

Key Step: The Difference Quotient

Dividing the result by \( h \) (for \( h \neq 0 \)), we obtain the difference quotient for \( g(x) \):

\[ \frac{g(x + h) - g(x)}{h} = \frac{1}{h} \int_x^{x+h} f(t) \, dt \]

For now let's assume that \( h > 0 \). Since \( f \) is continuous on \( [x, x + h] \), the Extreme Value Theorem says that there are numbers \( u \) and \( v \) in \( [x, x + h] \) such that \( f(u) = m \) and \( f(v) = M \), where \( m \) and \( M \) are the absolute minimum and maximum values of \( f \) on \( [x, x + h] \).

Result: The Bounding Inequality

By considering the areas of these rectangles, we have

\[ mh \leq \int_x^{x+h} f(t) \, dt \leq Mh \]

that is,

\[ f(u)h \leq \int_x^{x+h} f(t) \, dt \leq f(v)h \]

Since \( h > 0 \), we can divide this inequality by \( h \):

\[ f(u) \leq \frac{1}{h} \int_x^{x+h} f(t) \, dt \leq f(v) \]

Now we use the difference quotient derived earlier to replace the middle part of this inequality:

\[ f(u) \leq \frac{g(x + h) - g(x)}{h} \leq f(v) \]
Dynamic Visualization The graph above illustrates how the area under the curve \( f(x) \) between \( x \) and \( x+h \) is bounded by the rectangles of height \( m \) and \( M \). As \( h \) approaches 0, these bounds squeeze the average rate of change towards \( f(x) \).

This inequality can be proved in a similar manner for the case where \( h < 0 \). Now we let \( h \to 0 \). We then have \( u \to x \) and \( v \to x \), since \( u \) and \( v \) lie between \( x \) and \( x + h \). Therefore

\[ \lim_{h \to 0} f(u) = \lim_{u \to x} f(u) = f(x) \]
\[ \text{and} \quad \lim_{h \to 0} f(v) = \lim_{v \to x} f(v) = f(x) \]

because \( f \) is continuous at \( x \). We conclude, from the Squeeze Theorem, that

Conclusion of Part 1

\[ g'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{g(x + h) - g(x)}{h} = f(x) \]

If \( x = a \) or \( b \), then this equation can be interpreted as a one-sided limit. The properties of one-sided limits then show that \( g \) is continuous on \( [a, b] \).

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2

If \( f \) is continuous on \( [a, b] \), then

\[ \int_a^b f(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(a) \]

where \( F \) is any antiderivative of \( f \), that is, a function such that \( F' = f \).

PROOF   Let \( g(x) = \int_a^x f(t) \, dt \). We know from Part 1 that \( g'(x) = f(x) \); that is, \( g \) is an antiderivative of \( f \). If \( F \) is any other antiderivative of \( f \) on \( [a, b] \), then \( F'(x) = f(x) \).

Essential Result: Antiderivatives Differ by a Constant

To prove that \( F \) and \( g \) differ by a constant, let \( H(x) = F(x) - g(x) \). Then

\[ H'(x) = F'(x) - g'(x) = f(x) - f(x) = 0 \]

for all \( x \) in \( [a, b] \). Since a function with a zero derivative on an interval is constant, there exists a constant \( C \) such that \( H(x) = C \). Therefore, \( F \) and \( g \) differ by a constant:

\[ F(x) = g(x) + C \]

for \( a < x < b \). But both \( F \) and \( g \) are continuous on \( [a, b] \) and so, by taking limits of both sides of this equation (as \( x \to a^+ \) and \( x \to b^- \)), we see that it also holds when \( x = a \) and \( x = b \).

If we put \( x = a \) in the formula for \( g(x) \), we get

\[ g(a) = \int_a^a f(t) \, dt = 0 \]

So, using the equation \( F(x) = g(x) + C \) with \( x = b \) and \( x = a \), we have

Conclusion of Part 2

\[ F(b) - F(a) = [g(b) + C] - [g(a) + C] \]
\[ = g(b) - g(a) = g(b) = \int_a^b f(t) \, dt \]

Summary: The Core Results

Part 1: Derivative of an Integral

If \( f \) is continuous on \( [a, b] \), and \( g(x) = \int_a^x f(t) \, dt \), then \( g \) is continuous on \( [a, b] \), differentiable on \( (a, b) \), and its derivative is the original function:

\[ g'(x) = f(x) \]

Part 2: Evaluating a Definite Integral

If \( f \) is continuous on \( [a, b] \), and \( F \) is any antiderivative of \( f \) (i.e., \( F' = f \)), then the definite integral of \( f \) from \( a \) to \( b \) is the difference in the antiderivative evaluated at the endpoints:

\[ \int_a^b f(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(a) \]
