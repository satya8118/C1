Rolle's Theorem and its applications

A deceptively simple statement — a function that starts and ends at the same height must have a flat spot somewhere in between — turns out to prove things about roots, uniqueness, and races that have nothing obviously to do with tangent lines. Every problem below hides its solution behind a "Show solution" button: try it yourself first.

01The statement

Rolle's Theorem

Statement. Let \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) be a function such that:

  1. \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\),
  2. \(f\) is differentiable on \((a,b)\),
  3. \(f(a)=f(b)\).

Then there exists at least one point \(c\in(a,b)\) such that \(f'(c)=0\).

02The proof

Why Rolle's theorem is true

The proof uses two ingredients. Continuity on a closed interval gives us a highest and a lowest point. Differentiability then tells us that a highest or lowest point inside the interval has a flat tangent.

Ingredient 1 — Extreme Value Theorem. If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), there are points \(x_M,x_m\in[a,b]\) with \[ f(x_m)\le f(x)\le f(x_M)\quad\text{for all }x\in[a,b]. \] We write \(M=f(x_M)\) for the maximum and \(m=f(x_m)\) for the minimum. This is where continuity on the closed interval is used.
Ingredient 2 — Fermat's Lemma. Suppose \(f\) is defined on \((a,b)\), has a local maximum or local minimum at \(c\in(a,b)\), and is differentiable at \(c\). Then \(f'(c)=0\).
Proof of Fermat's Lemma. Take a local maximum at \(c\); for a minimum apply the argument to \(-f\). Choose \(\delta\gt0\) with \(f(x)\le f(c)\) whenever \(|x-c|\lt\delta\). Then:
  • Right of \(c\) (\(c\lt x\lt c+\delta\)): the numerator \(f(x)-f(c)\le0\) and the denominator \(x-c\gt0\), so \(\dfrac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}\le0\). Letting \(x\to c^+\) gives \(f'(c)\le0\).
  • Left of \(c\) (\(c-\delta\lt x\lt c\)): the numerator is still \(\le0\) but now the denominator is \(\lt0\), so the quotient is \(\ge0\). Letting \(x\to c^-\) gives \(f'(c)\ge0\).
Both inequalities hold for the same number \(f'(c)\) (the derivative exists, so the one-sided limits agree). Hence \(f'(c)=0\). \(\blacksquare\)
Explore · Fermat's Lemma at a maximum

The amber secant (left of the peak) always climbs, so its slope is \(\ge0\). The red secant (right of the peak) always falls, so its slope is \(\le0\). Squeeze \(h\to0\): both slopes are trapped on opposite sides of \(0\), so the common limit \(f'(c)\) can only be \(0\).

Fermat's Lemma does not reverse. For \(f(x)=x^3\) we have \(f'(0)=0\) but \(0\) is neither a maximum nor a minimum. A flat tangent is necessary for an interior extremum, not sufficient.

Proof of Rolle's Theorem

Step 1. Since \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), the Extreme Value Theorem gives a maximum \(M=f(x_M)\) and a minimum \(m=f(x_m)\).

Case 1: \(M=m\). Then \(m\le f(x)\le M\) forces \(f\) to be constant, so \(f'(x)=0\) for every \(x\); take \(c\) to be any point of \((a,b)\), for example the midpoint.

Case 2: \(M\gt m\). The common endpoint value \(f(a)=f(b)\) cannot equal both \(M\) and \(m\). So one of the following holds:

  • \(f(a)\ne M\). Then neither endpoint is a maximum point, so \(x_M\in(a,b)\). Put \(c=x_M\). It is a global, hence local, maximum, and \(f\) is differentiable at \(c\), so \(f'(c)=0\) by Fermat.
  • \(f(a)=M\). Then \(f(a)\ne m\) (because \(M\gt m\)), so neither endpoint is a minimum point and \(x_m\in(a,b)\). Put \(c=x_m\); again \(f'(c)=0\) by Fermat.
In every case there is \(c\in(a,b)\) with \(f'(c)=0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Where each hypothesis was used. Continuity on \([a,b]\) supplies \(M\) and \(m\) (Extreme Value Theorem). \(f(a)=f(b)\) forces at least one extremum into the interior. Differentiability on \((a,b)\) lets Fermat's Lemma turn that interior extremum into \(f'(c)=0\). Remove any one and the argument breaks, as the next section shows.

04Five uses of one small theorem

Applications of Rolle's Theorem

  1. Proving the existence of stationary points. If a function satisfies Rolle's hypotheses, it guarantees at least one point where the derivative is zero (a stationary point, which in the proof turns out to be a maximum or a minimum).
  2. Root uniqueness in equations. If two roots existed where they "shouldn't," Rolle's theorem would force the derivative to vanish somewhere between them — a contradiction if you can show the derivative never vanishes.
  3. Analysis of polynomial functions. Between any two distinct real roots of a polynomial, its derivative must have at least one real root.
  4. Establishing the Mean Value Theorem. Rolle's theorem is the special case \(f(a)=f(b)\) of the MVT, and it's the engine used to prove the MVT in general — that's the whole subject of the next lecture.
  5. Geometric interpretation for curve sketching. If a curve starts and ends at the same height, it must have a horizontal tangent somewhere in between.

Notice items 2 and 3 are really the same idea stated two ways — this "no two roots without a zero derivative in between" argument is the single most common use of Rolle's theorem you'll see, and drives the root-counting problems below.

05Problems 1–3

Checking the hypotheses — and watching them fail

Problem 1

Verify Rolle's Theorem for \(f(x)=x^2-4x+3\) on \([1,3]\).

\(f\) is a polynomial, so it's continuous and differentiable everywhere — both hypotheses 1 and 2 hold automatically. Check hypothesis 3: \(f(1)=1-4+3=0\) and \(f(3)=9-12+3=0\), so \(f(1)=f(3)\). All conditions hold, so by Rolle's theorem some \(c\in(1,3)\) has \(f'(c)=0\).

Find it explicitly: \(f'(x)=2x-4\), so \(f'(c)=0\implies 2c-4=0\implies c=2\). Indeed \(c=2\in(1,3)\). ✓

Problem 2 — a hypothesis failing

Does Rolle's Theorem apply to \(f(x)=|x|\) on \([-1,1]\)?

\(f\) is continuous on \([-1,1]\) and \(f(-1)=f(1)=1\), so hypotheses 1 and 3 hold. But \(f(x)=|x|\) is not differentiable at \(x=0\) — the graph has a sharp corner there, with different left- and right-hand slopes (\(-1\) and \(+1\)). Hypothesis 2 fails, so Rolle's Theorem simply does not apply here. (And indeed, there is no point in \((-1,1)\) where \(f'=0\) — the closest thing, \(x=0\), isn't even differentiable, which is exactly why the theorem needs that hypothesis.)
Problem 3

Apply Rolle's Theorem to \(f(x)=\cos x\) on \([0,2\pi]\).

\(\cos x\) is continuous and differentiable everywhere, so hypotheses 1 and 2 hold on any interval. \(f(0)=\cos0=1\) and \(f(2\pi)=\cos2\pi=1\), so \(f(0)=f(2\pi)\) — hypothesis 3 holds.

By Rolle's Theorem, some \(c\in(0,2\pi)\) has \(f'(c)=0\). Since \(f'(x)=-\sin x\): \(-\sin c=0\implies c=\pi\) (within the open interval). Indeed \(c=\pi\in(0,2\pi)\). ✓

06Problems 4–5

Counting roots with Rolle's theorem

Problem 4

Show that a polynomial \(P(x)\) of degree \(n\) has at most \(n\) real roots.

This is application 3 from Section 2, formalized — and it's proved by induction, using Rolle's theorem as the inductive step.

Induct on \(n\). Base case \(n=1\): a degree-1 polynomial \(P(x)=mx+k\) (\(m\ne0\)) has exactly one root, \(x=-k/m\) — certainly at most \(1\).

Inductive step: suppose every polynomial of degree \(n\) has at most \(n\) real roots, and let \(P(x)\) have degree \(n+1\). Suppose for contradiction that \(P\) has more than \(n+1\) real roots — at least \(n+2\) of them, say \(a_1<a_2<\cdots<a_{n+2}\), so \(P(a_1)=P(a_2)=\cdots=P(a_{n+2})=0\).

Apply Rolle's theorem to each consecutive pair: since \(P(a_i)=P(a_{i+1})=0\), there's some \(c_i\in(a_i,a_{i+1})\) with \(P'(c_i)=0\), for each \(i=1,\dots,n+1\). These \(n+1\) values \(c_1<c_2<\cdots<c_{n+1}\) are distinct (they live in disjoint intervals), so \(P'(x)\) — a degree-\(n\) polynomial — has at least \(n+1\) real roots. That contradicts the inductive hypothesis, which caps a degree-\(n\) polynomial at \(n\) roots.

So \(P(x)\) cannot have more than \(n+1\) real roots, completing the induction.

Problem 5

Show that \(x^3+x-1=0\) has exactly one real root.

Existence. Let \(f(x)=x^3+x-1\), continuous everywhere, with \(f(0)=-1\lt0\) and \(f(1)=1\gt0\). By the Intermediate Value Theorem it has a root in \((0,1)\).

Uniqueness. Suppose there were two roots \(r_1\lt r_2\). Then \(f(r_1)=f(r_2)=0\), and \(f\) is a polynomial, so Rolle's theorem gives \(c\in(r_1,r_2)\) with \(f'(c)=3c^2+1=0\). But \(3c^2+1\ge1\) for every real \(c\), a contradiction. So the root is unique.

07Problem 6

Two runners finish in a tie — did they ever match speeds?

Problem 6

Two runners start a race at the same time and finish in a tie. Prove that at some point during the race, they had exactly the same speed.

Let \(g(t)\) and \(h(t)\) be the position functions of the two runners over \([0,b]\), where \(b\) is the finishing time. "Start together" means \(g(0)=h(0)\); "finish in a tie" means \(g(b)=h(b)\).

Define \(f(t)=g(t)-h(t)\). Then \(f(0)=g(0)-h(0)=0\) and \(f(b)=g(b)-h(b)=0\), so \(f(0)=f(b)\). Position functions of runners are continuous and (assuming smooth running) differentiable, so \(f\) inherits both properties. Rolle's Theorem gives some \(c\in(0,b)\) with \(f'(c)=0\), i.e. \(g'(c)-h'(c)=0\), i.e. \(g'(c)=h'(c)\) — at time \(c\), both runners had exactly the same instantaneous speed.

This is the same "subtract to reduce to Rolle" move you'll see constantly once we reach the Mean Value Theorem in the next lecture — building a difference function is almost always the first move.

08Before the next lecture

Self-assessment

Part A — recall

State all three hypotheses of Rolle's Theorem, and the conclusion.

\(f\) continuous on \([a,b]\); \(f\) differentiable on \((a,b)\); \(f(a)=f(b)\). Conclusion: \(\exists c\in(a,b)\) with \(f'(c)=0\).
Part B — apply

Does Rolle's Theorem apply to \(f(x)=\sqrt[3]{x}\) (the real cube root) on \([-1,1]\)? Justify.

No. Two hypotheses fail: \(f(-1)=-1\ne1=f(1)\), and \(f\) is not differentiable at \(0\) (its tangent there is vertical, \(f'(x)=\frac13x^{-2/3}\to\infty\)). So the theorem does not apply, and indeed \(f'\) is never zero.
Part B — apply

Verify Rolle's Theorem for \(f(x)=x^3-3x^2+2x\) on \([0,1]\) — the polynomial factors as \(x(x-1)(x-2)\), so check the endpoint values first.

\(f(0)=0\), \(f(1)=1-3+2=0\), so \(f(0)=f(1)\); \(f\) is a polynomial, hence continuous and differentiable everywhere. All hypotheses hold. \(f'(x)=3x^2-6x+2\); setting \(f'(c)=0\) gives \(c=\frac{6\pm\sqrt{36-24}}{6}=1\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\). Only \(c=1-\frac1{\sqrt3}\approx0.42\) lies in \((0,1)\), which is the guaranteed point.
Part C — think

In the "polynomial has at most \(n\) roots" proof, why did we need the roots \(a_1<\cdots<a_{n+2}\) to be strictly increasing before applying Rolle's theorem to consecutive pairs?

Rolle's theorem needs a genuine interval \((a_i,a_{i+1})\) with \(a_i<a_{i+1}\) — if two "roots" coincided, there'd be no interval to work with, and the resulting critical points \(c_i\) might not be distinct, breaking the count of \(n+1\) distinct roots of \(P'\) that the contradiction relies on. Ordering the roots strictly is what guarantees \(n+1\) genuinely different intervals, hence \(n+1\) genuinely different critical points.
Coming up next: the Lagrange Mean Value Theorem — what happens when we drop the requirement \(f(a)=f(b)\) and ask instead for the tangent to match the secant line. Rolle's Theorem turns out to be the special case, and also the tool used to prove it.
