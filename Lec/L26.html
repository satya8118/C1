Reading a curve with derivatives

You know how to differentiate. Now we put derivatives to work: finding where a graph rises and falls, where it peaks and bottoms out, how it bends, and how to draw it. Every picture below is live, so drag, tick and compare. Every problem hides its solution behind a "Show solution" button.

01Slope tells direction

Reading a graph with the derivative

At school you learned that the derivative is the slope of the tangent. In this lecture you will see that this single number, computed at every point, lets you read the whole story of a curve: where it climbs, where it falls, where it turns, and how it bends.

Definitions. A function \(f\) is increasing on an interval \(I\) if \(x_1\lt x_2\) in \(I\) implies \(f(x_1)\lt f(x_2)\), and decreasing if \(x_1\lt x_2\) implies \(f(x_1)\gt f(x_2)\).

Increasing/Decreasing Test. Let \(f\) be continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\).

  1. If \(f'(x)\gt0\) for all \(x\in(a,b)\), then \(f\) is increasing on \([a,b]\).
  2. If \(f'(x)\lt0\) for all \(x\in(a,b)\), then \(f\) is decreasing on \([a,b]\).
  3. If \(f'(x)=0\) for all \(x\in(a,b)\), then \(f\) is constant on \([a,b]\).

Proof. Take \(a\le x_1\lt x_2\le b\). By the Mean Value Theorem on \([x_1,x_2]\) there is \(c\in(x_1,x_2)\) with \[ f(x_2)-f(x_1)=f'(c)\,(x_2-x_1). \] Since \(x_2-x_1\gt0\), the sign of \(f(x_2)-f(x_1)\) is the sign of \(f'(c)\). If \(f'\gt0\) everywhere this is positive (increasing); if \(f'\lt0\) it is negative (decreasing); if \(f'=0\) it is zero (constant). \(\blacksquare\)

Positive on an interval, not at a point. The test needs \(f'\gt0\) on a whole interval. Knowing \(f'(c)\gt0\) at a single point does not say that \(f\) is increasing near \(c\).
02Turning points

Critical points and local extrema

Local extrema. \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c\) if \(f(c)\ge f(x)\) for all \(x\) near \(c\) (say \(|x-c|\lt\delta\)), and a local minimum if \(f(c)\le f(x)\) for all \(x\) near \(c\).

Critical point. A point \(c\) in the interior of the domain is critical if \(f'(c)=0\) or \(f'(c)\) does not exist.

Fermat's Lemma (recalled from Rolle's theorem). If \(f\) has a local extremum at an interior point \(c\), then \(c\) is a critical point. So local extrema can only hide at critical points.

The converse is false. \(f(x)=x^3\) has \(f'(0)=0\) but no extremum at \(0\). So a critical point is only a candidate. We need a test to decide.

First Derivative Test. Let \(c\) be a critical point and \(f\) continuous at \(c\).

  • If \(f'\) changes from \(+\) to \(-\) at \(c\), then \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c\) (rises, then falls).
  • If \(f'\) changes from \(-\) to \(+\) at \(c\), then \(f\) has a local minimum at \(c\) (falls, then rises).
  • If \(f'\) does not change sign, there is no extremum at \(c\).

Why: by the Increasing/Decreasing Test \(f\) is increasing on one side of \(c\) and decreasing on the other (or monotone on both sides), which is exactly the shape of a peak, a valley, or a staircase.

03Global extrema

The highest and lowest values on an interval

Global (absolute) extrema. \(f(c)\) is the global maximum of \(f\) on \(I\) if \(f(c)\ge f(x)\) for every \(x\in I\); the global minimum is defined similarly. Local extrema compare with neighbours only; global extrema compare with everyone.

Extreme Value Theorem. If \(f\) is continuous on a closed bounded interval \([a,b]\), then \(f\) attains a global maximum and a global minimum on \([a,b]\).

Why "closed" and "continuous" matter. \(f(x)=x\) on the open interval \((0,1)\) has no maximum, since values come arbitrarily close to \(1\) but never reach it. And \(f(x)=1/x\) on \((0,1]\) has no maximum because it grows without bound near \(0\).

The Closed Interval Method. To find the global extrema of a continuous \(f\) on \([a,b]\):

  1. Find all critical points of \(f\) inside \((a,b)\).
  2. Compute \(f\) at each critical point and at the endpoints \(a\) and \(b\).
  3. The largest value is the global maximum; the smallest is the global minimum.

This works because the Extreme Value Theorem guarantees the extrema exist, and an extremum is either at an endpoint or in the interior, where Fermat's Lemma forces a critical point.

04Bending

Concavity, inflection points and the second derivative

Two curves can both rise from left to right, one like a bowl and one like a dome. The slope alone cannot tell them apart; how the slope changes can.

Concavity. \(f\) is concave up on \(I\) if \(f'\) is increasing on \(I\) (the tangent slopes grow as you move right, so the curve lies above its tangents). It is concave down if \(f'\) is decreasing on \(I\) (the curve lies below its tangents).

Concavity Test. If \(f''\gt0\) on \(I\), then \(f\) is concave up on \(I\); if \(f''\lt0\), concave down. Proof: apply the Increasing/Decreasing Test to \(f'\).

Inflection point. A point of the curve where \(f\) is continuous and the concavity changes from up to down or from down to up.

Careful. If \(f''(c)\) exists and \(c\) is an inflection point, then \(f''(c)=0\). But \(f''(c)=0\) does not guarantee an inflection point: \(f(x)=x^4\) has \(f''(0)=0\) yet is concave up on both sides of \(0\).

Second Derivative Test. Suppose \(f'(c)=0\) and \(f''(c)\) exists.

  • If \(f''(c)\gt0\), then \(f\) has a local minimum at \(c\).
  • If \(f''(c)\lt0\), then \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c\).
  • If \(f''(c)=0\), the test says nothing (\(x^3\), \(x^4\), \(-x^4\) all have \(f''(0)=0\) and behave differently).

Proof of the first part. Because \(f'(c)=0\), \[ f''(c)=\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f'(x)-f'(c)}{x-c}=\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f'(x)}{x-c}\gt0. \] So for \(x\) close to \(c\) the quotient \(\dfrac{f'(x)}{x-c}\) is positive: \(f'(x)\lt0\) for \(x\lt c\) and \(f'(x)\gt0\) for \(x\gt c\). That is a change from \(-\) to \(+\), so \(c\) is a local minimum by the First Derivative Test. The second part is the same with signs reversed. \(\blacksquare\)

05Curve tracing

Sketching a curve from its derivatives

A checklist for tracing \(y=f(x)\).

  1. Domain and any points to exclude.
  2. Intercepts with the axes (solve \(f(x)=0\) and compute \(f(0)\)).
  3. Symmetry: even (\(f(-x)=f(x)\)), odd (\(f(-x)=-f(x)\)), or periodic.
  4. Asymptotes: vertical (where \(f\) blows up), horizontal or oblique (behaviour as \(x\to\pm\infty\)).
  5. First derivative: intervals of increase and decrease; local extrema.
  6. Second derivative: intervals of concavity; inflection points.
  7. Assemble everything and sketch.

Worked example: \(f(x)=\dfrac{x}{1+x^2}\)

06Practice

Problems with solutions

Try each problem on paper first; then open the solution.

Problem 1

Find the intervals of increase and decrease and the local extrema of \(f(x)=x^3-6x^2+9x+1\).

\(f'(x)=3x^2-12x+9=3(x-1)(x-3)\). The critical points are \(x=1\) and \(x=3\). Sign of \(f'\): positive on \((-\infty,1)\), negative on \((1,3)\), positive on \((3,\infty)\). So \(f\) is increasing on \((-\infty,1)\) and \((3,\infty)\), decreasing on \((1,3)\). By the first derivative test there is a local maximum \(f(1)=5\) and a local minimum \(f(3)=1\).
Problem 2

Show that \(f(x)=x^3+x\) is increasing on all of \(\mathbb R\), and deduce that the equation \(x^3+x=0\) has exactly one real root.

\(f'(x)=3x^2+1\ge1\gt0\) for all \(x\), so \(f\) is increasing on every interval, hence on \(\mathbb R\). An increasing function takes each value at most once, so there is at most one root. Since \(f(0)=0\), the single root is \(x=0\).
Problem 3

Find the global maximum and minimum of \(f(x)=x^3-3x\) on \([-2,3]\).

\(f'(x)=3x^2-3=0\) gives \(x=\pm1\), both inside \((-2,3)\). Candidates: \(f(-2)=-2\), \(f(-1)=2\), \(f(1)=-2\), \(f(3)=18\). The global maximum is \(18\), at \(x=3\); the global minimum is \(-2\), attained at both \(x=-2\) and \(x=1\).
Problem 4

Find the intervals of concavity and the inflection points of \(f(x)=x^4-4x^3\).

\(f'(x)=4x^3-12x^2\), \(f''(x)=12x^2-24x=12x(x-2)\). So \(f''\gt0\) on \((-\infty,0)\) and \((2,\infty)\) (concave up), and \(f''\lt0\) on \((0,2)\) (concave down). The concavity changes at \(x=0\) and \(x=2\), so the inflection points are \((0,0)\) and \((2,-16)\).
Problem 5

A function has \(f^{\prime}(x)=(x-1)^2(x+2)\). Where does it have local extrema?

The critical points are \(x=1\) and \(x=-2\). The factor \((x-1)^2\) is never negative, so the sign of \(f'\) is the sign of \(x+2\). It changes from \(-\) to \(+\) at \(x=-2\) (local minimum), but does not change at \(x=1\) (it stays positive on both sides), so there is no extremum at \(x=1\), only a flat pause while still increasing.
Problem 6

Prove that \(e^x\gt1+x\) for every \(x\gt0\).

Let \(g(x)=e^x-1-x\). Then \(g'(x)=e^x-1\gt0\) for \(x\gt0\), so \(g\) is increasing on \([0,\infty)\) by the Increasing Test. Hence for \(x\gt0\), \(g(x)\gt g(0)=0\), i.e. \(e^x\gt1+x\).
Problem 7

Use the second derivative test on \(f(x)=x^3-3x\). Then explain why it fails for \(g(x)=x^4\) at \(0\) and what to do instead.

\(f'(x)=3x^2-3=0\) at \(x=\pm1\), and \(f''(x)=6x\). At \(x=1\): \(f''=6\gt0\), local minimum. At \(x=-1\): \(f''=-6\lt0\), local maximum. For \(g(x)=x^4\): \(g'(0)=0\) and \(g''(0)=0\), so the test is inconclusive. Use the first derivative test: \(g'(x)=4x^3\) is negative for \(x\lt0\) and positive for \(x\gt0\), so \(0\) is a local (indeed global) minimum.
Problem 8

Sketch \(f(x)=xe^{-x}\) by following the checklist.

Domain \(\mathbb R\). Intercept \((0,0)\) only. As \(x\to\infty\), \(xe^{-x}\to0\) (horizontal asymptote \(y=0\) on the right); as \(x\to-\infty\), \(f\to-\infty\). \(f'(x)=(1-x)e^{-x}\): positive for \(x\lt1\), negative for \(x\gt1\), so \(f\) increases then decreases with a global maximum \(f(1)=1/e\). \(f''(x)=(x-2)e^{-x}\): negative for \(x\lt2\) (concave down), positive for \(x\gt2\) (concave up), so there is an inflection point at \(x=2\), \(f(2)=2/e^2\). The curve rises through the origin to the peak at \(x=1\), then bends and flattens toward the \(x\)-axis. Compare it with the curve tracer.
