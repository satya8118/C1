Real Analysis · Continuity · Differentiation · Integration

Continuity, differentiation and integration: one story

Three chapters of calculus meet in a single object, the accumulation function \(A(x)=\int_a^x f\). Sweep out area, squeeze difference quotients, hunt for primitives that cannot exist, and build the logarithm from nothing but an integral. Every picture is live.

01The big picture

Three ideas, one object

Continuity says a graph has no breaks. Differentiation measures how steeply it climbs. Integration measures the area underneath. They look like three separate chapters, but one object ties them together: the accumulation function \[ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt. \] Build \(A\) from \(f\), and every question about \(f\) turns into a question about the three ideas at once. Is \(A\) continuous? Is it differentiable, and what is \(A'\)? Does every \(f\) arise as a derivative? This lecture lets you test each answer yourself.

02Integration

The accumulation function

Definition. Let \(f\) be Riemann integrable on every closed bounded subinterval of an interval \(I\), and fix \(a\in I\). The accumulation function of \(f\) based at \(a\) is \[ A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt,\qquad x\in I, \] with the convention \(\int_a^x=-\int_x^a\) when \(x\lt a\). It records the signed area collected as you sweep from \(a\) to \(x\): area above the axis counts positively, area below counts negatively.

03Integration ⇒ Continuity

Accumulating area never makes a jump

Theorem. Let \(f\) be Riemann integrable on every closed bounded subinterval of an interval \(I\), \(a\in I\), and \(A(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt\). Then \(A\) is continuous on \(I\) and therefore has the Intermediate Value Property: if \(A(u)\lt L\lt A(v)\), then \(A(\alpha)=L\) for some \(\alpha\) between \(u\) and \(v\).

Proof. Fix \(x_0\in I\) and a closed bounded interval \(J\subseteq I\) that contains a neighbourhood of \(x_0\) in \(I\). A Riemann integrable function is bounded, so there is \(M\gt0\) with \(|f(t)|\le M\) on \(J\). For \(x\in J\), \[ |A(x)-A(x_0)|=\Bigl|\int_{x_0}^{x}f(t)\,dt\Bigr|\le\Bigl|\int_{x_0}^{x}|f(t)|\,dt\Bigr|\le M\,|x-x_0|. \] Given \(\varepsilon\gt0\) put \(\delta=\varepsilon/M\). Then \(|x-x_0|\lt\delta\) implies \(|A(x)-A(x_0)|\lt\varepsilon\), so \(A\) is continuous at \(x_0\). Since \(x_0\) was arbitrary, \(A\) is continuous on \(I\). The Intermediate Value Property is the standard Intermediate Value Theorem applied to the continuous \(A\) on \([u,v]\). \(\blacksquare\)

Explore · The cone and the \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) box

The bound \(|f|\le M\) forces the graph of \(A\) (cyan) to stay inside the violet cone through \((x_0,A(x_0))\), whose slopes are \(\pm M\). The gold box is the \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) picture: any \(\varepsilon\) is answered by \(\delta=\varepsilon/M\). Try the jump functions: \(A\) bends sharply, but it never breaks.

Smoothing. Even when \(f\) has a jump, \(A\) is continuous. Integration makes a function better behaved than the one you started with.
04Continuity ⇒ Differentiation

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, part 1

FTC-1. Let \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R\) be integrable and continuous at a point \(x\in(a,b)\). Then \(A(x)=\int_a^xf\) is differentiable at \(x\) and \(A'(x)=f(x)\). In particular, if \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), then \(A\) is a primitive of \(f\).

Proof. Step 1: the difference quotient is an average. For \(h\gt0\), \[ \frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h}=\frac1h\int_x^{x+h}f(t)\,dt. \] Step 2: squeeze between extremes. Let \(m_h\) and \(M_h\) be the smallest and largest values of \(f\) on \([x,x+h]\) (they exist by the Extreme Value Theorem when \(f\) is continuous there). Then \(m_h h\le\int_x^{x+h}f\le M_h h\), so \[ m_h\le\frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}{h}\le M_h. \] Step 3: let \(h\to0^+\). By continuity of \(f\) at \(x\), \(m_h\to f(x)\) and \(M_h\to f(x)\). By the Squeeze Theorem the quotient tends to \(f(x)\). The case \(h\to0^-\) is identical with the interval \([x+h,x]\). Hence \(A'(x)=f(x)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Explore · Watch the squeeze

The shaded strip over \([x,x+h]\) is the new area. Its average height (gold line) equals the difference quotient. It is trapped between the lowest (cyan) and highest (coral) heights of \(f\) on the strip. Shrink \(h\): the walls close in on \(f(x)\), the white dot.

A first consequence: chain rule with FTC-1. If \(G(x)=\int_3^{x^2}\cos t\,dt\), write \(G=A\circ u\) with \(A(s)=\int_3^s\cos t\,dt\) and \(u(x)=x^2\). Since \(A'=\cos\), the chain rule gives \(G'(x)=\cos(x^2)\cdot2x\).
05Differentiation ⇒ Integration

Primitives, tables and uniqueness

Definition (primitive). Let \(I\) be an interval. A function \(F:I\to\mathbb R\) is a primitive (or antiderivative) of \(f:I\to\mathbb R\) if \(F\) is differentiable at every point of \(I\) and \(F'(x)=f(x)\) for every \(x\in I\).

By FTC-1, a continuous \(f\) always has one: the accumulation function. But there are many more.

Theorem (uniqueness on an interval). If \(F\) and \(G\) are primitives of \(f\) on an interval \(I\), then \(G=F+C\) for a constant \(C\).

Proof. Let \(H=G-F\). Then \(H'=f-f=0\) on \(I\). Fix \(x_0\in I\). For any \(x\in I\) the segment between \(x_0\) and \(x\) lies in \(I\), and the Mean Value Theorem gives \(c\) between them with \(\dfrac{H(x)-H(x_0)}{x-x_0}=H'(c)=0\). So \(H(x)=H(x_0)\) for every \(x\): \(H\) is constant. \(\blacksquare\)

Explore · Is this a primitive? The checker

Top: \(f\) in cyan, and the numerically computed derivative of the candidate \(F+C\) as a gold dashed curve on top of it. For \(1/x\) and \(-1/x^2\) the domain has two pieces, so tick the box and use a different constant on each piece: it is still a primitive.

Pathology: a disconnected domain. On \(D=(-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)\) both \(F(x)=\tfrac1x\) and \(G(x)=\tfrac1x+5\) (for \(x\gt0\)), \(G(x)=\tfrac1x-12\) (for \(x\lt0\)) have derivative \(-\tfrac1{x^2}\). But \(G-F\) is \(+5\) on one piece and \(-12\) on the other: not a single constant. The uniqueness proof used that the segment from \(x_0\) to \(x\) stays in the domain, which fails when the domain is split.

06Differentiation ⇒ Continuity

When no primitive exists: Darboux

Does every function have a primitive? No. A derivative, even a wild one, cannot skip values.

Darboux's Theorem. If \(F\) is differentiable on \([a,b]\) and \(y\) lies strictly between \(F'(a)\) and \(F'(b)\), then \(F'(c)=y\) for some \(c\in(a,b)\).

Proof. Suppose \(F'(a)\lt y\lt F'(b)\) (the other case is the same with \(F\) replaced by \(-F\)). Let \(g(x)=F(x)-yx\). Then \(g\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(g'(a)=F'(a)-y\lt0\), \(g'(b)=F'(b)-y\gt0\). Since \(g'(a)\lt0\), \(g\) takes values below \(g(a)\) just to the right of \(a\); since \(g'(b)\gt0\), it takes values below \(g(b)\) just to the left of \(b\). So the minimum of \(g\) on \([a,b]\), which exists by the Extreme Value Theorem, is not attained at an endpoint. It is attained at some \(c\in(a,b)\), where Fermat's Lemma gives \(g'(c)=0\), i.e. \(F'(c)=y\). \(\blacksquare\)

Consequence. A function with a jump discontinuity has no primitive. For \(f=\operatorname{sgn}\) on \([-1,1]\), a primitive \(F\) would have \(F'=-1\) on the left and \(+1\) on the right, yet \(F'\) would have to take the value \(\tfrac12\) in between. It never does.
Explore · Corners and bad points

The special point \(p\) is where the shape of \(f\) matters. Compare the slope of \(A\) just to the left (coral) and just to the right (cyan). If they disagree, \(A\) has a corner. If both equal \(f(p)\), FTC-1 holds. The "one point" example is subtle: \(A\) is smooth, but its slope \(0\) is not \(f(0)=1\).

Moral. Integrability and having a primitive are different properties. Continuity is a safe guarantee for both, and it is the bridge between the three ideas.
07All three at once

Defining \(\log\) and \(\exp\) by integration

Here is the three-way story in action. We do not start with the logarithm: we define it as an accumulation function, and let the theorems of this lecture do the rest.

Definition. The function \(f(t)=1/t\) is continuous (hence integrable) on \((0,\infty)\). Define \[ \log x=\int_1^x\frac1t\,dt\qquad(x\gt0),\qquad\text{so }\log1=0. \]

Integration gave us the function; continuity of \(A\) follows from Section 3; and differentiation follows from FTC-1 because \(1/t\) is continuous: \[ \log'(x)=\frac1x. \]

Product rule: \(\log(ab)=\log a+\log b\). Split the integral and substitute \(t=au\) in the second part: \[ \log(ab)=\int_1^a\frac{dt}t+\int_a^{ab}\frac{dt}t=\log a+\int_1^b\frac{a\,du}{au}=\log a+\log b. \] Consequently \(\log(a/b)=\log a-\log b\) and \(\log(a^m)=m\log a\) for integers \(m\).

Strictly increasing. \(\log'(x)=1/x\gt0\), so \(\log\) is strictly increasing (increasing test). Also \(\log b-\log a=\int_a^b\frac{dt}t\gt0\) for \(b\gt a\).

Onto \(\mathbb R\). \(\log2\gt0\), and \(\log(2^n)=n\log2\to\infty\), \(\log(2^{-n})\to-\infty\). \(\log\) is continuous, so by the Intermediate Value Theorem it takes every real value. Hence \(\log:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb R\) is a bijection.

Explore · Why \(\log(ab)=\log a+\log b\)

All areas are computed numerically from \(\int dt/t\), not from a log button. The gold region over \([a,ab]\) (top) and the gold region over \([1,b]\) (bottom) have the same area, which is the heart of the substitution \(t=au\).

The exponential. Since \(\log:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb R\) is a strictly increasing bijection, it has an inverse \(\exp:\mathbb R\to(0,\infty)\), with \(y=\exp x\iff\log y=x\). Taking \(\log\) of both sides turns the properties of \(\log\) into those of \(\exp\): \(\exp(a+b)=\exp a\cdot\exp b\), \(\exp(a-b)=\exp a/\exp b\), \(\exp(ma)=(\exp a)^m\). And since \(\log'(y)=1/y\gt0\), the inverse is differentiable with \[ \exp'(x)=\frac1{\log'(\exp x)}=\exp x. \] The number \(e\) is defined by \(\log e=1\), i.e. by area \(=1\) under \(1/t\) from \(1\) to \(e\).

08Concept checks

Test yourself

Pick an answer; you will see the reasoning straight away.

Q1. Let \(G(x)=\int_3^{x^2}\cos t\,dt\). What is \(G^{\prime}(x)\)?

FTC-1 gives \(A^{\prime}(s)=\cos s\) for \(A(s)=\int_3^s\cos t\,dt\); the chain rule with \(s=x^2\) multiplies by \(2x\). Option C is the value of \(G\), not its derivative.

Q2. Is every Riemann integrable function on \([a,b]\) guaranteed to have a primitive?

Integrable functions may have jumps; derivatives cannot. Continuity, not integrability, is the safe sufficient condition.

Q3. Let \(f(t)=\operatorname{sgn}t\) and \(A(x)=\int_0^xf\). Then \(A(x)=|x|\). What is \(A^{\prime}(0)\)?

The left slope is \(-1\) and the right slope is \(+1\): a corner. \(A\) is continuous at \(0\) (Section 3) but not differentiable there.

Q4. On \((-\infty,0)\cup(0,\infty)\), how many free constants does the family of primitives of \(-1/x^2\) have?

The domain is not an interval, so the Mean Value Theorem argument only shows that \(G-F\) is constant on each piece separately.

Q5. Why does \(F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt\) have the Intermediate Value Property even when \(f\) has jumps?

Boundedness of \(f\) gives a Lipschitz bound for \(F\), hence continuity, and the Intermediate Value Theorem applies to \(F\).

Q6. Let \(f(t)=0\) for \(t\ne0\) and \(f(0)=1\), and \(F(x)=\int_0^xf\). What is \(F^{\prime}(0)\)?

A single point does not change an integral, so \(F\equiv0\) and \(F^{\prime}(0)=0\ne f(0)\). FTC-1 needs continuity of \(f\) at the point.

Q7. With \(\log x=\int_1^x\frac{dt}t\), how do we know \(\log^{\prime}(x)=1/x\)?

FTC-1 applies to any accumulation function of a continuous integrand. No formula for \(\log\) is needed.
