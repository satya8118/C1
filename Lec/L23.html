Lecture 23 — Carathéodory's Criterion

Differentiability as factorisation, and what follows from it  ·  Calculus / Real Analysis

PART 1 · THE CRITERION

Carathéodory's characterisation of differentiability

Last time, differentiability meant a limit of a quotient. Carathéodory replaced the limit by a factorisation plus continuity, which is far easier to compute with (products, chains, inverses).

Theorem (Carathéodory)
Let $f:J\to\mathbb R$ and $c\in J$. Then $f$ is differentiable at $c$ if and only if there is a function $f_1:J\to\mathbb R$ such that
  1. $f(x)=f(c)+f_1(x)\,(x-c)$ for all $x\in J$, and
  2. $f_1$ is continuous at $c$.
In that case $f^{\prime}(c)=f_1(c)$.
How to read it
For $x\ne c$ condition 1 forces $f_1(x)=\dfrac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}$, the chord slope. The only freedom is the value $f_1(c)$, and condition 2 says: the chord-slope function can be completed continuously at $c$. The completing value is the derivative.
Chord-slope function $f_1$. Left: $f(x)=x^2$, $c=1$: $f_1(x)=x+1$ has a hole at $x=1$ that is filled continuously by $f_1(1)=2=f^{\prime}(1)$. Right: $f(x)=|x|$, $c=0$: $f_1=\pm1$ jumps, no value at $0$ can fill it, so $|x|$ is not differentiable at $0$.

($\Leftarrow$) A factorisation gives differentiability

Proof. Let $f_1$ satisfy 1 and 2, and put $\alpha=f_1(c)$. For $x\ne c$, $$|f(x)-f(c)-\alpha(x-c)|=|x-c|\,|f_1(x)-f_1(c)| .$$ Given $\varepsilon>0$, continuity of $f_1$ at $c$ gives $\delta>0$ with $|f_1(x)-f_1(c)|<\varepsilon$ whenever $|x-c|<\delta$. Then for $0<|x-c|<\delta$ the error is $<\varepsilon|x-c|$, so $f^{\prime}(c)=f_1(c)$. $\blacksquare$

($\Rightarrow$) Differentiability gives a factorisation

✎ Strategy Condition 1 dictates $f_1(x)$ for $x\ne c$. Continuity at $c$ then dictates $f_1(c)=\lim_{x\to c}f_1(x)=f^{\prime}(c)$. Define it that way and check.
Proof. Let $f$ be differentiable at $c$ and define $$f_1(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c},&x\ne c,\\[8pt] f^{\prime}(c),&x=c.\end{cases}$$ Condition 1. For $x\ne c$ multiply by $x-c$. For $x=c$ both sides equal $f(c)$. Condition 2. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}f_1(x)=\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}=f^{\prime}(c)=f_1(c)$. $\blacksquare$
PART 2 · CONSEQUENCES

What the criterion gives us for free

Corollary: differentiable ⟹ continuous
If $f$ is differentiable at $c$, then $f$ is continuous at $c$.
Proof. $f(x)=f(c)+f_1(x)(x-c)$ with $f_1$ continuous at $c$, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=f(c)+f_1(c)\cdot 0=f(c).$ $\blacksquare$
Direct proof. $|f(x)-f(c)|=\Bigl|\tfrac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}\Bigr|\,|x-c|\to|f^{\prime}(c)|\cdot0=0.$ $\blacksquare$
The converse is false
$f(x)=|x|$ is continuous everywhere but not differentiable at $0$: the chord slopes are $-1$ on the left and $+1$ on the right. Weierstrass (1872) gave a function that is continuous on all of $\mathbb R$ and differentiable at no point.
Theorem (Chain Rule)
Let $I,J$ be intervals, $g:I\to\mathbb R$ with $g(I)\subseteq J$, and $f:J\to\mathbb R$. If $g$ is differentiable at $c\in I$ and $f$ is differentiable at $g(c)$, then $f\circ g$ is differentiable at $c$ and $$(f\circ g)^{\prime}(c)=f^{\prime}\bigl(g(c)\bigr)\,g^{\prime}(c).$$
✎ Why the naive proof needs care The tempting computation $$\frac{f(g(x))-f(g(c))}{x-c}=\frac{f(g(x))-f(g(c))}{g(x)-g(c)}\cdot\frac{g(x)-g(c)}{x-c}$$ divides by $g(x)-g(c)$, which can be $0$ for $x$ arbitrarily close to $c$ (e.g. $g(x)=x^2\sin(1/x)$ near $0$). Carathéodory's factorisation avoids the division altogether.
Proof. Put $d=g(c)$.
Step 1: factorise both functions. By the theorem there are $g_1:I\to\mathbb R$ continuous at $c$ and $f_1:J\to\mathbb R$ continuous at $d$ with $$g(x)=g(c)+g_1(x)(x-c)\ \ (x\in I),\qquad f(y)=f(d)+f_1(y)(y-d)\ \ (y\in J),$$ and $g_1(c)=g^{\prime}(c)$, $f_1(d)=f^{\prime}(d)$.
Step 2: substitute. For $x\in I$ we have $g(x)\in J$, so we may take $y=g(x)$: $$\begin{aligned}f(g(x))&=f(d)+f_1\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\bigl(g(x)-d\bigr)\\&=f(g(c))+f_1\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\,g_1(x)\,(x-c).\end{aligned}$$
Step 3: continuity of the new factor. Let $\phi(x)=f_1\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\,g_1(x)$. Since $g$ is differentiable at $c$, it is continuous at $c$; since $f_1$ is continuous at $g(c)=d$, the composition $f_1\circ g$ is continuous at $c$. The function $g_1$ is continuous at $c$, so the product $\phi$ is continuous at $c$.
Step 4: conclude. We have shown $(f\circ g)(x)=(f\circ g)(c)+\phi(x)(x-c)$ for all $x\in I$ with $\phi$ continuous at $c$. By Carathéodory's theorem $f\circ g$ is differentiable at $c$ and $$(f\circ g)^{\prime}(c)=\phi(c)=f_1\bigl(g(c)\bigr)g_1(c)=f^{\prime}\bigl(g(c)\bigr)\,g^{\prime}(c).\qquad\blacksquare$$
Example
$h(x)=(3x^2+1)^5$ is $f\circ g$ with $g(x)=3x^2+1$ and $f(y)=y^5$. Then $h^{\prime}(x)=5(3x^2+1)^4\cdot6x=30x\,(3x^2+1)^4$.

SELF-ASSESSMENT A

Definitions and basic computations

Q1. State the ratio form and the increment form of differentiability at $c$. How are they related?

Answer. Ratio: $\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}=\alpha$. Increment: $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}=\alpha$. They are the same statement under $x=c+h$: $x\to c$ iff $h\to0$.

Q2. Prove from the definition that $\sqrt x$ has derivative $\frac1{2\sqrt c}$ at every $c>0$.

Proof. For $x>0,\ x\ne c$: $\dfrac{\sqrt x-\sqrt c}{x-c}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt x+\sqrt c}\to\dfrac1{2\sqrt c}$ as $x\to c$. $\blacksquare$

Q3. Show that $|x|$ is not differentiable at $0$ using the definition.

Proof. $\dfrac{|h|}{h}=+1$ for $h>0$ and $-1$ for $h<0$. The one-sided limits differ, so $\lim_{h\to0}|h|/h$ does not exist. $\blacksquare$

Q4. Use the error form to prove $f(x)=x^2$ has $f^{\prime}(3)=6$.

Proof. With $\alpha=6$: $|x^2-9-6(x-3)|=(x-3)^2$. Given $\varepsilon>0$ take $\delta=\varepsilon$; for $0<|x-3|<\delta$ we get $(x-3)^2<\varepsilon|x-3|$. $\blacksquare$

Q5. Explain why differentiability implies continuity, and give an example where the converse fails.

Answer. $|f(x)-f(c)|=\bigl|\tfrac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}\bigr||x-c|\to f^{\prime}(c)\cdot0=0$. Converse fails for $|x|$ at $0$: continuous there, but there is a corner (Q3).
SELF-ASSESSMENT B

Carathéodory and deeper results

Q6. State Carathéodory’s criterion in your own words. For $f(x)=x^3$ and $c=2$ find $f_1$ and verify both conditions.

Answer. $f_1(x)=\dfrac{x^3-8}{x-2}=x^2+2x+4$ for $x\ne2$, and $f_1(2)=12=f^{\prime}(2)$. Condition 1: $8+(x^2+2x+4)(x-2)=x^3$. Condition 2: $x^2+2x+4\to12=f_1(2)$.

Q7. Prove the Product Rule with the criterion.

Proof. Write $f=f(c)+f_1(x-c)$, $g=g(c)+g_1(x-c)$. Then $fg=f(c)g(c)+\bigl[f(c)g_1+g(c)f_1+f_1g_1(x-c)\bigr](x-c)$. The bracket $h_1$ is continuous at $c$ with $h_1(c)=f(c)g^{\prime}(c)+g(c)f^{\prime}(c)$. So $(fg)^{\prime}(c)=f(c)g^{\prime}(c)+f^{\prime}(c)g(c)$. $\blacksquare$

Q8. Let $f(x)=x^2\sin(1/x)$ for $x\ne0$ and $f(0)=0$. Show $f^{\prime}(0)$ exists and find it.

Proof. $\dfrac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}=h\sin(1/h)$ and $|h\sin(1/h)|\le|h|\to0$. By the sandwich lemma $f^{\prime}(0)=0$. $\blacksquare$

Q9. Write the error form with quantifiers and say what each means. Why $0<|x-c|$ rather than $|x-c|<\delta$?

Answer. $\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0:\ x\in J,\,0<|x-c|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(c)-\alpha(x-c)|<\varepsilon|x-c|$. Here $\varepsilon$ is any tolerance for the relative error, $\delta$ is our reply, and $\alpha$ is fixed first. At $x=c$ both sides are $0$ and the strict inequality $0<0$ would fail, so $x=c$ is excluded.

Q10. Let $f,g$ be differentiable at $c$. Prove that $f+g$ is differentiable at $c$ and $(f+g)^{\prime}(c)=f^{\prime}(c)+g^{\prime}(c)$.

Proof. Proof (Carathéodory). Write $f(x)=f(c)+f_1(x)(x-c)$ and $g(x)=g(c)+g_1(x)(x-c)$ with $f_1,g_1$ continuous at $c$. Adding, $$(f+g)(x)=(f+g)(c)+\bigl[f_1(x)+g_1(x)\bigr](x-c).$$ The bracket is continuous at $c$ (a sum of functions continuous at $c$), with value $f_1(c)+g_1(c)=f^{\prime}(c)+g^{\prime}(c)$. By the criterion, $(f+g)^{\prime}(c)=f^{\prime}(c)+g^{\prime}(c)$. $\blacksquare$
Direct proof. $\dfrac{(f+g)(x)-(f+g)(c)}{x-c}=\dfrac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}+\dfrac{g(x)-g(c)}{x-c}\to f^{\prime}(c)+g^{\prime}(c)$ by the sum law for limits. The same argument gives $(f-g)^{\prime}(c)=f^{\prime}(c)-g^{\prime}(c)$.

Q11. Let $f$ be differentiable at $c$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb R$. Prove that $\lambda f$ is differentiable at $c$ and $(\lambda f)^{\prime}(c)=\lambda f^{\prime}(c)$.

Proof. Proof (Carathéodory). From $f(x)=f(c)+f_1(x)(x-c)$ we get $$(\lambda f)(x)=(\lambda f)(c)+\bigl[\lambda f_1(x)\bigr](x-c).$$ The function $\lambda f_1$ is continuous at $c$ with value $\lambda f_1(c)=\lambda f^{\prime}(c)$. Hence $(\lambda f)^{\prime}(c)=\lambda f^{\prime}(c)$. $\blacksquare$
Direct proof. $\dfrac{\lambda f(x)-\lambda f(c)}{x-c}=\lambda\cdot\dfrac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}\to\lambda f^{\prime}(c)$. Consequence: differentiation is linear, $(\lambda f+\mu g)^{\prime}(c)=\lambda f^{\prime}(c)+\mu g^{\prime}(c)$.

Q12. Find the derivative of $5x^4-3x^2+7x-2$ using the power rule together with Q10 and Q11.

Solution. Differentiability is preserved under sums and scalar multiples (limits of sums and multiples). So the derivative is $5\cdot4c^3-3\cdot2c+7-0=20c^3-6c+7$.
SELF-ASSESSMENT C

Challenge problems

Q13. Let $f(x)=x^2$ for $x\in\mathbb Q$ and $f(x)=0$ otherwise. Show $f$ is differentiable at $0$ and at no other point.

Proof. At $0$: $\bigl|\tfrac{f(h)}{h}\bigr|\le\tfrac{h^2}{|h|}=|h|\to0$, so $f^{\prime}(0)=0$. At $c\ne0$: take rationals $r_n\to c$ and irrationals $s_n\to c$. If $c$ is rational, $f(s_n)=0\not\to c^2=f(c)$. If $c$ is irrational, $f(r_n)=r_n^2\to c^2\ne0=f(c)$. Either way $f$ is discontinuous at $c$, hence not differentiable there. $\blacksquare$

Q14. Suppose $f$ is differentiable at $c$ and $f^{\prime}(c)\ne0$. Prove there is $\delta>0$ with $f(x)\ne f(c)$ for $0<|x-c|<\delta$.

Proof. Write $f(x)-f(c)=f_1(x)(x-c)$ with $f_1(c)=f^{\prime}(c)\ne0$. By continuity of $f_1$ there is $\delta>0$ with $|f_1(x)-f^{\prime}(c)|<|f^{\prime}(c)|/2$, so $f_1(x)\ne0$ on $|x-c|<\delta$. Then $f(x)-f(c)\ne0$ for $x\ne c$. $\blacksquare$
Warning. This does not make $f$ one-to-one near $c$. Take $f(x)=x+2x^2\sin(1/x)$, $f(0)=0$: $f^{\prime}(0)=1$, but $f^{\prime}(x)=1+4x\sin(1/x)-2\cos(1/x)$ equals $-1$ at $x=\frac1{2\pi k}$ and $3$ at $x=\frac1{(2k+1)\pi}$, so $f$ is not monotone, hence not injective, on any interval around $0$. Injectivity needs $f^{\prime}$ continuous at $c$.

Q15. Let $f(x)=x\sin(1/x)$ for $x\ne0$, $f(0)=0$. Show $f$ is continuous but not differentiable at $0$.

Solution. $|f(x)|\le|x|\to0$, so $f$ is continuous at $0$. But $\dfrac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}=\sin(1/h)$ oscillates between $-1$ and $1$ as $h\to0$, so it has no limit. Compare Q8: the extra factor $h$ damps the oscillation. $\blacksquare$

Q16. Prove the Quotient Rule from the definition, for $g(c)\ne0$.

Proof. Since $g$ is continuous at $c$ and $g(c)\ne0$, $g\ne0$ near $c$. There $\dfrac{f(x)/g(x)-f(c)/g(c)}{x-c}=\dfrac1{g(x)g(c)}\Bigl[\dfrac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}\,g(c)-f(c)\dfrac{g(x)-g(c)}{x-c}\Bigr]$. Let $x\to c$ (using $g(x)\to g(c)$): $(f/g)^{\prime}(c)=\dfrac{f^{\prime}(c)g(c)-f(c)g^{\prime}(c)}{g(c)^2}$. $\blacksquare$
