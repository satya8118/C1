Calculus · Limits

The Limit of a Function

Before calculus can define a slope at a single instant or an area under a curve, it needs to make precise the phrase "gets closer and closer to." This lesson builds that treated by the book by Thomas & Finney do — starting from a function that's undefined exactly where we want to look, building a table of values, and only then writing down a definition for what we've already seen happening.

01A function with a hole in it

A function that's almost defined

The setup Consider \(f(x)=\dfrac{x^2-4}{x-2}\). Try plugging in \(x=2\): you get \(\dfrac{0}{0}\), which is undefined. So \(f\) has no value at \(x=2\) — there's a genuine hole in its domain right there. And yet, for every \(x\ne2\), we can simplify: \[ f(x)=\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}=\frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2}=x+2 \quad (x\ne2). \]

Away from \(x=2\), \(f\) is just the line \(y=x+2\) — and that line has a perfectly good value at \(x=2\): namely \(4\). The question calculus asks isn't "what is \(f(2)\)?" (there's no answer) but rather: as \(x\) gets closer and closer to \(2\), what does \(f(x)\) get closer and closer to?

Graph of f(x) = (x² − 4)/(x − 2)

The graph is the line \(y=x+2\) everywhere except a single open hole at \((2,4)\) — drawn as a hollow circle. The function is undefined right at the hole, but the line clearly aims straight at the point \((2,4)\) from both sides.

The key observation No matter how close \(x\) gets to \(2\) — from either direction — \(f(x)\) gets correspondingly close to \(4\). We say: the limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(2\) is \(4\), written \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2}f(x)=4\) — regardless of the fact that \(f(2)\) itself doesn't exist.
03Putting words to what we just saw

The informal definition of a limit

Informal Definition — Limit of a Function Let \(f(x)\) be defined on an open interval about \(c\), except possibly at \(c\) itself. If \(f(x)\) gets arbitrarily close to \(L\) for all \(x\) sufficiently close to \(c\) (on either side of \(c\)), we say the limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(c\) is \(L\), written \[ \lim_{x\to c} f(x) = L. \]
Three things worth underlining
  1. \(f\) need not be defined at \(c\). The whole point of our example was a function undefined exactly at the point we cared about.
  2. Even if \(f(c)\) exists, the limit doesn't have to equal it. A function's value at a point and its limit at that point are, in principle, two entirely different questions — they usually agree, but not always, as we'll see.
  3. "Approaches" means from both directions at once. Both the left-hand approach and the right-hand approach must reach the same target \(L\) for the (two-sided) limit to exist — our next section makes this precise.

04Approaching from just one direction

One-sided limits

Sometimes a function behaves completely differently depending on which side you approach from — a jump in the graph. To describe this, we separate the two directions.

Right-hand limit \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c^+}f(x)=L\) means \(f(x)\to L\) as \(x\) approaches \(c\) through values greater than \(c\).
Left-hand limit \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c^-}f(x)=L\) means \(f(x)\to L\) as \(x\) approaches \(c\) through values less than \(c\).
The two-sided limit exists exactly when both agree \[ \lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L \quad\iff\quad \lim_{x\to c^-}f(x)=L \ \text{ and }\ \lim_{x\to c^+}f(x)=L. \] If the left- and right-hand limits disagree, the (two-sided) limit simply does not exist at \(c\) — there's no single number both sides are heading toward.

\(g(x)=x+1\) for \(x<1\), and \(g(x)=x+2\) for \(x\ge1\). The left-hand limit heads to \(2\); the right-hand limit heads to \(3\). Since \(2\ne3\), \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}g(x)\) does not exist — even though both one-sided limits exist individually.

05A field guide to "does not exist"

Three ways a limit can fail to exist

"The limit doesn't exist" isn't one phenomenon — it's a family of different behaviors. Thomas & Finney catalogue three classic types.

Type 1 — Jump

Left- and right-hand limits both exist, but disagree — like \(g(x)\) above. Different targets, no single limit.

Type 2 — Unbounded

\(f(x)=\dfrac{1}{x^2}\) as \(x\to0\): values grow without bound. There's no finite number the function is heading toward.

Type 3 — Oscillation

\(f(x)=\sin(1/x)\) as \(x\to0\): the function oscillates between \(-1\) and \(1\) infinitely often, faster and faster, never settling.

Check yourself

Which of the three types above best describes \(f(x)=\dfrac1x\) as \(x\to0\)? (Consider both \(x\to0^-\) and \(x\to0^+\) separately first.)

As \(x\to0^+\), \(\frac1x\to+\infty\); as \(x\to0^-\), \(\frac1x\to-\infty\). This is really a blend of Types 1 and 2 — the one-sided behaviors don't even settle on finite numbers to disagree about, so the limit fails to exist for the "unbounded" reason on each side individually, and the two sides don't match either.

06A limit you'll use constantly

The famous limit: lim (sin x)/x = 1

\(f(x)=\dfrac{\sin x}{x}\) is undefined at \(x=0\) (again, \(\frac00\)) — but the table of values tells a striking story.

Remark Notice the pattern from Section 1 repeating: a function undefined exactly at the interesting point, whose values nonetheless approach a clear, finite target from both sides. This is the single most common shape a "find the limit" problem takes throughout calculus.
07Building bigger limits from small ones

Limit laws (informal statement)

Once you know a couple of basic limits, these rules let you combine them without going back to a table every time. Suppose \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L\) and \(\lim_{x\to c}g(x)=M\), both finite.

RuleStatement
Sum\(\lim_{x\to c}[f(x)+g(x)]=L+M\)
Difference\(\lim_{x\to c}[f(x)-g(x)]=L-M\)
Constant multiple\(\lim_{x\to c}[k\cdot f(x)]=k L\)
Product\(\lim_{x\to c}[f(x)g(x)]=LM\)
Quotient\(\lim_{x\to c}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\dfrac{L}{M}\), provided \(M\ne0\)
Power\(\lim_{x\to c}[f(x)]^n=L^n\)
Why this matters in practice Two limits every calculus student should have on instant recall: \(\lim_{x\to c}k=k\) (a constant function's limit is that constant) and \(\lim_{x\to c}x=c\) (the identity function's limit is just \(c\)). Combine those two with the rules above, and you can compute the limit of any polynomial or rational function (where the denominator's limit isn't zero) just by substituting \(x=c\) directly — no table needed.
Try it

Using the rules above, evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to3}(2x^2-5x+1)\).

Since this is a polynomial, direct substitution works: \(2(3)^2-5(3)+1=18-15+1=4\).
08Self-Assessment Quiz

Check your understanding

Part A — recall

State the informal definition of \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L\) in your own words.

As \(x\) gets arbitrarily close to \(c\) (from either side, without necessarily equaling \(c\)), the values \(f(x)\) get arbitrarily close to \(L\).
Part A — recall

True or false: if \(f(c)\) is undefined, then \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\) cannot exist.

False. Section 1's example, \(f(x)=\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}\), is undefined at \(x=2\), yet \(\lim_{x\to2}f(x)=4\) exists perfectly well. A function's value at a point and its limit at that point are separate questions.
Part B — apply

Let \(h(x)=3\) for \(x<0\) and \(h(x)=3\) for \(x>0\), but \(h(0)=9\). Does \(\lim_{x\to0}h(x)\) exist? If so, what is it?

Yes, it exists and equals \(3\). The limit only cares about values near \(0\), not the value at \(0\) — both one-sided limits are \(3\), so \(\lim_{x\to0}h(x)=3\), even though \(h(0)=9\) disagrees with it.
Part B — apply

For the jump function \(g(x)\) from Section 4 (\(g(x)=x+1\) for \(x<1\), \(g(x)=x+2\) for \(x\ge1\)), find \(\lim_{x\to1^-}g(x)\), \(\lim_{x\to1^+}g(x)\), and \(g(1)\).

\(\lim_{x\to1^-}g(x)=1+1=2\). \(\lim_{x\to1^+}g(x)=1+2=3\). \(g(1)=1+2=3\) (since the rule for \(x\ge1\) applies at \(x=1\)). Three genuinely different numbers — a good reminder that a one-sided limit, the function's actual value, and the two-sided limit (which here doesn't exist) can all disagree.
Part C — think

Sketch (mentally or on paper) a function with \(\lim_{x\to2^-}f(x)=5\), \(\lim_{x\to2^+}f(x)=5\), but \(f(2)=0\). Does \(\lim_{x\to2}f(x)\) exist? What's special — or not special — about this situation?

Yes, \(\lim_{x\to2}f(x)=5\) exists, exactly like Section 1's hole example — the limit is entirely about the approach, not the landing value. What's slightly different here from Section 1 is that \(f(2)\) is defined (equal to \(0\)) but simply doesn't match the limit — a removable discontinuity with an actual (wrong) value plugged in, rather than an undefined hole. Both situations lead to the same lesson: don't confuse "the function's value at \(c\)" with "the limit as \(x\to c\)."
Part C — think

Why does Type 3 (oscillation, like \(\sin(1/x)\) near \(0\)) not fit neatly into either "jump" or "unbounded"? What makes it a genuinely different failure mode?

A jump has two well-defined one-sided limits that simply disagree — the function still "settles" on each side. Unbounded behavior heads toward \(\pm\infty\) in a consistent direction. Oscillation does neither: \(\sin(1/x)\) keeps sweeping through every value between \(-1\) and \(1\) infinitely often as \(x\to0\), never settling toward any single number, however briefly, from either side. There isn't even a one-sided limit to speak of — the failure is more severe than a mere disagreement between two sides.
Where this goes next. With the informal picture in hand, the next natural step is the precise \(\varepsilon\text{-}\delta\) definition — turning "arbitrarily close" into an actual inequality you can prove things with. From there: continuity (what it means for a function to have no holes or jumps at all), and then the derivative itself, which is nothing but a very particular limit — the limit of a slope.
