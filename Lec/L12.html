Calculus · Limits, Part II

The Precise Definition of a Limit

Last time we said "\(f(x)\) gets arbitrarily close to \(L\) as \(x\) gets arbitrarily close to \(c\)" and left it at that — good enough to build intuition, not good enough to prove anything with. Today we turn "arbitrarily close" into an actual inequality: the \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) definition that every rigorous limit argument in this course rests on.

01Closing the gap in "arbitrarily close"

Why the informal definition isn't enough

"Gets arbitrarily close" is a fine intuition, but it's not a sentence you can do algebra with. It doesn't tell you how close is close enough, doesn't let you check a claimed limit is actually correct, and gives you nothing to write in a proof. What we need is a way to say "close" that comes with an actual number attached.

The strategy We'll measure closeness on the output side with a tolerance \(\varepsilon\) (epsilon) — how far \(f(x)\) is allowed to stray from \(L\) — and on the input side with a tolerance \(\delta\) (delta) — how far \(x\) is allowed to stray from \(c\). The claim "\(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L\)" will mean: no matter how small a tolerance \(\varepsilon\) you demand on the output, some tolerance \(\delta\) on the input is enough to guarantee it.
02A physical picture first

The generator shaft analogy

Picture a machinist manufacturing a cylindrical shaft that needs to fit a generator housing to a tight tolerance. The target diameter is \(L\). Nothing manufactured is ever perfectly exact, so the machinist works to whatever precision the customer demands: "the diameter must land within \(\varepsilon\) of \(L\)."

The machinist's problem

The customer sets \(\varepsilon\) — the allowed error in diameter. The machinist's job is to find a corresponding setting on the lathe that guarantees the finished shaft lands within that window.

Where the two tolerances come from The machinist controls the lathe's cutting position — call it \(x\), with the ideal setting at \(c\). No control setting is perfectly exact either, so the achieved diameter \(f(x)\) depends on how precisely \(x\) is set. The question becomes: how tight must the control setting be — that is, how small must \(|x-c|\) be — to guarantee the diameter \(f(x)\) lands within \(\varepsilon\) of \(L\)? That required tightness on the control setting is exactly what we'll call \(\delta\).

As the customer's demanded diameter tolerance \(\varepsilon\) gets stricter, the machinist typically has to tighten the control setting tolerance \(\delta\) to match. That back-and-forth — a smaller \(\varepsilon\) forcing a smaller \(\delta\) — is precisely the logical structure the formal definition captures.

03The definition

The ε–δ definition of a limit

Definition — Limit of a Function Let \(f(x)\) be defined on an open interval about \(c\), except possibly at \(c\) itself. We say that the limit of \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(c\) is the number \(L\), and write \[ \lim_{x\to c} f(x) = L, \] if for every number \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists a corresponding number \(\delta>0\) such that for all \(x\), \[ 0<|x-c|<\delta \quad\implies\quad |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon. \]
Reading the definition piece by piece
  • \(0<|x-c|\) — this rules out \(x=c\) itself. The limit never depends on what happens (or doesn't happen) exactly at \(c\).
  • \(|x-c|<\delta\) — \(x\) is within \(\delta\) of \(c\); together with the line above, \(x\) is near but not equal to \(c\).
  • \(|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon\) — the output \(f(x)\) is within \(\varepsilon\) of \(L\).
  • "For every \(\varepsilon\), there exists \(\delta\)" — the order of the quantifiers matters enormously: \(\varepsilon\) is chosen first (by an adversary, demanding precision), and only then do you get to choose \(\delta\) in response. \(\delta\) is allowed to depend on \(\varepsilon\) — and in practice, always does.

04Making the quantifiers concrete

The challenge–response game

Here's the cleanest way to feel the logic of "for every \(\varepsilon\), there's a \(\delta\)": treat it as a game. An opponent names a tolerance \(\varepsilon\), as small and mean as they like. You must respond with a \(\delta\) that works. If you succeed, they're allowed to come back with an even smaller \(\varepsilon\) — and you must find an even smaller \(\delta\). The limit statement is true exactly when you can always win, no matter how small they push \(\varepsilon\).

Spot the pattern

After a few rounds, do you notice a relationship between the winning \(\delta\) and the challenge \(\varepsilon\)? (We'll prove this pattern algebraically in Section 6.)

For \(f(x)=2x\), the winning \(\delta\) is always exactly \(\varepsilon/2\). Since the slope is \(2\), doubling the input-distance doubles the output-distance — so to keep the output within \(\varepsilon\), the input must stay within \(\varepsilon/2\). This is no coincidence; it's exactly the formula Section 6 derives for every linear function.
05Worked example 1

The two easiest possible limits

Before tackling anything curved, verify the definition on the two functions where there's nothing to compute at all.

Constant function: prove lim(x→c) k = k

Given \(\varepsilon>0\), what \(\delta\) works? (Trick question — think about what \(|f(x)-L|\) even equals here.)

Here \(f(x)=k\) and \(L=k\), so \(|f(x)-L|=|k-k|=0<\varepsilon\) for every \(x\), regardless of how close \(x\) is to \(c\). So literally any \(\delta>0\) works — say \(\delta=1\). The implication \(0<|x-c|<1\implies|k-k|<\varepsilon\) is true no matter what, since the right side is always true.
Identity function: prove lim(x→c) x = c

Given \(\varepsilon>0\), what \(\delta\) works? Hint: what is \(|f(x)-L|\) here, exactly?

Here \(f(x)=x\) and \(L=c\), so \(|f(x)-L|=|x-c|\) — literally the same quantity the hypothesis controls. So choose \(\delta=\varepsilon\) exactly: then \(0<|x-c|<\delta=\varepsilon\) directly gives \(|f(x)-L|=|x-c|<\varepsilon\). No algebra needed — the input tolerance and output tolerance are literally the same number for this function.
06Worked example 2

A general linear function: lim(x→c) (mx + b) = mc + b

Assume \(m\ne0\). This confirms the pattern the challenge game already revealed.

Try it yourself

Start by simplifying \(|f(x)-L|=|(mx+b)-(mc+b)|\) — factor out \(m\) — and solve the resulting inequality for \(|x-c|\).

\[ |f(x)-L| = |(mx+b)-(mc+b)| = |mx-mc| = |m||x-c|. \]

We want this \(<\varepsilon\), i.e. \(|m||x-c|<\varepsilon\), i.e. \(|x-c|<\dfrac{\varepsilon}{|m|}\). So choose \(\delta=\dfrac{\varepsilon}{|m|}\). Check: if \(0<|x-c|<\delta=\dfrac{\varepsilon}{|m|}\), then \(|f(x)-L|=|m||x-c|<|m|\cdot\dfrac{\varepsilon}{|m|}=\varepsilon\). Done — for every \(\varepsilon>0\), this \(\delta\) works.

Matches the game For \(f(x)=2x\) (so \(m=2\)), this formula gives \(\delta=\varepsilon/2\) — exactly the pattern from Section 4's game.
07Worked example 3

A curved function: lim(x→2) x² = 4

Now \(|f(x)-L|=|x^2-4|=|x-2||x+2|\) — a product of two factors, only one of which is directly controlled by \(\delta\). This is the first genuinely new idea: we need to bound the uncontrolled factor first.

Try it yourself

Suppose we agree, as a first restriction, that \(\delta\le1\). What does that force \(x\) to satisfy, and what bound does that put on \(|x+2|\)?

Factor: \(|x^2-4|=|x-2|\cdot|x+2|\). If we additionally require \(\delta\le1\), then \(|x-2|<\delta\le1\) forces \(1

Now \(|x^2-4|=|x-2||x+2|<5|x-2|\). We want this \(<\varepsilon\), so it's enough to have \(|x-2|<\dfrac\varepsilon5\). Combined with the earlier restriction \(\delta\le1\):

\[ \boxed{\delta=\min\left(1,\ \frac\varepsilon5\right)} \]

Check: if \(0<|x-2|<\delta\), then \(|x-2|<1\) (giving \(|x+2|<5\)) and \(|x-2|<\varepsilon/5\), so \(|x^2-4|=|x-2||x+2|<\dfrac\varepsilon5\cdot5=\varepsilon\). Done.

The general technique Whenever \(|f(x)-L|\) factors into "\((x-c)\) times something else," first impose a convenient restriction like \(\delta\le1\) to trap the "something else" inside a fixed numerical bound. Only then solve for how small \(\delta\) needs to be to control the whole product — and take the smaller of the two requirements. This two-step move (bound, then solve) is the standard opening for almost every non-linear \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) proof you'll meet.

08Before we move to continuity

Self-assessment

Part A — recall

Write the ε–δ definition of \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L\) from memory.

For every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists \(\delta>0\) such that for all \(x\), \(0<|x-c|<\delta\implies|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon\).
Part A — recall

In the definition, which is chosen first — \(\varepsilon\) or \(\delta\)? Why does the order matter?

\(\varepsilon\) is chosen first, and \(\delta\) is found in response to it — \(\delta\) is allowed to (and generally does) depend on \(\varepsilon\). If the order were reversed, the statement would be a completely different (and far stronger, usually false) claim: one single \(\delta\) that works for every \(\varepsilon\) at once.
Part B — apply

Using Section 6's formula, find a \(\delta\) that proves \(\lim_{x\to5}(3x-1)=14\) for \(\varepsilon=0.06\).

Here \(m=3\), so \(\delta=\varepsilon/|m|=0.06/3=0.02\).
Part B — apply

For \(\lim_{x\to2}x^2=4\) (Section 7) with \(\varepsilon=0.02\), what does the formula \(\delta=\min(1,\varepsilon/5)\) give? Which of the two terms is doing the limiting?

\(\varepsilon/5=0.004\), and \(\min(1,0.004)=0.004\). Here the \(\varepsilon/5\) term is the binding constraint — for small enough \(\varepsilon\), it's always the smaller of the two, since \(1\) is a fixed number but \(\varepsilon/5\to0\).
Part C — think

Why did the constant-function proof (Section 5) not need to use \(\varepsilon\) at all when choosing \(\delta\)? Connect this to the general "bound, then solve" technique from Section 7.

Because \(|f(x)-L|=0\) identically — the output-side quantity we needed to control was already zero, with nothing left depending on \(x\) or \(\delta\) at all. In Section 7's language, there was no "\(|x-c|\) times something" to factor; the something was already gone. It's the most extreme case of the general technique: sometimes the bound alone finishes the job, with no "solve" step required.
Part C — think

Construct, in outline, what you think the \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) proof for \(\lim_{x\to3}x^3=27\) would need. Which factor would you need to bound, and what convenient restriction on \(\delta\) would you impose first?

Factor \(x^3-27=(x-3)(x^2+3x+9)\). You'd impose a first restriction like \(\delta\le1\) to trap \(x\) in \((2,4)\), giving a fixed numerical bound on \(x^2+3x+9\) (its max on that interval, at \(x=4\), is \(16+12+9=37\)). Then solve \(|x-3|<\varepsilon/37\) for the second requirement, and take \(\delta=\min(1,\varepsilon/37)\) — exactly the same two-step shape as Section 7, just with a bigger bounding constant.
Where this goes next. With a rigorous definition in hand, the next natural step is continuity — the precise statement that a function has no holes or jumps, phrased as \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=f(c)\). From there, the derivative itself is defined as a very particular limit, and every \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) instinct you've built today comes back to prove the differentiation rules you'll soon take for granted.
