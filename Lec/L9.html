Lecture 9 · Functions, continued

Graphs, images, preimages, and inverses

Lecture 8 gave us the vocabulary — domain, codomain, range, one-one, onto. Today we make one-one and onto visual with two line tests, then build the machinery that will follow you through the rest of the course: the image of a set under \(f\), the preimage of a set under \(f\), and when \(f\) can be undone.

01Seeing a function

The graph of a function, and the vertical line test

Let \(f:X\to Y\) be a function. Its graph is the set of ordered pairs

\(G(f):=\{(x,y)\in X\times Y \mid y=f(x)\}.\)

Immediately, \(G(f)\subseteq X\times Y\). A natural question runs the other way: is every subset of \(X\times Y\) the graph of some function? Try plotting \(G(f)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) for \(f(x)=\tfrac1x\), the constant \(f(x)=y_0\), \(f(x)=x^3\), and \(f(x)=\sin x\) — all four are functions, all four look different, but they share one shape property.

No. Take \(X=Y=\mathbb{R}\) and the subset \(\{(1,2),(1,-2)\}\) — this says \(1\) maps to both \(2\) and \(-2\), violating the uniqueness requirement from Lecture 8. A subset of \(X\times Y\) is a function's graph exactly when every \(x\in X\) appears as the first coordinate of exactly one pair.
The Vertical Line Test. A curve in the coordinate plane is the graph of a function if and only if no vertical line intersects the curve more than once.

This is nothing but a picture of the uniqueness requirement: a vertical line at \(x=x_0\) crosses the curve once for every \(y\) with \((x_0,y)\) on the curve — more than one crossing means \(x_0\) has two outputs.

02Same idea, rotated 90°

The Horizontal Line Test

Once we know \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) is a function, its graph passes the vertical line test automatically. But we can now ask the graph two more questions, by testing with horizontal lines instead:

One-one test. \(f\) is one-one if and only if every horizontal line intersects the graph of \(f\) at most once.
Onto test. \(f\) is onto if and only if every horizontal line intersects the graph of \(f\) at least once.

Put the two together: \(f\) is a bijection exactly when every horizontal line meets the graph exactly once.

03Pushing a whole set through f

The image of a set, f(A)

We already met the range, \(R(f)=f(X)\), as the image of the whole domain. Nothing stops us from asking the same question about any subset \(A\subseteq X\):

\(f(A):=\{y\in Y \mid \exists x\in A,\ f(x)=y\} = \{f(x)\mid x\in A\}.\)

And correspondingly, \(y\notin f(A)\) exactly when \(\forall x\in A,\ f(x)\ne y\).

Worked example

Let \(g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\), \(g(x)=x^2\). Find \(g(A)\) for each: \(A=\{1\}\); \(A=\{-2,2\}\); \(A=(0,2]\); \(A=(-1,0)\); \(A=(-4,1]\); \(A=(-\infty,0)\); \(A=\mathbb{R}\); \(A=\emptyset\).

\(g(\{1\})=\{1\}\).   \(g(\{-2,2\})=\{4\}\) (both hit the same value).   \(g((0,2])=(0,4]\).   \(g((-1,0))=(0,1)\) (open at both ends — \(0\) itself is excluded since \(0\notin(-1,0)\)).   \(g((-4,1])=[0,16)\) (the minimum \(0\) is achieved at \(x=0\), but the supremum \(16\) is approached, not reached, since \(-4\notin A\)).   \(g((-\infty,0))=(0,\infty)\).   \(g(\mathbb{R})=[0,\infty)=R(g)\).   \(g(\emptyset)=\emptyset\).

Quick checks
  1. If \(A\ne\emptyset\), is \(f(A)\) also nonempty? (T/F)
  2. If \(f:X\to Y\) is constant, is \(g:\mathcal{P}(X)\to\mathcal{P}(Y)\), \(g(A)=f(A)\), also constant?
  3. If \(X\) is finite and \(f\) is one-one, is \(|f(A)|=|A|\) for every \(A\subseteq X\)?

1. True. Every \(x\in A\) contributes \(f(x)\) to \(f(A)\), so a nonempty \(A\) forces a nonempty \(f(A)\).

2. Almost — with one exception. If \(f(x)=y_0\) for all \(x\), then \(f(A)=\{y_0\}\) for every nonempty \(A\subseteq X\), so \(g\) is constant on \(\mathcal{P}(X)\setminus\{\emptyset\}\); but \(g(\emptyset)=f(\emptyset)=\emptyset\ne\{y_0\}\), so strictly speaking \(g\) is not constant on all of \(\mathcal{P}(X)\) — the empty set is the one input that breaks the pattern.

3. Yes. One-one means distinct elements of \(A\) get distinct images, so \(f\) restricted to \(A\) is a bijection onto \(f(A)\), giving \(|f(A)|=|A|\).

For \(f(x,y)=xy\) on \(\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}\): what is \(f(A)\) when \(A\) is the \(x\)-axis? (Every point has \(y=0\), so \(f(A)=\{0\}\).) When \(A\) is the open first quadrant? (\(f(A)=(0,\infty)\), since \(x,y>0\) makes \(xy\) range over all positive reals.) When \(A=\{(x,y)\mid x+y=0\}\)? (Here \(y=-x\), so \(f(x,-x)=-x^2\), giving \(f(A)=(-\infty,0]\).)

For \(f(x,y)=x^2+y^2\) and \(A=\{(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta)\mid\theta\in\mathbb{R}\}\) (the circle of radius 2): every point of \(A\) has \(x^2+y^2=4\), so \(f(A)=\{4\}\) — a whole circle collapses to a single point.

And two conceptual ones: if \(f(x)\in f(A)\), must \(x\in A\)? Not in general — \(f(x)\) could equal \(f(a)\) for some other \(a\in A\) without \(x\) itself being in \(A\) (this is exactly what fails when \(f\) isn't one-one). It is true whenever \(f\) is one-one. Can \(f(A)=f(B)\) with \(A\ne B\)? Yes — e.g. \(f(x)=x^2\), \(A=\{1\}\), \(B=\{-1\}\): different sets, same image \(\{1\}\).

04A notation, not an inverse

The preimage of a set, f⁻¹(B)

Let \(f:X\to Y\) and \(B\subseteq Y\). The inverse image (or preimage) of \(B\) under \(f\) is

\(f^{-1}(B):=\{x\in X \mid f(x)\in B\}.\)
A warning worth over-stating. \(f^{-1}(B)\) is notation, not a claim that an inverse function \(f^{-1}\) exists. It makes perfect sense for any function \(f:X\to Y\) and any subset \(B\subseteq Y\), whether or not \(f\) is invertible — it should be read as "the inverse image of \(B\) under \(f\)," never as "the image of \(B\) under \(f^{-1}\)." Two useful facts to anchor this: \(f^{-1}(\emptyset)=\emptyset\) always, and \(x\in f^{-1}(B)\iff f(x)\in B\).

When \(B=\{y\}\) is a single point, we simplify \(f^{-1}(\{y\})\) to \(f^{-1}(y)\).

05A genuine asymmetry worth remembering

Images play badly with ∩; preimages don't

Let \(f:X\to Y\) and \(A,B\subseteq X\). Four claims to test — some are always true, some aren't:

  1. If \(A\subseteq B\), then \(f(A)\subseteq f(B)\).
  2. \(f(A\cup B)=f(A)\cup f(B)\).
  3. \(f(A\cap B)=f(A)\cap f(B)\).
  4. \(f(X\setminus A)=Y\setminus f(A)\).
Claims 1 and 2 are always true. Claim 3 fails in general — only \(\subseteq\) holds, not equality. Claim 4 also fails in general. Both failures are worth proving carefully, since they're the two most commonly mis-remembered facts about images.
Claim 3, resolved: \(f(A\cap B)\subseteq f(A)\cap f(B)\) always, but equality can fail.

The \(\subseteq\) direction is quick: if \(y\in f(A\cap B)\), some \(x\in A\cap B\) has \(f(x)=y\); that same \(x\) is in both \(A\) and \(B\), so \(y\in f(A)\) and \(y\in f(B)\).

The reverse can fail: take \(f(x)=x^2\), \(A=\mathbb{R}^+\), \(B=\mathbb{R}^-\) (or equally, \(A=\mathbb{Q}\), \(B=\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}\)). Then \(A\cap B=\emptyset\), so \(f(A\cap B)=\emptyset\). But \(f(A)=f(B)=(0,\infty)\), so \(f(A)\cap f(B)=(0,\infty)\ne\emptyset\). Two disjoint sets can still have overlapping images.

The fix — and it's the punchline of the whole section:

\(f\) is one-one \(\iff\) \(\forall A,B\subseteq X,\ f(A\cap B)=f(A)\cap f(B)\).

(⟹) Suppose \(f\) is one-one. We already have \(f(A\cap B)\subseteq f(A)\cap f(B)\). For the reverse, take \(y\in f(A)\cap f(B)\): there exist \(a\in A\) and \(b\in B\) with \(f(a)=y=f(b)\). Since \(f\) is one-one, \(f(a)=f(b)\implies a=b\), so this common element lies in \(A\cap B\), giving \(y\in f(A\cap B)\).

(⟸) Suppose the equality holds for all \(A,B\). Take any \(x_1\ne x_2\) and let \(A=\{x_1\}\), \(B=\{x_2\}\). Then \(A\cap B=\emptyset\), so \(f(A\cap B)=\emptyset\), forcing \(f(A)\cap f(B)=\emptyset\), i.e. \(f(x_1)\ne f(x_2)\). That's exactly one-one-ness.

Claim 4, resolved: \(f(X\setminus A)=Y\setminus f(A)\) fails too — but in a very tidy, testable way.

Ask it in two halves. Is \(Y\setminus f(A)\subseteq f(X\setminus A)\)? Take \(y\notin f(A)\): no \(x\in A\) has \(f(x)=y\). Does that force some \(x\in X\setminus A\) with \(f(x)=y\)? Only if \(f\) actually reaches \(y\) at all — i.e. only if \(f\) is onto. And the reverse containment \(f(X\setminus A)\subseteq Y\setminus f(A)\) needs \(f\) to be one-one (otherwise some \(x\notin A\) might share its image with a point inside \(A\)).

\(\forall A\subseteq X,\ Y\setminus f(A)\subseteq f(X\setminus A) \iff f\text{ onto.}\)
\(\forall A\subseteq X,\ f(X\setminus A)\subseteq Y\setminus f(A) \iff f\text{ one-one.}\)
\(\forall A\subseteq X,\ f(X\setminus A)=Y\setminus f(A) \iff f\text{ bijective.}\)

A constant function is the fastest way to see both directions fail — try \(A=\emptyset\) too, as a sanity check on each claim.

Preimages, by contrast, are extremely well-behaved — for \(f:X\to Y\) and \(B_1,B_2\subseteq Y\):

\(B_1\subseteq B_2 \implies f^{-1}(B_1)\subseteq f^{-1}(B_2)\)
\(f^{-1}(B_1\cup B_2)=f^{-1}(B_1)\cup f^{-1}(B_2)\)
\(f^{-1}(B_1\cap B_2)=f^{-1}(B_1)\cap f^{-1}(B_2)\)
\(f^{-1}(B^c)=f^{-1}(B)^c\)

All four hold with no conditions on \(f\) whatsoever — no one-one, no onto required. This asymmetry (preimages always commute with set operations; images only sometimes do) is one of the most useful structural facts in all of analysis and topology, and it's why so many later definitions (continuity included) are phrased in terms of preimages rather than images.

06Running the machine in reverse

Inverse functions

Given \(f:X\to Y\), can we find \(g:Y\to X\) that exactly reverses it — if \(f\) sends \(x\) to \(y\), \(g\) sends \(y\) back to \(x\), and vice versa? If such a \(g\) exists, it's called an inverse of \(f\).

No. Suppose \(f\) has an inverse \(g:Y\to X\). Fix any \(y\in Y\); since \(g\) is a function, \(g(y)\) is some specific \(x\in X\), and reversing again must give \(f(x)=y\) — so every \(y\in Y\) has some preimage under \(f\) (that's onto). And that preimage must be unique: if \(f(x_1)=f(x_2)=y\), then \(g(y)\) can only equal one of \(x_1,x_2\), so \(x_1=x_2\) (that's one-one). In one line:
If \(f\) has an inverse, then \(\forall y\in Y,\ \exists! x\in X\ (f(x)=y)\).
That's exactly the statement that \(f\) is a bijection.
Does having an inverse force f to be onto? Yes — shown above.
Does having an inverse force f to be one-one? Yes — also shown above.
Yes. Onto guarantees every \(y\) has at least one preimage; one-one guarantees it has at most one. Together, every \(y\in Y\) has exactly one preimage, so the rule \(g(y)=\) "the unique \(x\) with \(f(x)=y\)" is itself a well-defined function \(Y\to X\) — and by construction it reverses \(f\) in both directions. Being a bijection is not just necessary for invertibility, it's sufficient.
Yes. Suppose \(g_1,g_2:Y\to X\) both invert \(f\). For any \(y\in Y\), write \(y=f(x)\) for the unique \(x\) (bijectivity); both \(g_1(y)\) and \(g_2(y)\) must equal that same \(x\), since inverting means recovering the unique preimage. So \(g_1=g_2\) pointwise, hence as functions. This is why we can safely write "the" inverse, \(f^{-1}\), once we know \(f\) is a bijection.
Yes. On this restricted domain (unlike \(\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\)), \(f\) is one-one — no two nonnegative numbers have the same square — and onto \([0,\infty)\), since every nonnegative number has a nonnegative square root. So \(f\) is a bijection, and its inverse is \(f^{-1}(x)=\sqrt{x}\), the nonnegative square root. This is precisely why "restrict the domain" is the standard trick for inverting functions like \(x^2\) or \(\sin x\) that fail to be one-one on all of \(\mathbb{R}\).
07Putting the pieces together

f⁻¹(f(A)) and f(f⁻¹(B)): what comes back out

Push a set \(A\subseteq X\) forward with \(f\), then pull it back with \(f^{-1}\) — do you land where you started? Let's build the intuition first, then state the exact theorem.

Yes, always, with no conditions on \(f\). Take \(x\in A\); then \(f(x)\in f(A)\) by definition, and hence \(x\in f^{-1}(f(A))\) by definition of preimage. So \(A\subseteq f^{-1}(f(A))\) is free. The interesting question is whether it can be a proper containment — and it can: if \(f\) is a constant function \(f(x)=y_0\) and \(A\) is a proper nonempty subset of \(X\), then \(f(A)=\{y_0\}\), and \(f^{-1}(f(A))=f^{-1}(\{y_0\})=X\), which is strictly bigger than \(A\).
Theorem. Let \(f:X\to Y\) be a function. Then:
  1. \(f\) is one-one \(\iff\) \(\forall A\subseteq X,\ f^{-1}(f(A))=A\).
  2. \(f\) is onto \(\iff\) \(\forall B\subseteq Y,\ f(f^{-1}(B))=B\).

Part 1 says: one-one is exactly the property that lets you always recover the original set after pushing forward and pulling back. Part 2 says: onto is exactly the property that lets you always recover a target set after pulling back and pushing forward. Notice the constant-function example above is a one-one failure, matching part 1 exactly.

(⟹, one-one \(\implies\) equality). We already have \(A\subseteq f^{-1}(f(A))\) for free. For the reverse, take \(x\in f^{-1}(f(A))\): then \(f(x)\in f(A)\), so \(f(x)=f(a)\) for some \(a\in A\). One-one-ness gives \(x=a\in A\).

(⟸, equality for all A \(\implies\) one-one). Suppose \(f(x_1)=f(x_2)\); we want \(x_1=x_2\). Apply the hypothesis to \(A=\{x_1\}\): \(f^{-1}(f(\{x_1\}))=\{x_1\}\). Since \(f(x_2)=f(x_1)\), we get \(x_2\in f^{-1}(f(\{x_1\}))=\{x_1\}\), so \(x_2=x_1\).

One more relationship to notice

Let \(y_1\ne y_2\) in \(Y\). What is \(f^{-1}(y_1)\cap f^{-1}(y_2)\)?

Always \(\emptyset\). If \(x\) were in both, then \(f(x)=y_1\) and \(f(x)=y_2\) simultaneously — impossible, since \(f(x)\) is a single value. This is why the sets \(\{f^{-1}(y)\}_{y\in Y}\) partition \(X\) into disjoint "fibers," one per point of the codomain — a picture worth keeping in mind for the rest of the course.
08Before next lecture

Self-assessment

Part A — recall

State the vertical line test and the two horizontal line tests in your own words.

Vertical: a curve is a function's graph iff no vertical line hits it more than once (uniqueness of output). Horizontal, one-one: no horizontal line hits the graph more than once (no two inputs share an output). Horizontal, onto: every horizontal line hits the graph at least once (every codomain value is reached).
Part B — apply

Let \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\), \(f(x)=x^2\). Find \(f^{-1}(1)\), \(f^{-1}(0)\), \(f^{-1}(-1)\), \(f^{-1}([0,1])\), and \(f^{-1}([4,16])\).

\(f^{-1}(1)=\{-1,1\}\). \(f^{-1}(0)=\{0\}\). \(f^{-1}(-1)=\emptyset\) (no real number squares to a negative). \(f^{-1}([0,1])=[-1,1]\). \(f^{-1}([4,16])=[-4,-2]\cup[2,4]\).
Part B — apply

For \(X=\{1,2,3\}\), \(Y=\{a,b,c,d\}\), which of these subsets of \(X\times Y\) is the graph of a one-one function? (a) \(\{(1,a),(2,b),(3,c),(2,d)\}\) (b) \(\{(1,b),(2,c),(3,d)\}\) (c) \(\{(1,b),(2,c),(3,b)\}\) (d) \(\{(1,b),(2,d)\}\)

(a) Not even a function: \(2\) appears twice, with two different outputs (\(b\) and \(d\)), violating uniqueness. (b) A function, and one-one — three distinct inputs, three distinct outputs. (c) A function (each input appears once), but not one-one, since \(1\) and \(3\) both map to \(b\). (d) Not a function at all on \(X\): \(3\) never appears as an input, so requirement 1 (every input used) fails.
Part C — think

Give two disjoint sets \(A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}\) and a function \(f\) with \(f(A)\cap f(B)\ne\emptyset\), different from the example in Section 5.

Take \(f(x)=\sin x\), \(A=[0,\pi]\), \(B=[\pi,2\pi]\) — these overlap only at the single point \(\pi\), so make them strictly disjoint: \(A=[0,\pi)\), \(B=(\pi,2\pi]\). Both \(f(A)\) and \(f(B)\) equal \([-1,1]\) (sine sweeps through its whole range on either interval), so \(f(A)\cap f(B)=[-1,1]\ne\emptyset\), even though \(A\cap B=\emptyset\). Any non-injective function easily produces such examples.
Part C — think

Let \(g_1:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}\), \(g_1(x,y)=x\). Describe \(g_1^{-1}([-1,2])\) geometrically. Then do the same for \(g_2(x,y)=x^2+y^2\) and \(g_2^{-1}([0,4])\).

\(g_1^{-1}([-1,2])=\{(x,y)\mid -1\le x\le 2\}\) — a vertical infinite strip between the lines \(x=-1\) and \(x=2\), with \(y\) totally unconstrained. \(g_2^{-1}([0,4])=\{(x,y)\mid x^2+y^2\le4\}\) — the closed disk of radius \(2\) centered at the origin (since \(g_2\) is always \(\ge0\), the lower bound \(0\) contributes nothing new, and the upper bound \(4\) is exactly "distance from origin \(\le2\)").
