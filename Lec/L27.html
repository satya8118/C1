Calculus · Applications of Differentiation II

Optimisation, modelling and L'Hôpital's rule

Calculus becomes powerful when it answers real questions: which rectangle encloses the most area, which box holds the most, which can uses the least metal. We then meet a rule that finishes limits that substitution cannot. Every picture is live, so slide, reshape and compare. Every problem hides its solution behind a "Show solution" button.

01Strategy

How to optimise anything

"Which shape uses the least material?", "Which price earns the most?", "Which route is shortest?" All of these ask for the largest or smallest value of some quantity. Last lecture you learned how to find global extrema. Now we turn real situations into functions you can differentiate.

A recipe for optimisation problems.

  1. Understand and draw. What is to be made largest or smallest? What is fixed?
  2. Name the variables and write the objective (the quantity to optimise) and the constraint (what is fixed).
  3. Use the constraint to write the objective as a function of one variable, say \(f(x)\).
  4. Find the domain that makes physical sense (lengths must be positive, and so on).
  5. Find the global maximum or minimum of \(f\) on that domain, with the closed interval method or the derivative tests.
  6. Answer the question asked, with units, and check that it is reasonable.

A safety check when the domain is not closed. Suppose \(f\) has a continuous derivative on an interval \(I\) and exactly one critical point \(c\). If \(f'\) changes from \(+\) to \(-\) at \(c\), then \(f(c)\) is the global maximum on \(I\). (Reason: with no other critical points, \(f'\) keeps one sign on each side of \(c\), so \(f\) is increasing before \(c\) and decreasing after \(c\).) The same holds for a minimum with the signs reversed.

02Model 1

Rectangles with a given perimeter: the square wins

Problem. Among all rectangles with the same perimeter \(P\), which has the largest area?
Explore · Reshaping a rectangle

Stretch the rectangle with the width slider while the perimeter stays fixed. The lower graph shows how the area \(A(w)\) rises and falls. Change \(P\) and notice that the peak is always at the square.

Solution. Let the width be \(w\) and the height \(h\). The constraint \(2(w+h)=P\) gives \(h=\tfrac P2-w\), so the area is \[ A(w)=w\Bigl(\tfrac P2-w\Bigr)=\tfrac P2\,w-w^2,\qquad 0\lt w\lt\tfrac P2. \] Differentiate: \(A'(w)=\tfrac P2-2w\), which vanishes only at \(w=\tfrac P4\). Since \(A'\gt0\) for \(w\lt\tfrac P4\) and \(A'\lt0\) for \(w\gt\tfrac P4\) (or \(A''=-2\lt0\)), this is the global maximum. Then \(h=\tfrac P2-\tfrac P4=\tfrac P4=w\): the best rectangle is a square, with area \(\tfrac{P^2}{16}\). \(\blacksquare\)

A second proof, no calculus. For positive numbers, \(wh\le\Bigl(\dfrac{w+h}{2}\Bigr)^2\) (this is \((w-h)^2\ge0\) rewritten). Since \(w+h=P/2\) is fixed, \(A\le(P/4)^2\), with equality exactly when \(w=h\). Two different methods, one answer.
03Model 2

The open box of greatest volume

Problem. From a square sheet of side \(L\), cut a square of side \(x\) from each corner and fold up the sides to make an open box. Which \(x\) gives the largest volume?
Explore · Cut and fold

The coral corners are removed. A tiny cut gives a wide, shallow box; a huge cut gives a tall, narrow one. Somewhere in between is the best box.

Solution. After the cut the base is a square of side \(L-2x\) and the height is \(x\), so \[ V(x)=x\,(L-2x)^2,\qquad 0\le x\le\tfrac L2. \] By the product and chain rules, \[ V'(x)=(L-2x)^2-4x(L-2x)=(L-2x)(L-6x). \] The critical points are \(x=\tfrac L2\) and \(x=\tfrac L6\). Use the closed interval method: \(V(0)=0\), \(V(\tfrac L2)=0\), and \(V(\tfrac L6)=\tfrac L6\bigl(\tfrac{2L}{3}\bigr)^2=\tfrac{2L^3}{27}\gt0\). So the best cut is \(x=\tfrac L6\), with volume \(\tfrac{2L^3}{27}\). For \(L=12\): \(x=2\) and \(V=128\). \(\blacksquare\)

04Model 3

A can that holds one litre with the least metal

Problem. A closed cylindrical can must hold \(V\) (here \(1000\ \text{cm}^3\)). What radius \(r\) and height \(h\) use the least metal?
Explore · Fat can or tall can?

The volume stays at one litre while the shape changes: a thin can needs a lot of side metal, a flat can needs a lot of lid. The total surface area \(S(r)\) has a single lowest point.

Solution. Volume: \(\pi r^2h=V\), so \(h=\dfrac{V}{\pi r^2}\). Surface area (side, top and bottom): \[ S(r)=2\pi rh+2\pi r^2=\frac{2V}{r}+2\pi r^2,\qquad r\gt0. \] Then \(S'(r)=-\dfrac{2V}{r^2}+4\pi r=0\) gives \(r^3=\dfrac{V}{2\pi}\). The only critical point is \(r=\sqrt[3]{V/2\pi}\). Since \(S''(r)=\dfrac{4V}{r^3}+4\pi\gt0\), it is a minimum, and \(S\to\infty\) as \(r\to0^+\) and as \(r\to\infty\), so it is the global minimum. The height is \[ h=\frac{V}{\pi r^2}=\frac{2\pi r^3}{\pi r^2}=2r. \] So the best can is exactly as tall as it is wide. For \(V=1000\): \(r\approx5.42\) cm, \(h\approx10.84\) cm. \(\blacksquare\)

Why real cans differ. Real tins and bottles also care about the cost of seams, the strength of the lid, how they fit in a hand or on a shelf. A model captures one goal; engineers weigh several.
05Practice

More modelling problems

Each problem follows the six-step recipe. Try it on paper, then open the solution.

Problem 1 · Fence along a river

A farmer has \(400\) m of fencing and wants a rectangular pen along a straight river. No fence is needed along the river. What dimensions give the largest area?

Let the two sides perpendicular to the river have length \(w\) and the side parallel to it have length \(\ell\). The fence is \(2w+\ell=400\), so \(\ell=400-2w\), and \(A(w)=w(400-2w)\) for \(0\lt w\lt200\). \(A'(w)=400-4w=0\) gives \(w=100\); \(A''=-4\lt0\), and \(A\to0\) at both ends, so this is the global maximum. Then \(\ell=200\) m and \(A=20\,000\ \text{m}^2\).
Problem 2 · Fixed area, least fence

A rectangle has area \(64\ \text{m}^2\). Show that its perimeter is smallest when it is a square, and find that perimeter.

With width \(x\gt0\) and height \(64/x\), the perimeter is \(P(x)=2\bigl(x+\tfrac{64}{x}\bigr)\). \(P'(x)=2\bigl(1-\tfrac{64}{x^2}\bigr)=0\) gives \(x=8\). Since \(P''(x)=\tfrac{256}{x^3}\gt0\), it is a minimum, and \(P\to\infty\) at both ends of \((0,\infty)\), so it is the global minimum. Then the height is \(64/8=8\): a square, with \(P=32\) m. (Together with the lecture's result: for a fixed perimeter the square has the most area, and for a fixed area it has the least perimeter.)
Problem 3 · Closest point

Find the point on the curve \(y=\sqrt x\) closest to the point \((4,0)\).

The squared distance from \((x,\sqrt x)\) to \((4,0)\) is \(D(x)=(x-4)^2+x\) for \(x\ge0\). (Minimising \(D\) is the same as minimising the distance, and avoids a square root.) \(D'(x)=2(x-4)+1=2x-7=0\) gives \(x=\tfrac72\). Since \(D''=2\gt0\) and \(D(0)=16\gt D(\tfrac72)=\tfrac{15}{4}\), this is the global minimum. The closest point is \(\bigl(\tfrac72,\sqrt{7/2}\bigr)\), at distance \(\sqrt{15/4}\approx1.94\).
Problem 4 · A product of two numbers

Two non-negative numbers \(x\) and \(y\) satisfy \(x+y=10\). Find the largest possible value of \(x^2y\).

Substitute \(y=10-x\): \(f(x)=x^2(10-x)=10x^2-x^3\) on \([0,10]\). \(f'(x)=20x-3x^2=x(20-3x)\), critical points \(x=0\) and \(x=\tfrac{20}{3}\). Closed interval method: \(f(0)=0\), \(f(10)=0\), \(f\bigl(\tfrac{20}{3}\bigr)=\tfrac{400}{9}\cdot\tfrac{10}{3}=\tfrac{4000}{27}\approx148.1\). The maximum is \(\tfrac{4000}{27}\), at \(x=\tfrac{20}{3}\), \(y=\tfrac{10}{3}\).
Problem 5 · A rectangle in a semicircle

A rectangle with one side on the diameter is inscribed in a semicircle of radius \(R\). Find the largest possible area.

Put the semicircle on \(y=\sqrt{R^2-x^2}\) and let the rectangle have corners at \((\pm x,0)\) and \((\pm x,\sqrt{R^2-x^2})\), \(0\le x\le R\). Area \(A(x)=2x\sqrt{R^2-x^2}\). Maximising \(A\) is the same as maximising \(A^2=4x^2(R^2-x^2)=4R^2x^2-4x^4\) (both are non-negative). \((A^2)'=8R^2x-16x^3=8x(R^2-2x^2)=0\) gives \(x=0\) or \(x=R/\sqrt2\). The area is \(0\) at \(x=0\) and at \(x=R\), and \(A(R/\sqrt2)=2\cdot\tfrac{R}{\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac{R}{\sqrt2}=R^2\). So the maximum area is \(R^2\), attained when the rectangle is \(\sqrt2R\) wide and \(R/\sqrt2\) tall.
06L'Hôpital's rule

When a limit looks like 0/0

Try to evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}\). Substituting \(x=0\) gives \(\tfrac00\), which means nothing by itself: \(\tfrac{x}{x}\to1\), \(\tfrac{2x}{x}\to2\), \(\tfrac{x^2}{x}\to0\) and \(\tfrac{x}{x^3}\to\infty\) are all of the form \(\tfrac00\) with different answers. Such a form is called indeterminate. The derivative gives a way to settle it.

The idea. Near \(a\), a differentiable function looks like its tangent line. If \(f(a)=g(a)=0\), then \(f(x)\approx f'(a)(x-a)\) and \(g(x)\approx g'(a)(x-a)\), so \[ \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\approx\frac{f'(a)(x-a)}{g'(a)(x-a)}=\frac{f'(a)}{g'(a)}. \] The ratio of the functions is close to the ratio of their slopes.
Explore · Watch the ratios converge

Each coloured curve is a ratio: cyan for \(f/g\), gold for \(f'/g'\), and so on. The white dashed line is the limit. Move the slider toward the limit point and compare the numbers: the derivative ratios settle at the same value as \(f/g\). The last example is a warning, so keep it for after the theorem.

Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem. Let \(f,g\) be continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\). Then there is \(c\in(a,b)\) with \[ \bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)\,g'(c)=\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)\,f'(c). \] Proof. Define \(\varphi(x)=f(x)\bigl(g(b)-g(a)\bigr)-g(x)\bigl(f(b)-f(a)\bigr)\). Then \(\varphi(a)=f(a)g(b)-g(a)f(b)=\varphi(b)\), and \(\varphi\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\). By Rolle's theorem \(\varphi'(c)=0\) for some \(c\in(a,b)\), which is the claim. \(\blacksquare\)

L'Hôpital's Rule. Suppose \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable near \(a\) (except possibly at \(a\)), \(g'(x)\ne0\) near \(a\), and \[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=\lim_{x\to a}g(x)=0\quad\text{or}\quad\lim_{x\to a}|f(x)|=\lim_{x\to a}|g(x)|=\infty. \] If \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}\) exists (finite or \(\pm\infty\)), then \[ \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}. \] The same holds for one-sided limits and for \(a=\pm\infty\).

Proof of the \(\tfrac00\) case, \(x\to a^+\). Define \(f(a)=g(a)=0\); then \(f,g\) are continuous on \([a,x]\) for \(x\) close to \(a\). By Cauchy's theorem on \([a,x]\) there is \(\xi\in(a,x)\) with \(f(x)\,g'(\xi)=g(x)\,f'(\xi)\). (Here \(g(x)\ne0\): otherwise Rolle would give a point where \(g'=0\).) Dividing, \[ \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{f'(\xi)}{g'(\xi)}. \] As \(x\to a^+\), \(\xi\) is squeezed between \(a\) and \(x\), so \(\xi\to a^+\) and the right side tends to \(L=\lim f'/g'\). Hence \(f/g\to L\). The case \(x\to a^-\) is the same, and the \(\tfrac\infty\infty\) case needs a longer argument that we omit. \(\blacksquare\)

07L'Hôpital's rule

Other forms, warnings and practice

Forms that are not yet a quotient. Reduce them to \(\tfrac00\) or \(\tfrac\infty\infty\) first:

FormTrickExample
\(0\cdot\infty\)write \(fg=\dfrac{f}{1/g}\)\(x\ln x\) as \(x\to0^+\)
\(\infty-\infty\)combine into one fraction\(\tfrac1x-\tfrac1{\sin x}\)
\(1^\infty,\ 0^0,\ \infty^0\)take \(\ln\) of \(y=f^g\): \(\ln y=g\ln f\), then \(y=e^{\lim\ln y}\)\(x^x\), \((1+\tfrac1x)^x\)

Three warnings.

  1. Check the form. Use the rule only for \(\tfrac00\) or \(\tfrac\infty\infty\) (Problem 10 shows what happens otherwise).
  2. Differentiate top and bottom separately. This is not the quotient rule for \((f/g)'\).
  3. The limit of \(f'/g'\) must exist. For \(\dfrac{x+\sin x}{x}\) as \(x\to\infty\) we have \(f'/g'=1+\cos x\), which keeps oscillating. The rule is silent, yet the real limit is \(1+\tfrac{\sin x}{x}\to1\). Try this in the explorer above.
Problem 6

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{e^x-1-x}{x^2}\).

At \(x=0\) the form is \(\tfrac00\). By the rule it equals \(\displaystyle\lim\frac{e^x-1}{2x}\), again \(\tfrac00\), which equals \(\displaystyle\lim\frac{e^x}{2}=\tfrac12\). So the limit is \(\tfrac12\).
Problem 7

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^+}x^x\).

This is a \(0^0\) form. Let \(y=x^x\); then \(\ln y=x\ln x=\dfrac{\ln x}{1/x}\), of the form \(\tfrac{-\infty}{\infty}\). By the rule, \(\displaystyle\lim\frac{1/x}{-1/x^2}=\lim(-x)=0\). So \(\ln y\to0\) and \(y\to e^0=1\).
Problem 8

Show that \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\Bigl(1+\frac1x\Bigr)^x=e\).

A \(1^\infty\) form. Let \(y=(1+\tfrac1x)^x\); then \(\ln y=\dfrac{\ln(1+1/x)}{1/x}\), of the form \(\tfrac00\). Differentiating top and bottom: \(\dfrac{\frac{-1/x^2}{1+1/x}}{-1/x^2}=\dfrac{1}{1+1/x}\to1\). So \(\ln y\to1\) and \(y\to e\).
Problem 9

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\Bigl(\frac1x-\frac1{\sin x}\Bigr)\).

An \(\infty-\infty\) form. Combine: \(\dfrac1x-\dfrac1{\sin x}=\dfrac{\sin x-x}{x\sin x}\), which is \(\tfrac00\). Apply the rule: \(\dfrac{\cos x-1}{\sin x+x\cos x}\), still \(\tfrac00\). Apply again: \(\dfrac{-\sin x}{2\cos x-x\sin x}\to\dfrac02=0\). So the limit is \(0\).
Problem 10 · Spot the error

A student computes \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{1+\cos x}{x^2}\) as \(\displaystyle\lim\frac{-\sin x}{2x}=-\frac12\). What went wrong, and what is the true limit?

The numerator tends to \(1+\cos0=2\), not \(0\), so the form is \(\tfrac20\), not indeterminate, and L'Hôpital's rule must not be used. Since the numerator is near \(2\) and \(x^2\to0^+\), the quotient grows without bound: the limit is \(+\infty\). Always check the form first.
Problem 11

Show that \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}x^ne^{-x}=0\) for every positive integer \(n\). What does this say about growth?

Write \(x^ne^{-x}=\dfrac{x^n}{e^x}\), an \(\tfrac\infty\infty\) form. Apply the rule \(n\) times: \(\dfrac{nx^{n-1}}{e^x}\to\dfrac{n(n-1)x^{n-2}}{e^x}\to\cdots\to\dfrac{n!}{e^x}\to0\). So the exponential beats every power of \(x\). Together with \(\ln x/x^p\to0\), the order of growth is \(\ln x\ll x^p\ll e^x\) for large \(x\).
