About the course

For whom

A core course for majors in Mathematics, Physics, Economics.

UWE for other majors.

Importance

  1. This course covers one variable calculus and applications.
  2. It forms the base for subsequent courses in advanced vector calculus and real analysis as well as for applications in probability, differential equations, optimization, etc.
  3. One of the themes of the course is to bring more rigour to the formulas and techniques students may have learned in school.
  4. Some of the approaches we will adopt are unusual. Understand them and you may be able to surprise professional mathematicians/scientists!

Calculus I is needed for:

  1. Maths Majors: Calculus II, Real Analysis I and II, Ordinary Differential Equations, Partial Differential Equations, Probability, Statistics,...
  2. Physics: Calculus II. And the other courses listed above if you pursue a Maths Minor.
  3. Economics: Probability, Statistics. Also for various Economics courses.

The Syllabus

  1. Numbers and Functions: The axioms for N and R, mathematical induction, real functions and their graphs.
  2. Integration: Upper and lower integrals, integrability of monotone functions, basic properties of integration, polynomials, logarithm and exponential functions.
  3. Continuous Functions: Functions, limits, continuity, Intermediate Value Theorem, trigonometric functions. Extreme Value Theorem, integrability of continuous functions.
  4. Differentiation: Tangent line, rates of change, derivative as function, algebra of derivatives, linear approximation, differentiation of inverse functions, derivatives of standard functions (polynomials, rational functions, trigonometric and inverse trigonometric functions).
  5. Applications of Differentiation: Absolute and local extrema, First Derivative Test, Rolle's Theorem, Mean Value Theorem, concavity, Second Derivative Test, curve sketching.
  6. Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: Antiderivatives, Indefinite Integrals, Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, techniques of integration.
    7. Lecture 1 · Introduction to Calculus

    Why calculus? The idea of a limit.

    Before we differentiate a single function, we need to ask a more basic question: why does calculus exist at all? Today we'll see the one idea — the limit — that people invented over and over, in different disguises, because ordinary arithmetic and geometry kept running out of road.

    01The problem with "no change"

    Calculus is the mathematics of change

    Most of the interesting quantities around us are moving targets: a price that drifts with demand, a population that grows and crashes, a planet sweeping out its orbit. A world where nothing changed would need no calculus — and would also be a fairly boring world to live in.
    Calculus is a tool that helps us understand how a change in one quantity is related to a change in another. How does the speed of a falling object change as a function of time? How does the level of water in a barrel change as a function of the amount of liquid poured into it? We see change occurring in nearly everything we observe in the world and universe, and powerful modern instruments help us see more and more.

    Where calculus shows up before you've even named it: if you know an object's position over time, \(x(t)\), differentiation hands you its velocity \(v(t)\) and acceleration \(a(t)\) for free. Run the same idea in reverse — knowing how fast something changes and asking what it adds up to — and you've invented integration.

    Two everyday demands keep forcing this machinery into existence:

    Finding the best choice — optimization. A business wants the price that maximizes profit. A city wants pipe diameters that minimize cost while meeting demand. A chemist wants the reagent ratio that maximizes yield. Whenever a quantity is modeled by a smooth function, its peaks and valleys occur where the rate of change is zero — which is exactly what a derivative measures.
    Finding the total effect of something that keeps changing — accumulation. A dam holds back water whose pressure grows with depth — strongest at the bottom, weakest at the top. To find the total force on the dam wall, you can't just multiply pressure by area, because pressure isn't constant. You have to add up infinitely many thin horizontal strips. That "adding up the infinitely thin" is integration.

    Calculus also drives biology: differential equations model how a population grows or collapses over time, which is how ecologists estimate the maximum number of animals that can be harvested each year without pushing a species toward extinction.

    Notice the pattern in all three stories — pricing, the dam, the population. In each one, ordinary arithmetic (multiply, add, divide) isn't quite enough, because the underlying quantity won't sit still long enough to be measured directly. Calculus is what you build when you refuse to accept "it's too irregular to measure" as an answer. Today's lecture is about the single trick that makes that refusal work: the limit.

    02A 2000-year-old warm-up

    How do you find the area of a circle?

    This sounds like it should have an easy answer — after all, we all know \(A=\pi r^2\). But why is that the formula, and how would you discover it if no one had told you? A circle is the one shape school geometry doesn't give you a direct formula for by cutting it into triangles.

    The Greek geometers' answer, sharpened by Archimedes around 250 BCE, was: approximate the circle by a shape you understand, then make the approximation better and better. Inscribe a regular polygon inside the circle. A triangle is a poor approximation; a hexagon is better; a 96-sided polygon is almost indistinguishable from the circle itself. As the number of sides \(n\) grows, the polygon's area gets arbitrarily close to the circle's area — and every polygon's area you already know how to compute, by cutting it into \(n\) identical triangles from the center.

    Archimedes wasn't the only one to notice this trick. Across different centuries and civilisations, mathematicians found their own way of chopping the circle into pieces and reassembling it — and every single one of them is really the same limiting idea wearing a different costume.

    Approach 2 — Leonardo da Vinci & Sato Moshun: unroll the sectors

    Slice the circle into an even number of equal pie-slices. Stand half of them apex-down and half apex-up, and interleave them in a single row, flat sides touching. The row is jagged — each slice's curved edge still bulges — but it roughly forms a parallelogram of base (half the circumference) \(\pi r\) and height \(r\). Cutting into thinner and thinner slices makes the bulges shallower and shallower, so the jagged strip straightens into an honest rectangle in the limit, with area \(\pi r\times r=\pi r^2\).

    Left: the circle split into \(2n\) alternating sectors. Right: the same pieces, rearranged into a single interlocking row. Cutting and rearranging never changes total area — it is always exactly \(\pi r^2\) — but only as \(n\to\infty\) does the row's shape become a true rectangle.

    Approach 3 — Rabbi Abraham bar Ḥiyya: unroll the rings into a triangle

    Instead of pie-slices, slice the disk into thin concentric rings, like an onion. Each ring, cut open and straightened, becomes a thin rectangular strip whose length is that ring's circumference — long for outer rings, short for inner ones. Stack the strips in order of length and they form a jagged staircase that silhouettes a triangle: base equal to the full circumference \(2\pi r\), height \(r\). As the rings get thinner, the staircase's steps shrink and the outline becomes a true triangle, with area \(\tfrac12\cdot 2\pi r\cdot r=\pi r^2\).

    Unlike the sector demo, here the staircase's own area genuinely changes with \(n\) — it always slightly overshoots, because each step is a rectangle sitting a little outside the true triangle's slanted edge. Watch the overshoot in the first row of the table shrink toward \(\pi r^2\) as \(n\) grows: this one you can watch converge numerically, not just visually.

    Three cultures, three centuries, one idea. Archimedes squeezed the circle between two polygons. Leonardo and Sato Moshun rearranged it into a strip. Rabbi Abraham bar Ḥiyya rearranged it into a triangle. None of them ever finishes the process — you can't actually cut infinitely many rings or draw a polygon with infinitely many sides. What each method gives you instead is a sequence of honest, finite, computable approximations whose limit is forced to be \(\pi r^2\), no matter which route you took to get there. That agreement — different roads, same limit — is exactly why we trust the answer.

    03From rock-counting to rockets

    A brief history of calculus

    Calculus is usually credited to Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, and rightly so — they turned it into a general, reusable method. But as the last section already showed you, the raw ideas behind calculus were centuries in the making. Interestingly, history ran in the opposite order from how we teach it: the very first "calculus-shaped" problems, like the ones you just worked through, were problems of accumulation — area, volume, length — not problems of rates of change. Differentiation came later.

    Timeline (redrawn)
    Archimedes, c. 250 BCE — method of exhaustion. The polygon-squeeze you just ran on the slider has a name: Archimedes used it not just for circles but for parabolic segments, spheres, and cylinders, always approximating a curved figure by "lots of sides" and refining. He was also the all-around engineer of the ancient world — the lever ("give me a place to stand and I will move the world"), the Archimedes screw for raising water, and the buoyancy principle he reportedly discovered while stepping into an overflowing bath.
    Isaac Newton, c. 1665 — fluxions. Working during a plague-year retreat from Cambridge, Newton developed what he called "fluxions" — his version of the derivative, describing how a quantity flows or changes instantaneously. He needed this machinery for physics: his second law, \(F=ma\), only makes sense once acceleration is understood as a rate of change of a rate of change.
    Gottfried Leibniz, c. 1675 — differentials. Working independently on the European continent, Leibniz developed the same ideas with his own notation: \(dx\) and \(dy\) for infinitesimally small changes, and the elongated-S symbol \(\int\) — short for "sum" — for the "total effect of infinitely many infinitesimal pieces" you saw in the rectangle- and ring-based demos. Most of the notation we still use today, including \(dy/dx\) and \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), traces back to Leibniz.
    The priority dispute. Newton and Leibniz developed calculus independently, within about a decade of each other, without knowing about each other's work. When both became aware of the overlap, a bitter dispute broke out over who deserved credit — a controversy that split British and Continental mathematicians for much of the following century and, historians now generally agree, held British mathematics back by clinging to Newton's clumsier notation out of loyalty. Today both are credited as independent co-founders.
    A sneak peek at the notation ahead. You don't need to understand these yet — just notice the shapes, because you'll be writing them fluently within a few weeks.

    Derivative, as a limit: \(\displaystyle \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\)    Power rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^2)=2x\)

    Indefinite integral: \(\displaystyle\int x\,dx=\frac{x^2}{2}+C\)    Definite integral of a constant: \(\displaystyle\int_a^b 1\,dx = b-a\)

    Calculus in real life, today. Rocket trajectories, bridge-load calculations, roller-coaster design, population and epidemic models, image compression, machine learning, and — per the dam example earlier — dams. Once a quantity can be modeled as a function, calculus is almost always somewhere in the pipeline, usually computed by software rather than by hand, but built on exactly the limiting ideas you saw today.

    Historical framing above draws on Barry Leung, "A Brief History Of Calculus," Math Puzzles (Medium, 2026).

    04Adding forever, finitely

    An infinite sum as the limit of partial sums

    Here's a puzzle that troubled the Greeks: if you keep adding smaller and smaller pieces, forever, can the total still be a normal, finite number? Add up

    \[ 1 + \frac12 + \frac14 + \frac18 + \frac{1}{16} + \cdots \]

    and intuition says the sum should either be infinite (you're adding forever!) or undefined (when do you stop?). Calculus's answer: track the partial sums — stop after 1 term, then 2, then 3 — and ask what number those partial sums approach.

    Interactive — filling the unit square
    k = 1
    Terms addedPartial sum \(S_k\)Distance from 2

    Each rectangle is exactly half the leftover space, so the shaded region can never overflow the width-2 rectangle — yet it fills more and more of it. The partial sums \(S_k = 2 - \tfrac{1}{2^{k-1}}\) never equal 2, but their limit is exactly 2.

    Formally, we say the infinite series converges to 2 and write \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^k}=2\). This is not a metaphor or an approximation — it is a precise, provable statement about a limit of a sequence of partial sums. The same reasoning is what let Archimedes pin down \(\pi\), and it's exactly what will let us define the area under a curved graph in a few weeks.

    05Where this course is headed

    Area under a curve as a limit of rectangle sums

    Now combine the last two ideas. Suppose you want the area between the curve \(y=x^2\) and the \(x\)-axis, from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\). There's no triangle-cutting trick this time — the top boundary is curved. So we do exactly what we did for the circle: approximate with shapes we understand (rectangles), then take a limit as we use more, thinner rectangles.

    As \(n\) grows, both sums are squeezed toward the same number, \(\tfrac13\), just like the two polygons were squeezed toward \(\pi r^2\). That common limit is what we will soon call a definite integral. This is not a new idea today — it's the circle-area idea, applied to a different shape.

    Same idea, three costumes. Polygon areas approaching a circle's area. Partial sums approaching 2. Rectangle sums approaching the area under a parabola. All three are the identical mathematical move: build a sequence of easy, computable approximations, and ask what number they approach. Calculus's entire integral half is built by making this move precise and general.

    06Why it's worth the effort

    A quick tour of problems only calculus solves cleanly

    The circle and the parabola are toy problems chosen because we can check the answer. The reason mathematicians kept refining this machinery for centuries is that the same limiting idea unlocks problems with no elementary shortcut at all.

    The dam problem. Water pressure increases with depth, so the force pushing on a dam wall is different at every height. Slice the wall into thin horizontal strips, treat the pressure as roughly constant on each thin strip, add up the force on every strip, then take the limit as the strips get infinitely thin. That limit — an integral — gives the exact total force, something no single multiplication could ever give you.
    Tracking a moving object. If you know a space shuttle's position as a function of time, differentiation gives its instantaneous velocity and acceleration at every single moment — not just its average speed between two checkpoints, but the true rate of change at an instant, which is itself defined as a limit.
    Maximizing profit. A firm's profit as a function of price is a smooth curve with a peak. Calculus doesn't need to test every possible price; it finds the peak directly by locating where the rate of change of profit is zero — the derivative vanishes exactly at the optimum.
    Population dynamics. A population's growth rate depends on its current size, which itself keeps changing — a feedback loop that plain algebra can't untangle. Differential equations model this directly, letting ecologists estimate sustainable harvest limits before a population is pushed toward collapse.

    Notice something all four examples share: in each one, the quantity you actually want (total force, instantaneous velocity, the maximizing price, future population) cannot be pinned down by a single algebraic step. Each one needed the same rescue: replace "compute it directly" with "approximate it, then take a limit of the approximations." That is the intellectual content of this entire course.

    A fifth example, and it's probably why some of you are here

    Picture standing on a foggy hillside at night. You can't see the valley floor, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a step in the downhill direction, feel the new slope, take another step, and repeat — eventually you reach the bottom. That, almost exactly, is how a machine learning model learns.

    A model makes a prediction, compares it to the correct answer, and measures how wrong it was with a single number called the loss. Training the model just means adjusting its internal numbers (its "weights") to make that loss as small as possible — which is exactly the maximizing-profit problem from the grid above, except now we're minimizing error instead of maximizing profit. And the tool for finding that minimum is the one you already know: the derivative tells you the slope, and you move opposite to it. Do that over and over, and it has a name — gradient descent.

    The curve is a toy loss function, \(L(m) = \tfrac{1}{8}(m-10)^2\), with its true minimum at \(m=10\).     Zoom out and the whole pipeline is calculus you'll have met by the end of this course: derivatives to measure slope, critical points (\(dL/dm=0\)) to locate the minimum, and — once a model has more than one weight — partial derivatives stacked into a vector called the gradient, pointing the whole multi-dimensional ball downhill at once. Every model behind the AI tools you've used, however large, is doing this same walk-downhill-on-purpose trick, just with millions of weights instead of one.

    07Before limits, derivatives, integrals — this

    Everything above was really about functions

    Look back at every example today. Position over time, \(x(t)\). Polygon area as a function of the number of sides. Partial sum as a function of how many terms you've added. Pressure as a function of depth. Profit as a function of price. Population as a function of time. In every single case, the object under study was a function — a rule assigning one output to each input — and the question was always: how does the output behave as the input changes, or as the input is pushed to some limiting value?

    This is why, before we can write down a single derivative rule, we have to be completely precise about what a function is: its domain (which inputs are allowed), whether it is defined at every point we care about, whether it varies smoothly or jumps abruptly, and what it means for its outputs to get "arbitrarily close" to some value as the input approaches a point. Every theorem in this course — the derivative rules, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, the tests for maxima and minima — is a statement about functions and their limits. Get comfortable with functions first, and the rest of the course is really just asking the same handful of questions about different ones.

    So that is exactly where the next lecture begins: not with a formula, but with the careful definition of a function, and what it means for a function to approach a limit. Functions whose domain and codomains subsets of real numbers. Hence we study various properties of real numbers before defining the function.

    08Before next lecture

    Self-assessment

    Part A — recall

    In your own words, what does it mean for a sequence of polygon areas to have a "limit" as \(n\to\infty\)?

    It means the areas get arbitrarily close to one fixed number as \(n\) grows without bound, even though no finite \(n\) ever makes the polygon exactly equal to the circle. "Arbitrarily close" will be made precise with \(\varepsilon\)–\(N\) language soon.
    Part A — recall

    Name two of today's four "only calculus solves this cleanly" examples, and identify what varying quantity made each one hard for plain algebra.

    E.g. the dam: pressure varies with depth, so force-per-strip isn't constant. Or profit maximization: profit varies with price along a curve, so no single multiplication locates the peak.
    Part B — apply

    Using the inscribed-polygon area formula \(\tfrac{n}{2}\sin\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{n}\right)\), compute the area for \(n=6\) with \(r=1\), and compare it to \(\pi\). Is a hexagon an over- or under-estimate?

    \(\tfrac{6}{2}\sin(60^\circ) = 3\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\approx 2.598\), versus \(\pi\approx3.1416\). The hexagon underestimates — every inscribed polygon sits inside the circle, so its area is always less than \(\pi r^2\).
    Part B — apply

    Write the partial sum \(S_4 = 1+\tfrac12+\tfrac14+\tfrac18\) as a single fraction, and find \(2-S_4\).

    \(S_4 = \tfrac{15}{8}\), and \(2-S_4 = \tfrac18\). In general \(2-S_k = \tfrac{1}{2^{k-1}}\), which shrinks toward 0 — that shrinking gap is exactly what "converges to 2" means.
    Part C — think

    The left-endpoint sum for \(y=x^2\) on \([0,1]\) always underestimates the true area, and the right-endpoint sum always overestimates it. Why does that happen for this particular function, and would it still be true for a function that isn't increasing?

    \(y=x^2\) is increasing on \([0,1]\), so on every subinterval the left endpoint gives the smallest height and the right endpoint gives the largest — hence under- and over-estimates respectively. For a function that isn't monotonic on the interval, neither sum is guaranteed to consistently over- or under-estimate; some subintervals could have the left height above the curve's average and others below.
    Part C — think

    All three demos today (circle, series, area under \(x^2\)) squeeze a limit between two approximations that close in from opposite sides. Can you think of a quantity from your own field of interest where a similar "approximate from both sides" strategy might apply?

    There's no single correct answer here — the point is practicing the pattern-recognition. Good candidates usually involve a quantity that varies continuously (cost, distance, probability, concentration) that you currently only know how to compute for constant or piecewise-constant cases.
