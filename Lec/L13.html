Real Analysis · Riemann Integration, Part I of III

Partitions and the first Riemann sums

Every Riemann sum problem is the same three moves: cut the interval into pieces, find the smallest and largest value of \(f\) on each piece, and add up the resulting rectangle areas. Today we make "cut into pieces" precise, then run that machinery on the two simplest possible functions — a constant, and \(f(x)=x\) — on the interval \([0,2]\).

01What the integral will mean

Signed area, and why integration is gentler than differentiation

Setup Let \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) be a bounded function on a closed, bounded interval — not necessarily continuous. Over the next three lectures we'll pin down exactly what it means for \(f\) to be Riemann integrable on \([a,b]\), and, when it is, define the number \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), its Riemann integral (also called the definite integral). Let the right endpoint vary and you get a function of \(x\) — the indefinite integral.
Definition — Signed Area \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) represents the signed area under \(y=f(x)\) for \(a\le x\le b\). Unlike ordinary geometric area, signed area assigns a direction: regions above the \(x\)-axis contribute positively, regions below contribute negatively. Writing \(\mathcal A_+\) for the area above and \(\mathcal A_-\) for the area below, \[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \mathcal A_+ - \mathcal A_-. \] The total can be positive, negative, or exactly zero if the two regions cancel.
f(x) = x² − 1 on [−1, 2]

Below the axis (\(-1\le x\le1\)) shaded lime, contributing negatively; above the axis (\(1\le x\le2\)) shaded pink, contributing positively. \(\int_{-1}^2(x^2-1)\,dx = \mathcal A_+-\mathcal A_-\).

Remark — Integration smooths, differentiation roughens The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus — integration and differentiation are inverse operations — is the deepest fact ahead of us, and it lets us compute many integrals explicitly. A striking consequence: integrability is a weaker requirement than differentiability. The indefinite integral of every continuous function exists and is differentiable, while the derivative of a continuous function need not exist at all. That's why we bother studying Riemann sums for kinked, jumpy, or wildly oscillating functions — integration forgives localized rough spots that differentiation cannot.
02Cutting the interval into pieces

Partitions of [a, b]

Definition — Partition A partition \(P\) of \([a,b]\) is a finite set of points \(P=\{x_0,x_1,\dots,x_n\}\) such that \[ a = x_0 < x_1 < x_2 < \cdots < x_{n-1} < x_n = b. \] The subintervals of \(P\) are \([x_{i-1},x_i]\) for \(i=1,\dots,n\), with corresponding subinterval widths defined by: \[ \Delta x_i = x_i - x_{i-1}. \]
Example On \([0,2]\), \(P_1=\{0,\,0.5,\,1.2,\,2\}\) is a valid partition, cutting the interval into \([0,0.5]\), \([0.5,1.2]\), \([1.2,2]\) with widths \(0.5,\,0.7,\,0.8\) — not equal.
Definition — Regular Partition A partition \(P_n\) is regular (or uniform) if every subinterval has equal width: \[ \Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}, \qquad x_i = a + i\Delta x \quad \text{for } i=0,\dots,n. \]
Check yourself

Is \(P_4=\{0,\,0.5,\,1.0,\,1.5,\,2.0\}\) on \([0,2]\) regular? Is \(P_1=\{0,\,0.5,\,1.2,\,2\}\) from above?

\(P_4\) is regular — every subinterval has width \(0.5=\frac{2-0}{4}\). \(P_1\) is not regular — its widths \(0.5,0.7,0.8\) aren't all equal.
Definition — Refinement A partition \(Q\) is a refinement of \(P\) (or finer than \(P\)) if every point of \(P\) is also a point of \(Q\), i.e., \(P\subseteq Q\).
Theorem — Partitions form a poset Let \(\mathcal P[a,b]\) be the set of all partitions of \([a,b]\), with \(P\preceq Q \iff Q\) refines \(P\). Then \((\mathcal P[a,b],\preceq)\) is a partially ordered set: reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive. It's also directed — for any \(P,Q\), their union \(P\cup Q\) is a common refinement of both.
03A common intuition that's false

Regular partitions don't always refine

Caution A tempting claim: "for every partition \(P\), there is a regular partition \(Q\) that refines it." This is false.
Try to see why, before reading on

Consider \(P=\{0,\sqrt2,2\}\) on \([0,2]\). Can any regular partition \(Q\) of \([0,2]\) satisfy \(P\subseteq Q\)?

A regular partition of \([0,2]\) into \(n\) parts has nodes \(x_i=\frac{2i}{n}\), a ratio of two integers — so every node is rational: \(P_n\subset\mathbb Q\). Since \(\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q\), no regular partition can ever contain the point \(\sqrt2\). The regular partition is permanently trapped inside \(\mathbb Q\) and can never hit \(P=\{0,\sqrt2,2\}\)'s irrational node.
A companion puzzle

What happens on \([0,\sqrt2]\) instead — do regular partitions of this interval land in \(\mathbb{Q}\) or not?

The opposite happens. A regular partition into \(n\) parts has nodes \(x_i=\frac{i\sqrt2}{n}\). For \(i>0\), this is a nonzero rational multiple of an irrational number, which is always irrational. So every node with \(i>0\) satisfies \(x_i\notin\mathbb Q\) — the rationality of regular-partition nodes depends entirely on the endpoints of the interval you start with.
The rescue — mesh, not node-inclusion If strict refinement can fail, how do we know regular partitions are still good enough for Riemann sums? Abandon "does it contain the same nodes" and focus on mesh size instead — the width of the widest subinterval. Strict refinement is only ever a tool for shrinking the gap \(U(f,P)-L(f,P)\); it turns out making the mesh small enough, on its own, is entirely sufficient — a fact we'll prove properly in Lecture II.
Definition — Mesh (Norm) The mesh (or norm) of a partition \(P\), written \(\|P\|\), is the width of its widest subinterval: \[ \|P\| = \max_{1\le i\le n} \Delta x_i. \] For a regular partition \(P_n\), \(\|P_n\|=\frac{b-a}{n}\).
04The central definition of the whole subject

Lower and upper sums, warmed up on a constant

Definition — Lower and Upper Riemann Sums Let \(f\) be bounded on \([a,b]\) and \(P=\{x_0,x_1,\dots,x_n\}\) a partition. On each subinterval \([x_{i-1},x_i]\), define: \[ m_i = \inf_{x\in[x_{i-1},x_i]} f(x), \qquad M_i = \sup_{x\in[x_{i-1},x_i]} f(x). \] Then the Lower Riemann Sum \(L(f,P)\) and Upper Riemann Sum \(U(f,P)\) are given by: \[ L(f,P) = \sum_{i=1}^n m_i\,\Delta x_i, \qquad U(f,P) = \sum_{i=1}^n M_i\,\Delta x_i. \]

Warm up on the simplest function there is: \(f(x)=c>0\) on \([0,2]\), with \(P\) an arbitrary partition (subintervals need not have equal width).

Try it yourself first

What are \(m_i\) and \(M_i\) on every subinterval, and what do \(L(f,P)\) and \(U(f,P)\) come out to?

Since \(f(x)=c\) everywhere, \(m_i=M_i=c\) for every \(i\). Then \[ L(f,P)=\sum_{i=1}^n c\,\Delta x_i = c\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-x_{i-1}) = c(x_n-x_0)=c(2-0)=2c \] using the telescoping sum \(\sum(x_i-x_{i-1})=x_n-x_0=b-a\). The identical computation gives \(U(f,P)=2c\).
Result — Constant Functions For \(f(x)=c\) on \([0,2]\) and any partition \(P\): \(L(f,P)=U(f,P)=2c\). The gap is already zero — no refinement is ever needed.
y = c over five unequal subintervals

Five differently-colored, unequal-width rectangles, all sharing the same height \(c\). No matter how you slice \([0,2]\), the total area is always \(c\cdot(b-a)=2c\).

Remark The telescoping identity \(\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-x_{i-1})=x_n-x_0=b-a\) is one of the most important algebraic tricks in this whole subject — it guarantees that for a constant function, the partition structure is irrelevant; only the total interval length matters.
05Scaffolding toward limits

f(x) = x on [0, 2]: our first real limit computation

\(f(x)=x\) is our first genuinely non-constant graph — and it's strictly increasing, so the minimum on each subinterval sits at the left endpoint and the maximum at the right. The true area is a triangle, giving us a known target to check our limit against.

The geometric target: A = ½ · base · height

Right triangle, vertices \((0,0)\), \((2,0)\), \((2,2)\): \(A=\frac12\cdot2\cdot2=2\).

Regular partition \(P_n\) of \([0,2]\): \(\Delta x=\frac{2}{n}\), \(x_i=\frac{2i}{n}\). Strictly increasing \(\implies m_i=f(x_{i-1})=\frac{2(i-1)}{n}\), \(M_i=f(x_i)=\frac{2i}{n}\).

Try the summation yourself

Using \(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^n i=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\), compute \(L(f,P_n)\) and \(U(f,P_n)\).

\[ L(f,P_n)=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{2(i-1)}{n}\cdot\frac2n = \frac{4}{n^2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}k = \frac{4}{n^2}\cdot\frac{(n-1)n}{2} = 2-\frac2n \] \[ U(f,P_n)=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{2i}{n}\cdot\frac2n = \frac{4}{n^2}\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2} = 2+\frac2n \] Gap: \(U(f,P_n)-L(f,P_n)=\frac4n\to0\) as \(n\to\infty\).
Result \[ \lim_{n\to\infty}L(f,P_n)=2, \qquad \lim_{n\to\infty}U(f,P_n)=2 \quad\implies\quad \int_0^2 x\,dx = 2. \]

06A sanity bound that holds for every function

Sums are trapped between m(b−a) and M(b−a)

Proposition Let \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R\) be bounded, with \(m\le f(x)\le M\) for all \(x\in[a,b]\). Then for every partition \(P\): \[ m(b-a) \le L(f,P) \le U(f,P) \le M(b-a). \]
Try to prove it yourself

Hint: start from \(m\le m_i\le M_i\le M\) on every subinterval, and use the telescoping sum \(\sum\Delta x_i=b-a\).

For every \(i\), by definition of infimum/supremum on a subinterval, \(m\le m_i\le M_i\le M\). Using \(\sum_{i=1}^n\Delta x_i=b-a\): \[ m(b-a)=m\sum_{i=1}^n\Delta x_i=\sum_{i=1}^n m\,\Delta x_i \le \sum_{i=1}^n m_i\,\Delta x_i=L(f,P) \] \[ \le \sum_{i=1}^n M_i\,\Delta x_i = U(f,P) \le \sum_{i=1}^n M\,\Delta x_i = M(b-a). \]

This is the fact that will make lower and upper integrals (the sup and inf over all partitions) well-defined in Lecture II — the sets of all lower/upper sums are bounded, so their supremum/infimum exist.

07The engine behind every integrability proof

Refining never hurts: L goes up, U goes down

Lemma — Effect of one new point Let \(Q\) be obtained from \(P\) by inserting a single new point \(c\) into a subinterval \([x_{k-1},x_k]\). Then splitting cannot decrease the infimum and cannot increase the supremum on that subinterval — consequently \(L(f,P)\le L(f,Q)\) and \(U(f,Q)\le U(f,P)\).
Try to prove it yourself

Hint: \([x_{k-1},c]\) and \([c,x_k]\) are both subsets of \([x_{k-1},x_k]\) — what does that say about lower bounds and upper bounds on the smaller sets?

Infimum: let \(m_k=\inf_{x\in[x_{k-1},x_k]}f(x)\). Since \([x_{k-1},c]\subset[x_{k-1},x_k]\), any lower bound for the bigger set is automatically a lower bound for the smaller one — so \(m_k\) lower-bounds \(f\) on \([x_{k-1},c]\) too, forcing \(m_k'=\inf_{x\in[x_{k-1},c]}f(x)\ge m_k\). Same argument gives \(m_k''=\inf_{x\in[c,x_k]}f(x)\ge m_k\). The infimum is non-decreasing under splitting.

Supremum: symmetric argument gives \(M_k'\le M_k\) and \(M_k''\le M_k\) — the supremum is non-increasing.

Consequence: \(m_k'(c-x_{k-1})+m_k''(x_k-c)\ge m_k(c-x_{k-1})+m_k(x_k-c)=m_k\Delta x_k\), so \(L(f,P)\le L(f,Q)\). Symmetrically, \(U(f,Q)\le U(f,P)\). Any refinement is a finite chain of single-point insertions, so the result holds for all refinements.

Corollary — Every lower sum ≤ every upper sum For any two partitions \(P_1,P_2\) of \([a,b]\): \(L(f,P_1)\le U(f,P_2)\).
Try to prove it yourself

Hint: consider the common refinement \(P^*=P_1\cup P_2\).

\(P^*=P_1\cup P_2\) refines both \(P_1\) and \(P_2\). By the lemma, refining increases lower sums and decreases upper sums: \(L(f,P_1)\le L(f,P^*)\) and \(U(f,P^*)\le U(f,P_2)\). On the single partition \(P^*\), trivially \(L(f,P^*)\le U(f,P^*)\) since \(m_i\le M_i\) everywhere. Chaining: \(L(f,P_1)\le L(f,P^*)\le U(f,P^*)\le U(f,P_2)\). Since \(P_1,P_2\) were arbitrary, every lower sum is \(\le\) every upper sum.
Why this matters This corollary is what will let us define the lower integral as \(\sup_P L(f,P)\) and the upper integral as \(\inf_P U(f,P)\) in Lecture II, and guarantee lower integral \(\le\) upper integral automatically, for every bounded function — integrability will turn out to mean these two numbers coincide.

Self-assessment

Question 1

Write down the mesh \(\|P_n\|\) of a regular partition of \([1,7]\) into \(n=12\) parts.

\(\|P_{12}\|=\frac{7-1}{12}=\frac12\).
Question 2

For \(f(x)=5\) (a constant) on \([1,7]\), what is \(L(f,P)\) for any partition \(P\)?

\(L(f,P)=5\cdot(7-1)=30\), regardless of how \(P\) is chosen.
Question 3

Redo the \(f(x)=x\) computation, but on \([0,4]\) instead of \([0,2]\). What should \(\int_0^4 x\,dx\) be, and can you guess the general pattern for \(\int_0^b x\,dx\)?

The triangle has base \(4\), height \(4\), so area \(\frac12\cdot4\cdot4=8\). In general, \(\int_0^b x\,dx=\frac{b^2}{2}\) — you'll see the fully general \([a,b]\) version, \(\frac{b^2-a^2}{2}\), proved via a "completing the square" trick in Lecture III.
Question 4 — think

Suppose \(P\subset[0,2]\) contains the point \(\pi/2\). Explain, using this lecture's subtlety, why no regular partition of \([0,2]\) can refine \(P\).

Every node of a regular partition of \([0,2]\) has the form \(\frac{2i}{n}\), a ratio of integers, hence rational. Since \(\pi/2\) is irrational, it can never appear as a node of any regular partition — so a regular partition can never contain \(P\)'s point \(\pi/2\), exactly as with \(\sqrt2\) earlier.
