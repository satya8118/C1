Lecture 17: Algebra of Continuous Functions & Applications

From $f(x)=x^2$ to Polynomials and Composite Expressions

Step 1 — Continuity Proof of $f(x) = x^2$ and $f(x) = x^n$ by Definition

We begin by directly proving the continuity of $f(x) = x^2$ at an arbitrary point $a \in \mathbb{R}$ using the $\epsilon$-$\delta$ limit framework, before extending it to general monomials $x^n$.

Example 1 — Continuity of $f(x) = x^2$
Prove directly from the $\epsilon$-$\delta$ definition that $f(x) = x^2$ is continuous at any point $a \in \mathbb{R}$, showing $\lim_{x \to a} x^2 = a^2$.
Proof:
Let $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\epsilon > 0$. We want to find $\delta > 0$ such that if $|x - a| < \delta$, then $|x^2 - a^2| < \epsilon$.
Factoring the expression: $$|x^2 - a^2| = |x - a||x + a|$$ To control the factor $|x + a|$, we assume a preliminary restriction $\delta \le 1$. Then: $$|x - a| < 1 \implies |x| - |a| \le |x - a| < 1 \implies |x| < |a| + 1$$ By the triangle inequality: $$|x + a| \le |x| + |a| < (|a| + 1) + |a| = 2|a| + 1$$ Therefore, under the restriction $|x - a| < 1$, we have: $$|x^2 - a^2| = |x - a||x + a| < |x - a|(2|a| + 1)$$ To ensure this product is less than $\epsilon$, we choose: $$\delta = \min\left(1, \frac{\epsilon}{2|a| + 1}\right)$$ Whenever $|x - a| < \delta$, both restrictions hold, yielding $|x^2 - a^2| < \epsilon$. Hence, $f(x) = x^2$ is continuous at $a$. $\blacksquare$
Generalization — Continuity of Monomials $f(x) = x^n$
Let $n \in \mathbb{N}$. The monomial function $f(x) = x^n$ is continuous at any $a \in \mathbb{R}$.
Proof:
Using the algebraic identity $x^n - a^n = (x - a)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} x^k a^{n-1-k}$: $$|x^n - a^n| = |x - a| \cdot \left| \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} x^k a^{n-1-k} \right| \le |x - a| \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} |x|^k |a|^{n-1-k}$$ Assuming $\delta \le 1$, we have $|x| < |a| + 1$. Thus, each term is bounded by $(|a| + 1)^{n-1}$. Letting $M = n(|a| + 1)^{n-1} > 0$, we get $|x^n - a^n| < M|x - a|$. Choosing $\delta = \min\left(1, \frac{\epsilon}{M}\right)$ proves that $f(x) = x^n$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. $\blacksquare$

Step 2 — Algebra of Continuous Functions

Theorem 3.1.5 — Algebra of Continuous Functions
Let $f,g:J\to\mathbb{R}$ be continuous at $a\in J,$ and let $\alpha\in\mathbb{R}.$ Then:
  1. $f+g$ is continuous at $a.$
  2. $\alpha f$ is continuous at $a.$
  3. The set of functions $J\to\mathbb{R}$ continuous at $a$ is a real vector space.
  4. $fg$ is continuous at $a.$
  5. If $f(a)\ne 0,$ there exists $\delta>0$ such that $f(x)\ne 0$ for all $x\in(a-\delta,a+\delta)\cap J,$ and $1/f$ is continuous at $a.$
  6. $|f|$ is continuous at $a.$
  7. $\max\{f,g\}$ and $\min\{f,g\}$ are continuous at $a.$
  8. If $f_1:J_1\to\mathbb{R}$ is continuous at $a_1\in J_1,$ $f_2:J_2\to\mathbb{R}$ is continuous at $a_2:=f_1(a_1),$ and $f_1(J_1)\subseteq J_2,$ then $f_2\circ f_1$ is continuous at $a_1.$

Step 3 — Elaborative Proofs for Theorem 3.1.5

Proof of (1) $f+g$: Let $\epsilon > 0$. By continuity of $f$ and $g$ at $a$, there exist $\delta_1, \delta_2 > 0$ such that: $$|x-a| < \delta_1 \implies |f(x)-f(a)| < \frac{\epsilon}{2}, \quad |x-a| < \delta_2 \implies |g(x)-g(a)| < \frac{\epsilon}{2}$$ Set $\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2)$. If $|x-a| < \delta$: $$|(f+g)(x) - (f+g)(a)| \le |f(x)-f(a)| + |g(x)-g(a)| < \frac{\epsilon}{2} + \frac{\epsilon}{2} = \epsilon \quad \blacksquare$$
Proof of (2) & (3) $\alpha f$ & Vector Space: If $\alpha = 0$, $\alpha f = 0$, which is trivially continuous. If $\alpha \neq 0$, choose $\delta > 0$ such that $|x-a| < \delta \implies |f(x)-f(a)| < \frac{\epsilon}{|\alpha|}$. Then $|\alpha f(x) - \alpha f(a)| = |\alpha||f(x)-f(a)| < \epsilon$. Because continuous functions are closed under addition and scalar multiplication, they form a real vector space. $\blacksquare$
Proof of (4) $fg$: We express $|f(x)g(x) - f(a)g(a)| \le |g(x)||f(x)-f(a)| + |f(a)||g(x)-g(a)|$. Since $g$ is continuous at $a$, choosing $\epsilon = 1$ yields a $\delta_1 > 0$ such that $|x-a| < \delta_1 \implies |g(x)| < |g(a)| + 1$. Next, pick $\delta_2$ so $|f(x)-f(a)| < \frac{\epsilon}{2(|g(a)|+1)}$ and $\delta_3$ so $|g(x)-g(a)| < \frac{\epsilon}{2(|f(a)|+1)}$. Setting $\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2, \delta_3)$ proves $|(fg)(x) - (fg)(a)| < \epsilon$. $\blacksquare$
Proof of (5) $1/f$: Let $\epsilon_0 = \frac{|f(a)|}{2} > 0$. There exists $\delta_1 > 0$ such that $|x-a| < \delta_1 \implies |f(x) - f(a)| < \frac{|f(a)|}{2}$. By reverse triangle inequality, $|f(x)| > \frac{|f(a)|}{2} > 0$. Now, for any $\epsilon > 0$, choose $\delta_2 > 0$ such that $|f(x)-f(a)| < \frac{|f(a)|^2 \epsilon}{2}$. Choosing $\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2)$ gives: $$\left|\frac{1}{f(x)} - \frac{1}{f(a)}\right| = \frac{|f(x)-f(a)|}{|f(x)||f(a)|} < \frac{|f(x)-f(a)|}{\frac{|f(a)|^2}{2}} < \epsilon \quad \blacksquare$$
Proof of (6) $|f|$: By the reverse triangle inequality, $||f(x)| - |f(a)|| \le |f(x) - f(a)|$. Given $\epsilon > 0$, choose $\delta > 0$ from the continuity of $f$. Then $||f(x)| - |f(a)|| < \epsilon$. $\blacksquare$
Proof of (7) $\max\{f,g\}$ and $\min\{f,g\}$: Use the algebraic identities: $$\max\{f,g\} = \frac{f + g + |f - g|}{2}, \quad \min\{f,g\} = \frac{f + g - |f - g|}{2}$$ Since sums, differences, absolute values, and scalar multiplications of continuous functions are continuous (by parts 1, 2, and 6), both max and min are continuous. $\blacksquare$
Proof of (8) $f_2 \circ f_1$: Let $\epsilon > 0$. By continuity of $f_2$ at $a_2 = f_1(a_1)$, $\exists \eta > 0$ such that $|y - a_2| < \eta \implies |f_2(y) - f_2(a_2)| < \epsilon$. By continuity of $f_1$ at $a_1$, using $\eta$ as tolerance, $\exists \delta > 0$ such that $|x - a_1| < \delta \implies |f_1(x) - f_1(a_1)| < \eta$. Thus, $|x - a_1| < \delta \implies |(f_2 \circ f_1)(x) - (f_2 \circ f_1)(a_1)| < \epsilon$. $\blacksquare$

Step 4 — Applications of Algebra of Continuous Functions

We now solve problems establishing the continuity of complex functions purely using the algebra of limits/continuous functions without resorting to manual $\epsilon$-$\delta$ arguments.

Problem 1: Prove that $f(x) = \frac{1}{1 + x^2}$ is continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$ using the algebra of continuous functions.
Solution:
  1. Let $g(x) = x^2$. As proved in Step 1, $g(x)$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.
  2. Let $h(x) = 1$ (the constant function), which is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.
  3. By Theorem 3.1.5(1) [Sum Rule], $k(x) = h(x) + g(x) = 1 + x^2$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.
  4. Since $1 + x^2 \ge 1 > 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, $k(x) \neq 0$ everywhere.
  5. By Theorem 3.1.5(5) [Reciprocal Rule], $f(x) = \frac{1}{k(x)} = \frac{1}{1 + x^2}$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. $\blacksquare$
Problem 2: Prove that every rational function $R(x) = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ is continuous at every point $a$ where $Q(a) \neq 0$.
Solution:
  1. Any polynomial $P(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n a_k x^k$ and $Q(x) = \sum_{j=0}^m b_j x^j$ are continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ by the sum, constant scalar, and product rules of continuous functions (Theorem 3.1.5(1,2,4)).
  2. At any point $a$ where $Q(a) \neq 0$, the reciprocal function $\frac{1}{Q(x)}$ is continuous at $a$ by Theorem 3.1.5(5).
  3. By Theorem 3.1.5(4) [Product Rule], $R(x) = P(x) \cdot \frac{1}{Q(x)} = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ is continuous at $a$. $\blacksquare$
Problem 3: Prove that $f(x) = \frac{x^3 + 2x + 1}{x^2 - 4}$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{-2, 2\}$.
Solution:
  1. The numerator $P(x) = x^3 + 2x + 1$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ as a polynomial.
  2. The denominator $Q(x) = x^2 - 4$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ as a polynomial.
  3. $Q(x) = 0 \iff x^2 - 4 = 0 \iff x = \pm 2$.
  4. For any $a \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{-2, 2\}$, $Q(a) \neq 0$. Thus, by Quotient/Algebra rules, $f(x) = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{-2, 2\}$. $\blacksquare$
Problem 4: Prove that $f(x) = \sqrt{x^2 + 5}$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.
Solution:
  1. Let $g(x) = x^2 + 5$. As a polynomial, $g(x)$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. Furthermore, $g(x) \ge 5 > 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
  2. Let $h(y) = \sqrt{y}$ for $y \in (0, \infty)$, which is known to be continuous on its positive domain.
  3. Since $g(\mathbb{R}) \subseteq [5, \infty) \subset (0, \infty)$, we apply Theorem 3.1.5(8) [Composition Rule].
  4. Thus, $f(x) = (h \circ g)(x) = \sqrt{x^2 + 5}$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. $\blacksquare$
Problem 5: Prove that $f(x) = |\sin x + x^3|$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.
Solution:
  1. $g(x) = \sin x$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ (proved in Lecture 16).
  2. $h(x) = x^3$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ as a monomial (proved in Step 1).
  3. By Theorem 3.1.5(1) [Sum Rule], $k(x) = \sin x + x^3$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.
  4. By Theorem 3.1.5(6) [Absolute Value Rule], $f(x) = |k(x)| = |\sin x + x^3|$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. $\blacksquare$
Problem 6: Prove that $f(x) = \max\{x, x^2\}$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$.
Solution:
  1. Let $g(x) = x$ (identity function, continuous on $\mathbb{R}$).
  2. Let $h(x) = x^2$ (square function, continuous on $\mathbb{R}$).
  3. By Theorem 3.1.5(7) [Max Rule], $f(x) = \max\{g(x), h(x)\} = \max\{x, x^2\}$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. $\blacksquare$
