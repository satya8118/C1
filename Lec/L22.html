Lecture 22 — Differentiability

The derivative as the best affine approximation  ·  Calculus / Real Analysis

PART 1 · THE IDEA

Approximating a function by a straight line

Differential calculus rests on one idea: near a point, replace a complicated function by an affine one. Let $J$ be an interval, $c\in J$ and $f:J\to\mathbb R$. Among all lines $a+b(x-c)$, which one hugs the graph of $f$ most closely near $c$?

What should “closely” mean?
Write the error of an approximation as $E(x)=f(x)-a-b(x-c).$ Asking only $E(x)\to0$ is too weak: any line through $(c,f(c))$ achieves that when $f$ is continuous. We demand more — the error must vanish faster than the distance to $c$: $$\lim_{x\to c}\frac{E(x)}{x-c}=0 .$$

Two things follow at once. First, $E(x)=(x-c)\cdot\frac{E(x)}{x-c}\to 0\cdot 0=0$, so $f(x)\to a$: the constant must be $a=f(c)$, and $f$ has to be continuous at $c$. Second, the condition then reads $$\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}-b\;\to\;0,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad b=\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}.$$ So the best slope $b$, if it exists, is a limit of slopes of chords.

Both dashed lines pass through $(c,f(c))$. The tangent has a tiny error (green bar); a line of any other slope has an error (amber bar) that stays proportional to $|h|$. Slide $h\to0$: only the tangent's error shrinks relative to $|h|$.
Conclusion of the heuristic
The line $y=f(c)+\alpha(x-c)$ is the best affine approximation at $c$ exactly when $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}=\alpha$. This $\alpha$ is what we will call the derivative.
PART 2 · DEFINITION

Differentiability at a point

Definition
Let $f:J\to\mathbb R$ and $c\in J$. We say $f$ is differentiable at $c$ if there is a real number $\alpha$ with $$\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}=\alpha .$$ Such an $\alpha$ is unique; we write $f'(c)=\alpha$ and call it the derivative of $f$ at $c$. The function $f$ is differentiable on $J$ if it is differentiable at every point of $J$.
The definition in quantifiers
$f$ is differentiable at $c$ if and only if $$\begin{aligned}&\exists\,\alpha\in\mathbb R\;\;\forall\,\varepsilon>0\;\;\exists\,\delta>0\;\;\forall\,x\in J:\\&\qquad 0<|x-c|<\delta\;\Longrightarrow\;\Bigl|\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}-\alpha\Bigr|<\varepsilon .\end{aligned}$$ Reading it, quantifier by quantifier:
  • $\exists\,\alpha$: one number is chosen first, and it may not depend on $\varepsilon$ or $x$. This is $f'(c)$.
  • $\forall\,\varepsilon>0$: an arbitrary tolerance is then set by an opponent.
  • $\exists\,\delta>0$: we answer with a neighbourhood size, which may depend on $\varepsilon$ (and on $c$).
  • $\forall\,x$ with $0<|x-c|<\delta$: every such $x$ must pass, and $x=c$ is excluded since the quotient is undefined there.
Negation ($f$ is not differentiable at $c$): $$\begin{aligned}&\forall\,\alpha\in\mathbb R\;\;\exists\,\varepsilon>0\;\;\forall\,\delta>0\;\;\exists\,x\in J:\\&\qquad 0<|x-c|<\delta\;\text{ and }\;\Bigl|\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}-\alpha\Bigr|\ge\varepsilon .\end{aligned}$$ For $|x|$ at $0$: given any $\alpha$, take $\varepsilon=1/2$; in every $\delta$-neighbourhood the quotient equals $\pm1$, and it cannot be within $1/2$ of $\alpha$ for both signs.

Two equivalent forms

Increment form
Put $h=x-c$, so $x=c+h$: $$\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}=\alpha .$$
Error form ($\varepsilon$–$\delta$)
There is $\alpha\in\mathbb R$ such that for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ with $$\begin{aligned}&x\in J,\;0<|x-c|<\delta\\&\Longrightarrow\;|f(x)-f(c)-\alpha(x-c)|<\varepsilon\,|x-c| .\end{aligned}$$
Why the error form is the same thing
$\bigl|\tfrac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}-\alpha\bigr|<\varepsilon$ becomes the error form after multiplying by $|x-c|>0$. We prefer it in proofs: there is no division, and it says precisely “error $\ll |x-c|$”.
✎ Working strategy To show $f$ is differentiable at $c$: expand the increment as $$f(x)-f(c)=\alpha\,(x-c)+\text{error},$$ guess $\alpha$ from the term linear in $x-c$, then prove $\dfrac{|\text{error}|}{|x-c|}\to0$.
The slope of the secant through $(c,f(c))$ and $(c+h,f(c+h))$ is the difference quotient. As $h\to0$ the secant rotates into the tangent, and its slope approaches $f'(c)$ from both sides.
PART 3 · FIRST EXAMPLES

Constant and affine functions

(a) Constant function $f(x)=C$

Claim
$f$ is differentiable at every $c\in J$, and $f'(c)=0$.
Proof. Try $\alpha=0$. For every $x\in J$, $$|f(x)-f(c)-0\cdot(x-c)|=|C-C|=0<\varepsilon|x-c|\quad(x\ne c).$$ So every $\delta>0$ works for every $\varepsilon$, and $f'(c)=0$. $\blacksquare$

(b) Affine function $f(x)=ax+b$

Claim
$f$ is differentiable at every $c\in J$, and $f'(c)=a$.
✎ Rough work $f(x)-f(c)=(ax+b)-(ac+b)=a(x-c)$. This is already a multiple of $x-c$ with no error left over, so $\alpha=a$.
Proof. With $\alpha=a$: $\;|f(x)-f(c)-a(x-c)|=|a(x-c)-a(x-c)|=0<\varepsilon|x-c|$ for all $x\ne c$, so any $\delta$ works. Hence $f'(c)=a$. $\blacksquare$
Chords of a line all have the same slope, so there is nothing to take a limit of: the tangent is the line. Left: slope $0$. Right: slope $a$.
PART 4 · A GENUINE ERROR TERM

The power function $f(x)=x^n$

Claim
For $n\in\mathbb N$, $f(x)=x^n$ is differentiable at every $c\in\mathbb R$ and $\;f'(c)=nc^{\,n-1}$.

The case $n=1$ is the affine case, so let $n\ge2$.

✎ Rough work — binomial expansion $$\begin{aligned}(c+h)^n-c^n&=nc^{n-1}h+\binom n2c^{n-2}h^2+\cdots+h^n\\&=nc^{n-1}h+\underbrace{\sum_{k=2}^{n}\binom nk c^{n-k}h^k}_{\text{every term contains }h^2}.\end{aligned}$$ So $\alpha=nc^{n-1}$, and the sum is the error to be controlled.
Error estimate. For $|h|<1$ we have $|h|^k\le|h|^2$ when $k\ge2$, hence $$\Bigl|\sum_{k=2}^{n}\binom nk c^{n-k}h^k\Bigr|\le|h|^2\underbrace{\sum_{k=2}^{n}\binom nk|c|^{n-k}}_{=:M}\;,$$ where $M\ge1$ depends only on $n$ and $c$ (the $k=n$ term is $1$).
Proof. Let $\varepsilon>0$ and choose $\delta=\min\{1,\varepsilon/M\}$. If $0<|h|<\delta$, then $$|f(c+h)-f(c)-nc^{n-1}h|\le M|h|^2<M|h|\,\delta\le\varepsilon|h| .$$ So $f'(c)=nc^{n-1}$. $\blacksquare$
Case $n=3,\ c=1$ (so $M=4$). The true error $3h^2+h^3$ (blue) lies under the bound $Mh^2$ (amber), which lies under the allowed error $\varepsilon h$ (red) until $h=\varepsilon/M$. That value is the $\delta$ of the proof.

PART 5 · SHORT ANSWER QUESTIONS

Practice problems

Try each problem on paper first, then open the solution.

SAQ 1. Prove from the definition that $f(x)=\dfrac1x$ is differentiable at every $c\neq0$ and $f^{\prime}(c)=-\dfrac1{c^2}$.

Rough work. $\dfrac{1/x-1/c}{x-c}=\dfrac{c-x}{xc(x-c)}=-\dfrac1{xc}$.
Proof. For $|x-c|<|c|/2$ we have $x\ne0$, and there the quotient equals $-\dfrac1{xc}$. As $x\to c$ this tends to $-\dfrac1{c^2}$. $\blacksquare$

SAQ 2. Using the error form, find a $\delta$ (in terms of $\varepsilon$) that works for $f(x)=x^2$ at $c=2$.

$\alpha=4$ and $|x^2-4-4(x-2)|=(x-2)^2$. We need $(x-2)^2<\varepsilon|x-2|$, i.e. $|x-2|<\varepsilon$. So $\delta=\varepsilon$ works.

SAQ 3. Let $f(x)=x|x|$. Show $f$ is differentiable at $0$ and find $f^{\prime}(0)$.

$\dfrac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}=\dfrac{h|h|}{h}=|h|\to0$ as $h\to0$. Hence $f^{\prime}(0)=0$. $\blacksquare$

SAQ 4. Show that $f(x)=\sqrt{|x|}$ is not differentiable at $0$, even though it is continuous there.

$\dfrac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}=\dfrac{\sqrt{|h|}}{h}$, which equals $\dfrac1{\sqrt h}\to+\infty$ for $h\to0^+$ and $-\dfrac1{\sqrt{|h|}}\to-\infty$ for $h\to0^-$. No finite limit exists. Geometrically the tangent at the origin is vertical. $\blacksquare$

SAQ 5. Suppose $|f(x)|\le x^2$ for all $x\in\mathbb R$. Prove $f$ is differentiable at $0$ with $f^{\prime}(0)=0$.

Putting $x=0$ gives $f(0)=0$. Then $\Bigl|\dfrac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}\Bigr|=\dfrac{|f(h)|}{|h|}\le|h|\to0$, so $f^{\prime}(0)=0$ by the sandwich lemma. $\blacksquare$

SAQ 6. If $f$ is differentiable at $c$, show $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(c+h)-f(c-h)}{2h}=f^{\prime}(c)$. Is the converse true?

Split: $\dfrac{f(c+h)-f(c-h)}2\cdot\dfrac1h=\dfrac12\Bigl[\dfrac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}+\dfrac{f(c-h)-f(c)}{-h}\Bigr]\to\dfrac12\bigl[f^{\prime}(c)+f^{\prime}(c)\bigr]=f^{\prime}(c)$.
Converse is false: for $f(x)=|x|$ at $0$ the symmetric quotient is $\dfrac{|h|-|h|}{2h}=0\to0$, yet $|x|$ is not differentiable at $0$.

SAQ 7. Find the equation of the tangent line to $y=x^3$ at $c=1$, and use the error form to explain in what sense it is the best linear approximation.

$f(1)=1$ and $f^{\prime}(1)=3$, so the tangent is $y=1+3(x-1)$. The error is $x^3-1-3(x-1)=(x-1)^2(x+2)$, and $\dfrac{|\text{error}|}{|x-1|}=|x-1|\,|x+2|\to0$. Any other slope $m\ne3$ leaves error $\approx(3-m)(x-1)$, which is of the same order as $|x-1|$.
