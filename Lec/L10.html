Lecture 10 · Functions, continued

Composition, and building bijections

Today has two acts. First: chaining functions together with composition, and asking whether one-one and onto survive the chain. Second, the fun part: using nothing but linear maps and a little cleverness, we'll build explicit bijections between sets that look nothing alike — finite intervals, half-lines, all of \(\mathbb{R}\), and infinite sets like \(\mathbb{N}\) and \(\mathbb{Z}\) that turn out to be "the same size."

01Chaining functions

Composition of functions

Let \(f:X\to Y\) and \(g:Y\to Z\) be two functions — note the codomain of \(f\) matches the domain of \(g\), which is exactly what makes chaining possible. The composition of \(f\) and \(g\) is the function \(g\circ f:X\to Z\) defined by

\((g\circ f)(x):=g(f(x)).\)
The composition diagram

Read \(g\circ f\) right-to-left: apply \(f\) first, then \(g\). The direct arrow \(X\to Z\) labeled \(g\circ f\) is shorthand for "go through \(Y\)."

Try it

Let \(f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\), \(f(x)=x^2\), \(g(x)=x+2\). Is \(g\circ f = f\circ g\)?

No. \((g\circ f)(x)=g(x^2)=x^2+2\), while \((f\circ g)(x)=f(x+2)=(x+2)^2=x^2+4x+4\). At \(x=1\): \(3\) versus \(9\) — composition is not commutative in general. Order matters, which is exactly why the notation reads right-to-left.
Try it

Let \(f:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)\), \(f(x)=x^2\), and \(g:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}\), \(g(x)=\sqrt{x}\) (the nonnegative root). Is \(g\circ f=f\circ g\)?

Still no, and this pair is worth sitting with. \((g\circ f)(x)=\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\) — defined for every real \(x\), and equal to \(x\) only when \(x\ge0\). \((f\circ g)(x)=(\sqrt x)^2=x\) — but this is only defined for \(x\ge0\), since \(g\) needs a nonnegative input. So even ignoring the values, the two composites don't even share a domain: \(g\circ f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) but \(f\circ g:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)\). "Squaring then rooting" and "rooting then squaring" are genuinely different operations.
\(AA^T = \begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a&c\\b&d\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a^2+b^2 & ac+bd\\ ac+bd & c^2+d^2\end{bmatrix}\), so \((g\circ f)(A)=\operatorname{trace}(AA^T)=a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2\) — the sum of squares of every entry of \(A\). (This quantity has a name you may meet later: the square of the Frobenius norm of \(A\).)
02What survives the chain

Composing well-behaved functions

Let \(f:X\to Y\) and \(g:Y\to Z\). Three questions, each worth proving rather than just believing:

  1. If both \(f\) and \(g\) are one-one, is \(g\circ f\) one-one?
  2. If both \(f\) and \(g\) are onto, is \(g\circ f\) onto?
  3. If both \(f\) and \(g\) are bijective, is \(g\circ f\) bijective?

1. Yes. Suppose \((g\circ f)(x_1)=(g\circ f)(x_2)\), i.e. \(g(f(x_1))=g(f(x_2))\). Since \(g\) is one-one, \(f(x_1)=f(x_2)\). Since \(f\) is one-one, \(x_1=x_2\).

2. Yes. Take any \(z\in Z\). Since \(g\) is onto, \(z=g(y)\) for some \(y\in Y\). Since \(f\) is onto, \(y=f(x)\) for some \(x\in X\). So \(z=g(f(x))=(g\circ f)(x)\) — every \(z\) is hit.

3. Yes — immediately, since bijective just means one-one and onto simultaneously, and both properties survive by 1 and 2.

This is genuinely useful: to build a complicated bijection, it's often much easier to build it as a composition of several simple bijections, each individually easy to verify. That's exactly the strategy for the rest of this lecture.

Yes, in more than one way — e.g. \(f(x)=4x\), \(g(x)=e^x\), giving \((g\circ f)(x)=e^{4x}\). Breaking a complicated function into a chain of simple, individually-understood steps is precisely the chain rule's home turf once we reach differentiation — this is the same decomposition instinct, just without derivatives yet.
03The workhorse construction

A bijection between any two intervals

Here's a question that sounds like it should be hard: is there a bijection between \([a,b]\) and \([c,d]\), no matter how differently sized these intervals are? The answer is yes, and the construction is just "the straight line through the two corner points."

Interactive — the linear bijection [a,b] → [c,d]
x = 2.5

We want \(f:[a,b]\to[c,d]\) with \(f(a)=c\) and \(f(b)=d\) — the graph is the line segment from \((a,c)\) to \((b,d)\). Its equation is \(y-c=\left(\frac{d-c}{b-a}\right)(x-a)\), which rearranges to:

\(f(x)=\left(\dfrac{x-a}{b-a}\right)(d-c)+c.\)

A useful way to read this: \(\frac{x-a}{b-a}\) rescales \(x\) to a fraction between \(0\) and \(1\) of the way across \([a,b]\); multiplying by \((d-c)\) and adding \(c\) places that same fraction across \([c,d]\).

Special case worth naming: composing \(f:[a,b]\to[0,1]\), \(f(x)=\frac{x-a}{b-a}\), with \(g:[0,1]\to[c,d]\), \(g(x)=(d-c)x+c\), gives exactly the same map — \([0,1]\) is a convenient "universal middleman" for building a bijection between any two closed intervals.

Notation and a shortcut worth memorizing

Write \(A\approx B\) to mean "there is a bijection from \(A\) to \(B\)." Using \(f(x)=(b-a)x+a\) and its inverse \(g(x)=\frac{x-a}{b-a}\), which interval-type pairs does this immediately handle?

\((0,1)\approx(a,b)\), \([0,1)\approx[a,b)\), \((0,1]\approx(a,b]\), \([0,1]\approx[a,b]\) — and running \(g\) the other direction gives all four reverse statements too. Because the map is linear (hence continuous and strictly monotonic), it automatically sends endpoints to endpoints and open ends to open ends, so all four "flavors" of interval transfer for free from one same-flavor pair.
Shifting by a constant is a bijection on all of \(\mathbb{R}\), so it restricts to bijections on any matching pair of rays: \((0,\infty)\approx(a,\infty)\), \([0,\infty)\approx[a,\infty)\), \((-\infty,0]\approx(-\infty,a]\), \((-\infty,0)\approx(-\infty,a)\) — with \(g(x)=x-a\) providing each inverse.
04Counting the "same size" of infinite sets

Bijections between ℕ, ℤ, and its even/odd twins

Let \(W=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\), and let \(\mathbb{E}\) and \(\mathbb{O}\) denote the even and odd natural numbers. All of these "look" like they should be different sizes than \(\mathbb{N}\) — \(W\) has one extra element, \(\mathbb{E}\) and \(\mathbb{O}\) each look like "half" of \(\mathbb{N}\). Bijections say otherwise.

\(\mathbb{N}\to W\): \(f(n)=n-1\). Every \(n\ge1\) lands on a unique \(w\ge0\), and every \(w\ge0\) is hit by \(n=w+1\).
\(\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{O}\): \(f(n)=2n-1\). \(1,2,3,4,\dots\mapsto1,3,5,7,\dots\) — check it yourself: is this one-one? Onto \(\mathbb{O}\)?
One-one: \(2n_1-1=2n_2-1\implies n_1=n_2\), immediate. Onto: any odd number has the form \(2k-1\) for some \(k\ge1\in\mathbb{N}\) by definition of "odd," and that \(k\) is exactly the preimage. Both conditions hold, so it's a bijection.
Problems for discussion
  1. Find a bijection \(\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{O}\).
  2. Is \(f:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\), \(f(m,n)=2^{m-1}(2n-1)\), a bijection?
  3. Construct a bijection \(\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Z}\).

1. \(f(n)=n+1\) works: every even \(n\) maps to the next odd number, and it's a bijection by the same style of argument as above (or just compose the \(\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{E}\) and \(\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{O}\) bijections: \(\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{O}\)).

2. Yes — and it's worth seeing why, since it's cleverer than it looks. Every natural number \(N\) has a unique factorization \(N=2^{m-1}(2n-1)\), where \(2^{m-1}\) is the largest power of \(2\) dividing \(N\) (the "binary trailing zeros," essentially) and the odd leftover factor is \(2n-1\). Existence and uniqueness of this factorization is exactly one-one-ness and onto-ness of \(f\). This single map is a classical explicit bijection \(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\).

3. Zig-zag through the integers alternating sides of \(0\):

\[ f(n)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{n-1}{2} & n\text{ odd}\\[4pt] -\dfrac{n}{2} & n\text{ even}\end{cases} \]

giving \(1\mapsto0,\ 2\mapsto-1,\ 3\mapsto1,\ 4\mapsto-2,\ 5\mapsto2,\dots\)

The same bijection, viewed from the ℤ side. Lay \(\mathbb{Z}\) out zig-zagging and read off which natural number lands on each integer:
z0−11−22−33−44⋯
n123456789⋯

Reading the table as a function \(f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{N}\):

\[ f(n)=\begin{cases}2n+1 & n\ge0\\ -2n & n<0\end{cases} \]

This is (essentially) the inverse of the zig-zag map above, going the other direction — a good exercise is to check the two really do undo each other.

05The most famous thought experiment about ∞

Hilbert's Hotel

A hotel has infinitely many rooms, numbered \(1,2,3,\dots\), and every single room is occupied — completely full, no vacancies. A new guest arrives. Can the manager still fit them in?

Interactive — Hilbert's Hotel

Every room 1–10 (shown here as a stand-in for infinitely many) starts occupied by "guest n."

Move the guest currently in room \(n\) to room \(n+1\), for every \(n\) simultaneously. This is exactly the bijection \(f(n)=n+1\) from \(\mathbb{N}\) to \(\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}\) — every existing guest still has a room, and room \(1\) is now free for the newcomer. This only works because the hotel is infinite; try the same trick with a 10-room hotel and guest 10 has nowhere to go.
Move the guest in room \(n\) to room \(2n\) — this frees up every odd-numbered room, \(1,3,5,7,\dots\), which is itself an infinite collection of rooms (exactly \(\mathbb{O}\), from Section 4). Put the infinitely many new guests into those odd rooms, one per room. This is precisely the bijection \(\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{E}\) freeing up a copy of \(\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{O}\) worth of space.

Hilbert's Hotel is a vivid way to internalize something Lecture 3's inverse-function theorem already hinted at: on an infinite set, a map can be one-one without being onto (shift by 1 misses room 1), which is impossible on a finite set of matching size. Infinite sets have room to spare in a way finite sets never do.

06When the endpoints get in the way

Three constructions worth remembering

The linear map from Section 3 handles \([a,b]\leftrightarrow[c,d]\) beautifully, but it can't change whether an endpoint is open or closed — a linear bijection sends endpoints to endpoints, period. So three genuinely different tricks are needed for three genuinely different obstacles.

Trick 1 — closed to open: \([0,1]\to(0,1)\).

Direct linear maps can't do this (they'd need to send an endpoint to a non-endpoint). Instead, sacrifice a countable "staircase" of points to make room: let \(A=\{0,1,\frac12,\frac13,\frac14,\dots\}\subseteq[0,1]\) and \(B=\{\frac12,\frac13,\frac14,\dots\}\subseteq(0,1)\). Define \(f(0)=\frac12\), \(f(1)=\frac13\), and \(f\!\left(\frac1n\right)=\frac1{n+2}\) for \(n\ge2\) — this shifts \(A\) bijectively onto \(B\), sliding every point of \(A\) two steps down the staircase. Everywhere else, \([0,1]\setminus A=(0,1)\setminus B\) exactly (both equal \(\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\frac1{n+1},\frac1n\right)\)), so just use the identity there. Glue the two pieces together and you get a bijection \([0,1]\to(0,1)\).

Trick 2 — bounded to unbounded: \((0,1)\to\mathbb{R}\).

Hint: is \(f(x)=\tan x\) a bijection from \(\left(-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2\right)\) to \(\mathbb{R}\)? (It is — you'll be in a position to prove this rigorously once we cover continuity and the intermediate value theorem.) Compose that with the interval-rescaling map from Section 3, \((0,1)\to\left(-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2\right)\), and you get a bijection \((0,1)\to\mathbb{R}\) as a two-step composition — exactly the "build complicated bijections from simple ones" strategy from Section 2.

Trick 3 — a self-map with a twist: is \(f(x)=\frac{1-x}{1+x}\) a bijection \([0,1]\to[0,1]\)?
No — check the range: \(f(0)=1\) and \(f(1)=0\), and \(f\) is strictly decreasing on \([0,1]\) (you can verify this using the quotient rule once we reach differentiation, or directly by cross-multiplying), so it does map \([0,1]\) onto \([0,1]\) bijectively after all — it's just decreasing rather than increasing, unlike every example so far in this lecture. It's a useful reminder that "bijection" says nothing about monotonic direction.
Yes. One-one: cross-multiplying \(\frac{x_1+1}{x_1-1}=\frac{x_2+1}{x_2-1}\) forces \(x_1=x_2\) after simplification. Onto: solving \(y=\frac{x+1}{x-1}\) for \(x\) gives \(x=\frac{y+1}{y-1}\), which is defined for every \(y\ne1\) — so every target value has an explicit preimage, and in fact this formula shows \(f\) is its own inverse, \(f=f^{-1}\).
07Before next lecture

Self-assessment

Part A — recall

Write down the definition of \(g\circ f\), and state which of one-one, onto, bijective are preserved when both \(f\) and \(g\) have that property.

\((g\circ f)(x)=g(f(x))\). All three properties (one-one, onto, bijective) are preserved by composition when both \(f\) and \(g\) have them.
Part B — apply

Construct an explicit bijection \([2,5]\to[10,20]\).

Using \(f(x)=\left(\frac{x-a}{b-a}\right)(d-c)+c\) with \(a=2,b=5,c=10,d=20\): \(f(x)=\left(\frac{x-2}{3}\right)(10)+10=\frac{10x-20}{3}+10=\frac{10x+10}{3}\). Check: \(f(2)=10\), \(f(5)=20\). ✓.
Part B — apply

Is \(f:[0,\infty)\to[0,1)\), \(f(x)=\frac{x}{x+1}\), a bijection? If so, find its inverse.

Yes. One-one: if \(\frac{x_1}{x_1+1}=\frac{x_2}{x_2+1}\), cross-multiplying gives \(x_1(x_2+1)=x_2(x_1+1)\implies x_1=x_2\). Onto \([0,1)\): solve \(y=\frac{x}{x+1}\) for \(x\): \(y(x+1)=x\implies yx+y=x\implies y=x(1-y)\implies x=\frac{y}{1-y}\), which is a well-defined nonnegative number exactly when \(0\le y<1\) — matching the codomain precisely. So \(f^{-1}(y)=\frac{y}{1-y}\).
Part C — think

If \(g\circ f\) is one-one, must \(f\) be one-one? Must \(g\) be one-one?

\(f\) must be one-one: if \(f(x_1)=f(x_2)\), then \(g(f(x_1))=g(f(x_2))\), i.e. \((g\circ f)(x_1)=(g\circ f)(x_2)\), and one-one-ness of \(g\circ f\) forces \(x_1=x_2\). But \(g\) need not be one-one — \(g\) only ever gets tested on the outputs of \(f\), so \(g\) could collide two points outside \(f\)'s range without it showing up in \(g\circ f\) at all. Example: \(f:\{1\}\to\{1,2\}\), \(f(1)=1\); \(g:\{1,2\}\to\{1\}\), \(g(1)=g(2)=1\). Then \(g\circ f\) is trivially one-one on its single-point domain, but \(g\) itself is not one-one.
Part C — think

Using Hilbert's Hotel intuition, explain in one or two sentences why \(\mathbb{N}\) and \(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\) can have a bijection between them even though \(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\) "looks" like a two-dimensional grid and \(\mathbb{N}\) is just a line.

Dimension is a geometric notion, not fundamentally a counting one — for infinite sets, "how many points" and "how many dimensions" are independent questions. Just as Hilbert's Hotel can absorb infinitely many new guests into a hotel that was already full by re-indexing everyone, the map \(f(m,n)=2^{m-1}(2n-1)\) re-indexes the two-dimensional grid \(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\) into the one-dimensional line \(\mathbb{N}\) with no points left over and none doubled up — a genuinely surprising fact that only shows up once sets are infinite.
Where this goes next: the bijection tricks from today — especially "same size" arguments like \(\mathbb{N}\approx\mathbb{Z}\) versus the much harder question of whether \(\mathbb{N}\approx\mathbb{R}\) — are the seed of cardinality and countability, a cornerstone of the real analysis course this calculus sequence is building toward.
