Problem 1 — Riemann Integrability of a Non-Polynomial Function

[5 marks]

Define \(f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}\) by \[ f(x)=\begin{cases}1, & x=\dfrac{1}{n}\ \text{for some } n\in\{1,2,3,\dots\},\\[6pt] 0, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases} \]

(a) Show directly from the definition (using upper and lower sums) that \(f\) is Riemann integrable on \([0,1]\).
(b) Compute \(\displaystyle\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx.\)

Step 1 — Why this function is interesting

\(f\) is not a polynomial, not continuous, and has infinitely many discontinuities — at \(1,\tfrac12,\tfrac13,\dots\) and also at \(0\). Yet it is bounded and (as shown) Riemann integrable.

Step 2 — Riemann criterion

A bounded function \(f\) on \([a,b]\) is Riemann integrable if and only if for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is a partition \(P\) with \[ U(P,f)-L(P,f)<\varepsilon . \]

Step 3 — Partition construction (Part (a))

Fix \(\varepsilon>0\). Choose \(N\) with \(\tfrac1N<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\). On \(\left[\tfrac1N,1\right]\) there are exactly \(N\) spikes: \[ \frac1N,\ \frac{1}{N-1},\ \dots,\ \frac12,\ 1 . \] Enclose each in a tiny interval of length \(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2N}\). Total length of these \(N\) intervals is \(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\). Take the partition \(P\) consisting of the endpoint \(\tfrac1N\) and the endpoints of all these tiny intervals.

Spikes at \(x=1,\tfrac12,\tfrac13,\dots\); pink strips enclose each spike with arbitrarily small total length.

Step 4 — Bound \(U(P,f)-L(P,f)\)

Split \([0,1]\) into three kinds of pieces:

  • On \([0,\tfrac1N]\): \(0\le f\le 1\), so contribution \(\le 1\cdot\tfrac1N<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\).
  • On the \(N\) tiny intervals: total contribution \(\le N\cdot\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2N}=\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\).
  • On the remaining subintervals: \(f\equiv 0\), so no contribution.

Hence \(U(P,f)-L(P,f)<\varepsilon\). Since \(\varepsilon>0\) is arbitrary, \(f\) is Riemann integrable on \([0,1]\).

Step 5 — Value of the integral (Part (b))

Every subinterval of every partition contains points where \(f=0\), so \(m_i=0\) on every subinterval and \(L(P,f)=0\) for every partition \(P\). Therefore \[ \int_0^1 f(x)\,dx=\sup_P L(P,f)=0 . \]

Answer: \(f\) is Riemann integrable on \([0,1]\), and \(\displaystyle\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx=0\).

Problem 2 — Area Between Two Curves

[5 marks]

Find the area of the region enclosed between the graphs of \[ y=x^{3}\qquad\text{and}\qquad y=3x^{2}-2x, \] between \(x=0\) and \(x=2\).

Step 1 — Intersections

\[ x^{3}=3x^{2}-2x \;\Longrightarrow\; x^{3}-3x^{2}+2x=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x(x-1)(x-2)=0 . \] So the curves meet at \(x=0,1,2\). These split \([0,2]\) into \([0,1]\) and \([1,2]\).

Cyan: \(y=x^{3}\). Pink: \(y=3x^{2}-2x\). Green shading: two enclosed regions.

Step 2 — Which curve is on top?

On \((0,1)\): at \(x=0.5\), \(x^{3}=0.125\) vs \(3x^{2}-2x=-0.25\). So \(x^{3}\) is on top.
On \((1,2)\): at \(x=1.5\), \(x^{3}=3.375\) vs \(3x^{2}-2x=3.75\). So \(3x^{2}-2x\) is on top.

Step 3 — Set up the area

\[ A=\int_0^1\bigl(x^{3}-3x^{2}+2x\bigr)dx +\int_1^2\bigl(-x^{3}+3x^{2}-2x\bigr)dx . \]

Step 4 — Evaluate

\[ \int_0^1\bigl(x^{3}-3x^{2}+2x\bigr)dx =\Bigl[\tfrac{x^{4}}{4}-x^{3}+x^{2}\Bigr]_0^1 =\tfrac14-1+1=\tfrac14 . \] \[ \int_1^2\bigl(-x^{3}+3x^{2}-2x\bigr)dx =\Bigl[-\tfrac{x^{4}}{4}+x^{3}-x^{2}\Bigr]_1^2 =0-\Bigl(-\tfrac14\Bigr)=\tfrac14 . \]

Step 5 — Add

\[ A=\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac12 . \]

Answer: \(\displaystyle A=\frac12\) square units.

Problem 3 — Arc Length, Volume and Surface of Revolution

[5 marks]

Consider the curve \(y=x^{2}\) on \(0\le x\le 1\).

(a) Find the arc length of the curve.
(b) Find the volume obtained by revolving the region under the curve about the \(x\)-axis.
(c) Find the surface area obtained by revolving the curve itself about the \(x\)-axis.

Step 0 — The three formulas

For \(y=f(x)\) on \([a,b]\): \[ L=\int_a^b\!\sqrt{1+(f')^{2}}\,dx,\quad V=\pi\!\int_a^b\! f^{2}\,dx,\quad S=2\pi\!\int_a^b\! f\sqrt{1+(f')^{2}}\,dx . \]

Region under \(y=x^{2}\) on \([0,1]\); the yellow strip at \(x=0.7\) is a representative element for the volume/surface integrals.

Step 1 — Derivatives

\(f(x)=x^{2}\), so \(f'(x)=2x\) and \[ 1+(f')^{2}=1+4x^{2}. \]

Part (a) — Arc length

The arc length element is \[ ds=\sqrt{dx^{2}+dy^{2}}=\sqrt{1+(f'(x))^{2}}\,dx =\sqrt{1+4x^{2}}\,dx . \]

The tiny segment \(ds\) on the curve is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with sides \(dx\) and \(dy\). Adding many such segments gives the arc length.

Therefore \[ L=\int_0^1\sqrt{1+4x^{2}}\,dx . \] Substitute \(2x=\sinh t\), so \(dx=\tfrac12\cosh t\,dt\), \(\sqrt{1+4x^{2}}=\cosh t\), limits \(t=0\) to \(T=\ln(2+\sqrt5)\): \[ L=\tfrac12\!\int_0^{T}\!\cosh^{2}t\,dt =\tfrac14\Bigl[t+\tfrac{\sinh 2t}{2}\Bigr]_0^{T}. \] Now \(\sinh T=2,\ \cosh T=\sqrt5\), so \(\sinh 2T=4\sqrt5\). Hence \[ L=\frac{2\sqrt5+\ln(2+\sqrt5)}{4}\approx 1.4789 . \]

Answer (a): \(\displaystyle L=\frac{2\sqrt5+\ln(2+\sqrt5)}{4}\approx 1.4789\).

Part (b) — Volume of revolution

Rotating the region under \(y=x^{2}\) about the \(x\)-axis produces a solid whose cross-section at position \(x\) is a disk of radius \(y=x^{2}\) and thickness \(dx\): \[ dV=\pi y^{2}\,dx=\pi x^{4}\,dx . \]

Revolving the region under \(y=x^{2}\) about the \(x\)-axis produces a horn-shaped solid. Each cross-section perpendicular to the axis is a disk; the highlighted one shows the representative element \(dV=\pi y^{2}\,dx\).

Integrate over the interval: \[ V=\pi\!\int_0^1\!x^{4}\,dx =\pi\Bigl[\tfrac{x^{5}}{5}\Bigr]_0^1 =\frac{\pi}{5}\approx 0.6283 . \]

Answer (b): \(V=\pi/5\approx 0.6283\).

Part (c) — Surface area of revolution

When the curve itself is rotated, the small surface element is the lateral surface of a frustum of slant length \(ds\) and mean radius \(y\): \[ dS=2\pi y\,ds=2\pi y\sqrt{1+(y')^{2}}\,dx =2\pi x^{2}\sqrt{1+4x^{2}}\,dx . \]

The surface of revolution. The highlighted frustum element has slant length \(ds\) and its lateral area is \(2\pi y\,ds\) — this is the surface element.

Integrate: \[ S=2\pi\!\int_0^1\!x^{2}\sqrt{1+4x^{2}}\,dx . \] With the same substitution, \[ S=\frac{\pi}{4}\!\int_0^{T}\!\sinh^{2}t\cosh^{2}t\,dt =\frac{\pi}{32}\Bigl[\tfrac{\sinh 4t}{4}-t\Bigr]_0^{T}. \] Since \(\sinh 2T=4\sqrt5\) and \(\cosh 2T=9\), \(\sinh 4T=72\sqrt5\). Therefore \[ S=\frac{\pi}{32}\Bigl[18\sqrt5-\ln(2+\sqrt5)\Bigr]\approx 3.8098 . \]

Answer (c): \(\displaystyle S=\frac{\pi}{32}\bigl[18\sqrt5-\ln(2+\sqrt5)\bigr] \approx 3.8098\).

Compare the three elements: \(ds=\sqrt{1+4x^{2}}\,dx\) (length), \(dV=\pi x^{4}\,dx\) (volume), \(dS=2\pi x^{2}\sqrt{1+4x^{2}}\,dx\) (surface). All come from the same curve but with different geometric "weights".

Problem 4 — Fundamental Theorem of Calculus in Action

[5 marks]

Let \(f(t)=1\) for \(t\in[0,1]\) and \(f(t)=2\) for \(t\in(1,2]\). For \(x\in[0,2]\) define \[ F(x)=\int_0^x f(t)\,dt . \]

(a) Find an explicit formula for \(F(x)\).
(b) Sketch the graph of \(F\).
(c) Where is \(F\) continuous? Where differentiable?
(d) How does this illustrate the FTC?

Part (a) — Compute \(F(x)\)

For \(0\le x\le 1\): \(F(x)=\int_0^x 1\,dt=x\).
For \(1<x\le 2\): \(F(x)=\int_0^1 1\,dt+\int_1^x 2\,dt=1+2(x-1)=2x-1\).

\[ F(x)=\begin{cases} x, & 0\le x\le 1,\\ 2x-1, & 1<x\le 2.\end{cases} \]

Part (b) — Graph of \(F\)

\(F\) is piecewise linear — slope 1 on \([0,1]\), slope 2 on \([1,2]\). The corner at \((1,1)\) is where \(F\) fails to be differentiable.

Part (c) — Continuity and differentiability

At \(x=1\): \[ \lim_{x\to 1^-}F=1,\quad \lim_{x\to 1^+}F=1,\quad F(1)=1, \] so \(F\) is continuous at \(1\) (and hence on all of \([0,2]\)).

Left derivative at \(1\): \(1\). Right derivative at \(1\): \(2\). Since \(1\neq 2\), \(F\) is not differentiable at \(x=1\). Elsewhere \(F'(x)=f(x)\).

Part (d) — Connection with the FTC

FTC Part 1: if \(f\) is continuous at \(c\), then \(F'(c)=f(c)\). Here \(f\) has a jump at \(x=1\), and precisely there \(F\) fails to be differentiable. Everywhere \(f\) is continuous, \(F'(x)=f(x)\).

FTC Part 2: \[ \int_0^2 f(t)\,dt=1\cdot 1+2\cdot 1=3 =F(2)-F(0). \] Both parts agree, even though \(f\) is only piecewise continuous.

Key point: continuity of \(f\) at a point is only needed for differentiability of \(F\) at that point; \(F\) remains continuous regardless.

Problem 5 — A Quadratic Polynomial from Integral Conditions

[5 marks]

Find \(P(x)=ax^{2}+bx+c\) such that \[ P(0)=P(1)=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \int_0^1 P(x)\,dx=1 . \] Verify by direct substitution.

Step 1 — Apply \(P(0)=0\)

\(P(0)=c=0\), so \(P(x)=ax^{2}+bx\).

Step 2 — Apply \(P(1)=0\)

\(a+b=0\Rightarrow b=-a\), so \(P(x)=a(x^{2}-x)\).

Step 3 — Apply the integral condition

\[ \int_0^1 a(x^{2}-x)\,dx =a\Bigl[\tfrac{x^{3}}{3}-\tfrac{x^{2}}{2}\Bigr]_0^1 =a\Bigl(\tfrac13-\tfrac12\Bigr) =-\frac{a}{6}=1 . \] Hence \(a=-6\), \(b=6\), \(c=0\).

\(P(x)=6x(1-x)\); the shaded area (with sign) equals \(1\).

Answer: \(\displaystyle P(x)=6x(1-x)=-6x^{2}+6x\).

Step 4 — Verification

\(P(0)=0\), \(P(1)=0\), and \[ \int_0^1 6x(1-x)\,dx =6\Bigl[\tfrac{x^{2}}{2}-\tfrac{x^{3}}{3}\Bigr]_0^1 =6\cdot\tfrac16=1 . \checkmark \]
