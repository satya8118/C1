SAQ 1: Double Zeros & Derivatives

Let \( f \) be a polynomial. Suppose that \( f \) has a double zero at \( a \) and \( b \) (with \( a < b \)). (A polynomial \( f \) has a double zero at \( x = c \) if \( f(x) = (x - c)^2 g(x) \) for some polynomial \( g \)).

Show that \( f'(x) \) has at least three roots in \( [a, b] \).

Hint: What are the values of \( f(a), f'(a), f(b), \) and \( f'(b) \)? Apply Rolle's Theorem to \( f(x) \) on the interval \( [a, b] \).
Solution:

Since \( f \) has a double zero at both \( a \) and \( b \):

  • \( f(a) = 0 \) and \( f'(a) = 0 \)
  • \( f(b) = 0 \) and \( f'(b) = 0 \)

By Rolle's Theorem applied to \( f(x) \) on \( [a, b] \), since \( f(a) = f(b) = 0 \), there exists at least one point \( c \in (a, b) \) such that \( f'(c) = 0 \).

Collecting roots of \( f'(x) \):

  1. \( x = a \) (since \( f'(a) = 0 \))
  2. \( x = c \) where \( c \in (a, b) \)
  3. \( x = b \) (since \( f'(b) = 0 \))

Since \( a < c < b \), these three roots are distinct and lie in \( [a, b] \). \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 2: Parallel Tangent Lines

Suppose that \( f \) and \( g \) are continuous on \( [a, b] \) and that \( f' \) and \( g' \) are continuous on \( (a, b) \). Assume that \( f(a) = g(a) \) and \( f(b) = g(b) \).

Prove that there is a number \( c \) in \( (a, b) \) such that the line tangent to the graph of \( f \) at \( (c, f(c)) \) is parallel to the line tangent to the graph of \( g \) at \( (c, g(c)) \).

Hint: Define an auxiliary function \( h(x) = f(x) - g(x) \) and apply Rolle's Theorem to \( h(x) \).
Solution:

Define \( h(x) = f(x) - g(x) \).

  • \( h(a) = f(a) - g(a) = 0 \)
  • \( h(b) = f(b) - g(b) = 0 \)

By Rolle's Theorem, there exists \( c \in (a, b) \) such that \( h'(c) = 0 \).

\[ h'(c) = f'(c) - g'(c) = 0 \implies f'(c) = g'(c) \]

Since derivative values equal tangent slopes, equal slopes imply parallel tangent lines at \( x = c \). \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 3: Second Derivative Zeroes

Let \( f \) be twice differentiable on \( (a, b) \), and suppose that \( f(x) = 0 \) at three distinct points in \( (a, b) \). Prove that there is a point \( x_0 \) in \( (a, b) \) at which \( f''(x_0) = 0 \).

Hint: Use Rolle's Theorem twice: first to find roots of \( f'(x) \), then apply it to \( f'(x) \) to find roots of \( f''(x) \).
Solution:

Let three roots be \( x_1 < x_2 < x_3 \).

Applying Rolle's Theorem to \( f(x) \) on \( [x_1, x_2] \) and \( [x_2, x_3] \):

  • Exists \( c_1 \in (x_1, x_2) \) such that \( f'(c_1) = 0 \).
  • Exists \( c_2 \in (x_2, x_3) \) such that \( f'(c_2) = 0 \).

Now apply Rolle's Theorem to \( f'(x) \) on \( [c_1, c_2] \):

Since \( f'(c_1) = f'(c_2) = 0 \), there exists \( x_0 \in (c_1, c_2) \) such that \( f''(x_0) = 0 \). \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 4: Strictly Increasing Functions

Prove that if \( f \) is continuous on \( [a, b] \) and differentiable on \( (a, b) \), and \( f'(x) > 0 \) for all \( x \in (a, b) \), then \( f \) is strictly increasing on \( [a, b] \).

Hint: Pick two points \( x_1 < x_2 \) in \( [a, b] \) and apply the Mean Value Theorem on \( [x_1, x_2] \).
Solution:

For any \( x_1 < x_2 \) in \( [a, b] \), MVT guarantees a point \( c \in (x_1, x_2) \) such that:

\[ \frac{f(x_2) - f(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} = f'(c) \implies f(x_2) - f(x_1) = f'(c)(x_2 - x_1) \]

Since \( f'(c) > 0 \) and \( x_2 - x_1 > 0 \), their product is positive, so \( f(x_2) > f(x_1) \). Thus, \( f \) is strictly increasing. \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 5: Degree of Polynomials

If \( f'''(x) = 0 \) on \( (-\infty, \infty) \), show that there are constants \( A \), \( B \), and \( C \) such that \( f(x) = Ax^2 + Bx + C \).

Hint: Work backward by repeatedly applying the fact that if a derivative is zero everywhere, the original function is constant.
Solution:
  1. Since \( (f'')' = 0 \), \( f''(x) = 2A \) for some constant \( A \).
  2. Since \( (f'(x) - 2Ax)' = 0 \), \( f'(x) = 2Ax + B \).
  3. Since \( (f(x) - (Ax^2 + Bx))' = 0 \), \( f(x) = Ax^2 + Bx + C \). \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 6: Function Majorization

A function \( f \) is said to majorize a function \( g \) on \( [a, b] \) if \( f(x) \ge g(x) \) for all \( a \le x \le b \).

If \( m > n > 0 \), show that \( x^n \) majorizes \( x^m \) on \( [0, 1] \).

Hint: Express \( x^m \) as \( x^n \cdot x^{m-n} \) and consider what happens when raising numbers strictly between 0 and 1 to positive powers.
Solution:

Since \( m > n > 0 \), we have \( m - n > 0 \).

  • At \( x = 0 \) and \( x = 1 \): \( x^n = x^m \).
  • For \( x \in (0, 1) \): raising a fraction to a positive power gives a value less than 1, so \( x^{m-n} < 1 \).

Multiply both sides by \( x^n \):

\[ x^n \cdot x^{m-n} < x^n \cdot 1 \implies x^m < x^n \]

Thus, \( x^n \ge x^m \) on \( [0, 1] \), so \( x^n \) majorizes \( x^m \). \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 7: Constant Derivative

Suppose that \( f \) is differentiable on the whole real line \( (-\infty, \infty) \) and that \( f'(x) \) is constant. Prove that \( f \) is linear.

Hint: Let \( f'(x) = m \) for all \( x \). You can prove \( f \) is linear in two ways: (1) apply MVT on the interval between \( 0 \) and \( x \), or (2) define an auxiliary function \( g(x) = f(x) - mx \) and check its derivative.
Solution (Both Methods):

Let \( m \) be the constant value of \( f'(x) \), so that \( f'(x) = m \) for all \( x \in (-\infty, \infty) \).

Method 1: Using the Mean Value Theorem Directly

Fix any real number \( x \ne 0 \). Since \( f \) is differentiable everywhere, it is continuous on the closed interval between \( 0 \) and \( x \), and differentiable on the open interval between them.

By the Mean Value Theorem applied to \( f \) on the interval between \( 0 \) and \( x \), there exists a point \( c \) between \( 0 \) and \( x \) such that:

\[ \frac{f(x) - f(0)}{x - 0} = f'(c) \]

Since \( f'(c) = m \) everywhere:

\[ \frac{f(x) - f(0)}{x} = m \implies f(x) - f(0) = mx \implies f(x) = mx + f(0) \]

Setting the constant \( C = f(0) \), we get \( f(x) = mx + C \), which is a linear function.

Method 2: Using an Auxiliary Function

Define an auxiliary function \( g(x) = f(x) - mx \).

Differentiating \( g(x) \) with respect to \( x \):

\[ g'(x) = f'(x) - m = m - m = 0 \quad \text{for all } x \in (-\infty, \infty) \]

Since the derivative of \( g(x) \) is zero everywhere, \( g(x) \) must be a constant function. Let \( g(x) = C \) for some constant \( C \).

Evaluating at \( x = 0 \):

\[ C = g(0) = f(0) - m(0) = f(0) \]

Substituting \( g(x) = f(0) \) back into the definition of \( g(x) \):

\[ f(x) - mx = f(0) \implies f(x) = mx + f(0) \]

Thus, \( f(x) \) is of the form \( mx + C \), so \( f \) is linear. \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 8: Optimization - Minimal Perimeter

Of all rectangles with area 1, which has the smallest perimeter?

Hint: Let sides be \( x \) and \( y \). Use area \( xy = 1 \) to write perimeter \( P(x) = 2x + \frac{2}{x} \) and minimize it using calculus.
Solution:

Perimeter function: \( P(x) = 2x + 2x^{-1} \)

\[ P'(x) = 2 - \frac{2}{x^2} = 0 \implies x^2 = 1 \implies x = 1 \]

Second derivative test: \( P''(1) = 4 > 0 \) (local minimum).

Since \( x = 1 \), \( y = \frac{1}{1} = 1 \).

The rectangle with minimal perimeter is a square of dimensions \( 1 \times 1 \). \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 9: Distance Optimization

Find the point on the arc of the parabola \( y = x^2 \) for \( 0 < x < 1 \) which is nearest to the point \( (0, 1) \).

Hint: Minimize the square of distance function \( D(x) = x^2 + (x^2 - 1)^2 \).
Solution:

Squared distance function: \( f(x) = x^2 + (x^2 - 1)^2 = x^4 - x^2 + 1 \)

\[ f'(x) = 4x^3 - 2x = 2x(2x^2 - 1) = 0 \]

For \( x \in (0, 1) \), we get \( 2x^2 - 1 = 0 \implies x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \).

Corresponding \( y \)-value: \( y = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{2} \).

The nearest point is \( \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \frac{1}{2} \right) \). \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 10: Extreme Value Existence

Find a function defined on \( [0, 1] \) which does not have a maximum value on \( [0, 1] \).

Hint: The Extreme Value Theorem states continuous functions on closed intervals must have maximums. Therefore, your function must be discontinuous.
Solution:

Consider the discontinuous function:

\[ f(x) = \begin{cases} x & \text{if } 0 \le x < 1 \\ 0 & \text{if } x = 1 \end{cases} \]

Range is \( [0, 1) \). The upper bound is 1, but it is never attained anywhere in \( [0, 1] \). Thus, it has no maximum. \(\blacksquare\)

One positive number plus the square of another equals 48. Choose the numbers so that their product is as large as possible.

Find a function on \( [-1, 1] \) which is continuous but which is not differentiable at its maximum point.

SAQ 13: Least Squares Approximation

Given \( n \) numbers, \( a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n \), find a number \( x \) which best approximates them in the sense that the sum of the squares of the differences between \( x \) and the \( n \) numbers is as small as possible.

Hint: Define a function \( S(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x - a_i)^2 \). Expand or differentiate \( S(x) \) with respect to \( x \), set the derivative equal to zero, and solve for \( x \).
Solution:

Step 1: Set up the sum of squares function.

We want to minimize the function:

\[ S(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x - a_i)^2 = (x - a_1)^2 + (x - a_2)^2 + \dots + (x - a_n)^2 \]

Step 2: Find the critical point.

Differentiate \( S(x) \) with respect to \( x \):

\[ S'(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} 2(x - a_i) = 2 \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} x - \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \right) \]

Since \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} x = n x\), setting \( S'(x) = 0 \) gives:

\[ 2 \left( nx - \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \right) = 0 \implies nx = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \implies x = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \]

Step 3: Verify the minimum.

Taking the second derivative:

\[ S''(x) = \frac{d}{dx} \left[ 2 \left( nx - \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \right) \right] = 2n \]

Since \( n \ge 1 \), \( S''(x) = 2n > 0 \), confirming that this critical point gives a global minimum.

Conclusion: The number \( x \) that minimizes the sum of squared differences is the arithmetic mean of the given numbers: \[ x = \bar{a} = \frac{a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_n}{n} \] \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 14: Proof of the Product Rule

Prove the Product Rule for differentiation: \[ (fg)'(x_0) = f'(x_0)g(x_0) + f(x_0)g'(x_0) \] using the limit definition of the derivative and geometric decomposition.

Hint: Think of \( f(x) \) and \( g(x) \) as lengths of the sides of a rectangle, where area is \( f(x)g(x) \). Express the total change in area by decomposing the lighter region into three smaller sub-rectangles, then evaluate the limit as \( \Delta x \to 0 \).
Proof (Product Rule):

To find \((fg)'(x_0)\), we start with the limit definition of the derivative:

\[ \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{(fg)(x_0 + \Delta x) - (fg)(x_0)}{\Delta x} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + \Delta x)g(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)g(x_0)}{\Delta x} \quad \text{--- (1)} \]

Simplifying this expression is not as straightforward as for the sum rule. We may make use of a geometric device: think of \( f(x) \) and \( g(x) \) as the lengths of the sides of a rectangle; then \( f(x)g(x) \) is its area. The rectangles for \( x = x_0 \) and \( x = x_0 + \Delta x \) are shown below:

The area of the large rectangle is \( f(x_0 + \Delta x)g(x_0 + \Delta x) \); that of the darker rectangle is \( f(x_0)g(x_0) \). The difference \( f(x_0 + \Delta x)g(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)g(x_0) \) is the area of the lighter region, which can be decomposed into three rectangles having areas:

  • \([f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)] g(x_0)\)
  • \([f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)] [g(x_0 + \Delta x) - g(x_0)]\)
  • \(f(x_0) [g(x_0 + \Delta x) - g(x_0)]\)

Thus we have the identity:

\[ \begin{aligned} f(x_0 + \Delta x)g(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)g(x_0) &= [f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)] g(x_0) \\ &\quad + f(x_0) [g(x_0 + \Delta x) - g(x_0)] \\ &\quad + [f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)] [g(x_0 + \Delta x) - g(x_0)] \quad \text{--- (2)} \end{aligned} \]

(If you do not like geometric arguments, you can verify this identity algebraically.)

Substituting (2) into (1), we obtain:

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \Big\{ &\frac{f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)}{\Delta x} g(x_0) + f(x_0) \frac{g(x_0 + \Delta x) - g(x_0)}{\Delta x} \\ &+ \frac{[f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)][g(x_0 + \Delta x) - g(x_0)]}{\Delta x} \Big\} \quad \text{--- (3)} \end{aligned} \]

By the sum and constant multiple rules for limits, (3) equals:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\left[ \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)}{\Delta x} \right] g(x_0) + f(x_0) \left[ \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{g(x_0 + \Delta x) - g(x_0)}{\Delta x} \right] \\ &\quad + \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{[f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)][g(x_0 + \Delta x) - g(x_0)]}{\Delta x} \quad \text{--- (4)} \end{aligned} \]

We recognize the first two limits in (4) as \( f'(x_0) \) and \( g'(x_0) \), so the first two terms give: \[ f'(x_0)g(x_0) + f(x_0)g'(x_0) \] — precisely the product rule.

To show that the third limit, represented geometrically by the small rectangle in the upper right-hand corner of the figure, is zero, we use the continuity of \( g \). By the product rule for limits:

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} &\left[ \frac{f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)}{\Delta x} \cdot [g(x_0 + \Delta x) - g(x_0)] \right] \\ &= \left[ \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)}{\Delta x} \right] \cdot \left[ \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} [g(x_0 + \Delta x) - g(x_0)] \right] \\ &= f'(x_0) \cdot 0 = 0 \end{aligned} \]
Conclusion: \[ (fg)'(x_0) = f'(x_0)g(x_0) + f(x_0)g'(x_0) \] \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 15: Symmetry Properties of Step Function Integrals

Let \( s : [-a, a] \to \mathbb{R} \) be a step function on a symmetric interval about zero, where \( a > 0 \). Prove the following:

  1. If \( s \) is even (i.e., \( s(-t) = s(t) \)), then: \[ \int_{-a}^{a} s(t) \, dt = 2 \int_{0}^{a} s(t) \, dt \]
  2. If \( s \) is odd (i.e., \( s(-t) = -s(t) \)), then: \[ \int_{-a}^{a} s(t) \, dt = 0 \]
Hint: Use the additivity property of integrals to split \( \int_{-a}^{a} s(t) \, dt \) into \( \int_{-a}^{0} s(t) \, dt + \int_{0}^{a} s(t) \, dt \). For the integral over \( [-a, 0] \), use a substitution \( u = -t \) (or partition symmetry) to express it in terms of an integral over \( [0, a] \).
Proof (Using Step Function Definition):

Step 1: Partitioning the Domain

Since \( s \) is a step function on \( [-a, a] \), there exists a partition \( P = \{t_0, t_1, \dots, t_n\} \) of \( [-a, a] \) containing \( 0 \), where \( s(t) = c_k \) (a constant) on each open subinterval \( (t_{k-1}, t_k) \).

By domain additivity of step function integrals:

\[ \int_{-a}^{a} s(t) \, dt = \int_{-a}^{0} s(t) \, dt + \int_{0}^{a} s(t) \, dt \quad \text{--- (1)} \]

Step 2: Expressing Integrals as Finite Sums

For any subinterval \( I_k = (t_{k-1}, t_k) \subset [0, a] \), let \( s(t) = c_k \). The symmetric reflection interval is \( I_k' = (-t_k, -t_{k-1}) \subset [-a, 0] \), which has the same length \( \Delta t_k = t_k - t_{k-1} \).

The integral over \( [0, a] \) is the finite sum:

\[ \int_{0}^{a} s(t) \, dt = \sum_{k} c_k \Delta t_k \]
(a) Proof for Even Step Functions

If \( s \) is even, then on the reflected interval \( (-t_k, -t_{k-1}) \), \( s(t) = s(-t) = c_k \).

\[ \int_{-a}^{0} s(t) \, dt = \sum_{k} c_k \Delta t_k = \int_{0}^{a} s(t) \, dt \]

Substituting into (1):

\[ \int_{-a}^{a} s(t) \, dt = \int_{0}^{a} s(t) \, dt + \int_{0}^{a} s(t) \, dt = 2 \int_{0}^{a} s(t) \, dt \]
(b) Proof for Odd Step Functions

If \( s \) is odd, then on the reflected interval \( (-t_k, -t_{k-1}) \), \( s(t) = -s(-t) = -c_k \).

\[ \int_{-a}^{0} s(t) \, dt = \sum_{k} (-c_k) \Delta t_k = -\int_{0}^{a} s(t) \, dt \]

Substituting into (1):

\[ \int_{-a}^{a} s(t) \, dt = -\int_{0}^{a} s(t) \, dt + \int_{0}^{a} s(t) \, dt = 0 \]
Conclusion:

(a) Even \( s(t) \): \( \int_{-a}^{a} s(t) \, dt = 2 \int_{0}^{a} s(t) \, dt \)
(b) Odd \( s(t) \): \( \int_{-a}^{a} s(t) \, dt = 0 \) \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 16: Properties of Function Compositions

Suppose \( f : X \to Y \) and \( g : Y \to Z \). Show the following:

  1. If \( g \circ f \) is injective (one-to-one), then \( f \) is injective.
  2. If \( g \circ f \) is surjective (onto), then \( g \) is surjective.
Hint:
For (a): Start by assuming \( f(x_1) = f(x_2) \) for some \( x_1, x_2 \in X \), then apply \( g \) to both sides.
For (b): Take an arbitrary element \( z \in Z \). Use the surjectivity of \( g \circ f \) to find an element in \( X \) that maps to \( z \).
Proof:
(a) If \( g \circ f \) is injective, then \( f \) is injective.

To show that \( f \) is injective, we must prove that for any \( x_1, x_2 \in X \), if \( f(x_1) = f(x_2) \), then \( x_1 = x_2 \).

Assume \( f(x_1) = f(x_2) \).

Applying \( g \) to both sides gives: \[ g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) \]

By definition of function composition, this means: \[ (g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2) \]

Since \( g \circ f \) is given to be injective, it follows that \( x_1 = x_2 \).

Therefore, \( f \) is injective.

(b) If \( g \circ f \) is surjective, then \( g \) is surjective.

To show that \( g \) is surjective, we must prove that for every \( z \in Z \), there exists some element \( y \in Y \) such that \( g(y) = z \).

Let \( z \in Z \) be an arbitrary element.

Since \( g \circ f : X \to Z \) is surjective, there exists an element \( x \in X \) such that: \[ (g \circ f)(x) = z \]

By definition of composition, this means: \[ g(f(x)) = z \]

Define \( y = f(x) \). Since \( f : X \to Y \), we have \( y \in Y \).

Thus, \( g(y) = z \), which proves that every element in \( Z \) has a pre-image in \( Y \) under \( g \).

Therefore, \( g \) is surjective.

Conclusion:
(a) \( g \circ f \) injective \(\implies f\) injective.
(b) \( g \circ f \) surjective \(\implies g\) surjective. \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 17: Bijectivity of a Rational Function

Show that \( g : \mathbb{R} \setminus \{-3/2\} \to \mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\} \), defined by: \[ g(x) = \frac{4x - 1}{2x + 3} \] is a bijection.

Hint:
1. Injectivity: Assume \( g(x_1) = g(x_2) \), cross-multiply, and simplify to show \( x_1 = x_2 \).
2. Surjectivity: Set \( y = \frac{4x - 1}{2x + 3} \), solve for \( x \) in terms of \( y \), and verify that for any \( y \ne 2 \), there is a valid domain value \( x \ne -3/2 \).
Proof:

A function is a bijection if it is both injective (one-to-one) and surjective (onto).

Part 1: Injectivity (One-to-One)

Let \( x_1, x_2 \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{-3/2\} \) and assume \( g(x_1) = g(x_2) \):

\[ \frac{4x_1 - 1}{2x_1 + 3} = \frac{4x_2 - 1}{2x_2 + 3} \]

Cross-multiplying yields:

\[ (4x_1 - 1)(2x_2 + 3) = (4x_2 - 1)(2x_1 + 3) \]

Expanding both sides:

\[ 8x_1 x_2 + 12x_1 - 2x_2 - 3 = 8x_1 x_2 + 12x_2 - 2x_1 - 3 \]

Cancel \( 8x_1 x_2 \) and \( -3 \) from both sides:

\[ 12x_1 - 2x_2 = 12x_2 - 2x_1 \implies 14x_1 = 14x_2 \implies x_1 = x_2 \]

Thus, \( g \) is injective.

Part 2: Surjectivity (Onto)

Let \( y \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\} \) be an arbitrary element in the codomain. We must find \( x \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{-3/2\} \) such that \( g(x) = y \):

\[ y = \frac{4x - 1}{2x + 3} \]

Multiply by \( 2x + 3 \) and solve for \( x \):

\[ \begin{aligned} y(2x + 3) &= 4x - 1 \\ 2xy + 3y &= 4x - 1 \\ 3y + 1 &= 4x - 2xy \\ 3y + 1 &= x(4 - 2y) \\ x &= \frac{3y + 1}{4 - 2y} \end{aligned} \]

Since \( y \ne 2 \), the denominator \( 4 - 2y \ne 0 \), so \( x \) is well-defined.

Next, verify that \( x \ne -3/2 \):

\[ \frac{3y + 1}{4 - 2y} = -\frac{3}{2} \implies 2(3y + 1) = -3(4 - 2y) \implies 6y + 2 = -12 + 6y \implies 2 = -12 \quad \text{(Contradiction)} \]

Hence, for every \( y \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\} \), there exists an \( x \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{-3/2\} \) such that \( g(x) = y \).

Thus, \( g \) is surjective.

Conclusion:
Since \( g \) is both injective and surjective, \( g \) is a bijection with inverse: \[ g^{-1}(y) = \frac{3y + 1}{4 - 2y} \] \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 18: Upper Bound Limit

Suppose \( x \in \mathbb{R} \) satisfies: \[ \frac{n}{n+1} \le x \le 1 \quad \text{for every } n \in \mathbb{N} \] Show that \( x = 1 \).

Hint: Use the Squeeze Theorem by taking the limit as \( n \to \infty \) across the inequality chain, or use a proof by contradiction by assuming \( x < 1 \) and using the Archimedean Property with \( \epsilon = 1 - x > 0 \).
Proof (Two Methods):
Method 1: Taking Limits (Squeeze Argument)

We are given that for all \( n \in \mathbb{N} \):

\[ \frac{n}{n+1} \le x \le 1 \]

Taking the limit as \( n \to \infty \) on all sides of the inequality:

\[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n}{n+1} \le \lim_{n \to \infty} x \le \lim_{n \to \infty} 1 \]

Evaluating the limits:

\[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n}{n+1} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{1 + \frac{1}{n}} = 1 \]

Therefore:

\[ 1 \le x \le 1 \]

By the Squeeze Theorem (or standard inequality properties), we conclude that \( x = 1 \).

Method 2: Proof by Contradiction (Archimedean Property)

We already know \( x \le 1 \). Suppose for contradiction that \( x < 1 \).

Then \( \epsilon = 1 - x > 0 \).

By the Archimedean Property of real numbers, there exists a natural number \( N \in \mathbb{N} \) such that:

\[ N+1 > \frac{1}{\epsilon} = \frac{1}{1-x} \]

Rearranging this inequality:

\[ 1 - x > \frac{1}{N+1} \implies x < 1 - \frac{1}{N+1} = \frac{N}{N+1} \]

This contradicts the given statement that \( \frac{n}{n+1} \le x \) for every \( n \in \mathbb{N} \) (specifically for \( n = N \)).

Thus, our assumption that \( x < 1 \) must be false. Therefore, \( x = 1 \).

Conclusion: \[ x = 1 \] \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 19: Integrals of the Greatest Integer Function

Let \([x]\) (or \(\lfloor x \rfloor\)) denote the greatest integer function. Prove the following for each \( n \in \mathbb{N} \):

  1. \[ \int_{0}^{n} [x] \, dx = \frac{n(n - 1)}{2} \]
  2. \[ \int_{0}^{n^2} [\sqrt{x}] \, dx = \frac{n(n - 1)(4n + 1)}{6} \]
Hint:
For (a): Break the integral \(\int_{0}^{n}\) into a sum of integrals over subintervals \([k, k+1]\) where \([x] = k\).
For (b): Break the integral \(\int_{0}^{n^2}\) over subintervals \([k^2, (k+1)^2]\) where \([\sqrt{x}] = k\), and use the standard sum formulas for \(\sum k\) and \(\sum k^2\).
Proof:
(a) Proof for \(\int_{0}^{n} [x] \, dx\)

The greatest integer function \([x]\) is a step function. On each subinterval \([k, k+1)\) for an integer \( k \), we have \([x] = k\).

Splitting the interval of integration \([0, n]\) into \( n \) unit subintervals:

\[ \int_{0}^{n} [x] \, dx = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \int_{k}^{k+1} [x] \, dx \]

Since \([x] = k\) on \([k, k+1)\):

\[ \int_{k}^{k+1} [x] \, dx = \int_{k}^{k+1} k \, dx = k \cdot ((k+1) - k) = k \]

Summing these up:

\[ \int_{0}^{n} [x] \, dx = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} k = 0 + 1 + 2 + \dots + (n-1) = \frac{(n-1)n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} \]
(b) Proof for \(\int_{0}^{n^2} [\sqrt{x}] \, dx\)

For \( x \in [k^2, (k+1)^2) \), we have \( k \le \sqrt{x} < k+1 \), which implies \([\sqrt{x}] = k\).

Splitting the integral \([0, n^2]\) at the perfect square points \( k^2 \) for \( k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1 \):

\[ \int_{0}^{n^2} [\sqrt{x}] \, dx = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \int_{k^2}^{(k+1)^2} [\sqrt{x}] \, dx \]

Evaluating the integral on each subinterval:

\[ \int_{k^2}^{(k+1)^2} k \, dx = k \cdot \left( (k+1)^2 - k^2 \right) = k \cdot (2k + 1) = 2k^2 + k \]

Summing from \( k = 0 \) to \( n-1 \):

\[ \int_{0}^{n^2} [\sqrt{x}] \, dx = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} (2k^2 + k) = 2 \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} k^2 + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} k \]

Using standard summation formulas for \( m = n-1 \):

  • \(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} k = \frac{(n-1)n}{2}\)
  • \(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} k^2 = \frac{(n-1)n(2n-1)}{6}\)

Substituting these formulas in:

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_{0}^{n^2} [\sqrt{x}] \, dx &= 2 \cdot \frac{n(n-1)(2n-1)}{6} + \frac{n(n-1)}{2} \\ &= \frac{n(n-1)(2n-1)}{3} + \frac{n(n-1)}{2} \\ &= \frac{n(n-1)}{6} \left[ 2(2n-1) + 3 \right] \\ &= \frac{n(n-1)}{6} (4n - 2 + 3) \\ &= \frac{n(n-1)(4n+1)}{6} \end{aligned} \]
Conclusion:
(a) \( \int_{0}^{n} [x] \, dx = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} \)
(b) \( \int_{0}^{n^2} [\sqrt{x}] \, dx = \frac{n(n-1)(4n+1)}{6} \) \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 21: Uniqueness of Real Roots

Show that the polynomial equation \( x^3 + 2x - 5 = 0 \) has exactly one real root.

Hint:
1. Use the Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT) to show that at least one root exists.
2. Use the derivative \( f'(x) \) to show that the function is strictly increasing (or use Rolle's Theorem) to prove that there cannot be more than one root.
Proof:

Let \( f(x) = x^3 + 2x - 5 \). Since \( f(x) \) is a polynomial, it is continuous and differentiable on the entire real line \( (-\infty, \infty) \).

Step 1: Existence of at least one root (Intermediate Value Theorem)

Evaluating \( f(x) \) at selected points:

  • \( f(0) = (0)^3 + 2(0) - 5 = -5 < 0 \)
  • \( f(2) = (2)^3 + 2(2) - 5 = 8 + 4 - 5 = 7 > 0 \)

Since \( f(x) \) is continuous on the interval \( [0, 2] \) and \( f(0) < 0 < f(2) \), by the Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT), there exists at least one real number \( c \in (0, 2) \) such that \( f(c) = 0 \).

Step 2: Uniqueness of the root (Strict Monotonicity)

Differentiating \( f(x) \) with respect to \( x \):

\[ f'(x) = 3x^2 + 2 \]

Since \( x^2 \ge 0 \) for all real numbers \( x \), we have \( 3x^2 + 2 \ge 2 > 0 \) for all \( x \in \mathbb{R} \).

Because \( f'(x) > 0 \) everywhere, \( f(x) \) is a strictly increasing function on \( (-\infty, \infty) \). A strictly increasing function is one-to-one (injective) and can assume the value \( 0 \) at most once.

Conclusion:
Since \( f(x) \) assumes the value \( 0 \) at least once (by IVT) and at most once (by strict monotonicity), the equation \( x^3 + 2x - 5 = 0 \) has exactly one real root. \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 22: Unique Real Root of a Cubic Polynomial

Prove that the equation \( x^3 + px + q = 0 \) has exactly one real root if \( p > 0 \).

Hint:
1. Differentiate \( f(x) = x^3 + px + q \) and use \( p > 0 \) to show that \( f(x) \) is strictly increasing (at most one root).
2. Examine the limits \( \lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) \) and \( \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) \), then apply the Intermediate Value Theorem to guarantee at least one root.
Proof:

Let \( f(x) = x^3 + px + q \). Since \( f(x) \) is a polynomial, it is continuous and differentiable everywhere on \( \mathbb{R} \).

Step 1: At most one root (Strict Monotonicity)

Differentiating \( f(x) \) with respect to \( x \):

\[ f'(x) = 3x^2 + p \]

Since \( x^2 \ge 0 \) for all \( x \in \mathbb{R} \) and we are given that \( p > 0 \), it follows that:

\[ f'(x) = 3x^2 + p \ge p > 0 \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathbb{R} \]

Because \( f'(x) > 0 \) everywhere, \( f(x) \) is a strictly increasing function on \( (-\infty, \infty) \). Therefore, \( f(x) \) can assume the value \( 0 \) at most once.

Step 2: At least one root (Intermediate Value Theorem)

Evaluating the end behavior of \( f(x) \) as \( x \to \pm\infty \):

\[ \lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = +\infty \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = -\infty \]

By the definition of limits, there exist real numbers \( u \) and \( v \) such that: \[ f(u) > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad f(v) < 0 \]

Since \( f(x) \) is continuous on the interval \([v, u]\) and \( f(v) < 0 < f(u) \), by the Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT), there exists at least one number \( c \in (v, u) \) such that \( f(c) = 0 \).

Conclusion:
Since \( f(x) \) assumes the value \( 0 \) at least once (by IVT) and at most once (by strict monotonicity), the equation \( x^3 + px + q = 0 \) has exactly one real root when \( p > 0 \). \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 23: Application of the Mean Value Theorem

Suppose that \( f(x) \) is differentiable everywhere, that \( f(2) = -3 \), and that \( 1 < f'(x) < 2 \) for all \( x \in (2, 5) \). Show that: \[ 0 < f(5) < 3 \]

Hint: Apply the Mean Value Theorem (MVT) to the function \( f(x) \) on the closed interval \( [2, 5] \). Express \( f(5) - f(2) \) in terms of \( f'(c) \) for some \( c \in (2, 5) \), then use the given bounds \( 1 < f'(c) < 2 \).
Proof:

Since \( f(x) \) is differentiable everywhere, it is continuous on \( [2, 5] \) and differentiable on \( (2, 5) \).

Step 1: Apply the Mean Value Theorem

By the Mean Value Theorem (MVT), there exists at least one number \( c \in (2, 5) \) such that:

\[ f'(c) = \frac{f(5) - f(2)}{5 - 2} \]

Substitute \( f(2) = -3 \) into the equation:

\[ f'(c) = \frac{f(5) - (-3)}{3} = \frac{f(5) + 3}{3} \]

Multiplying both sides by 3 gives:

\[ f(5) + 3 = 3 f'(c) \quad \text{--- (1)} \]
Step 2: Apply the Derivative Bounds

We are given that \( 1 < f'(x) < 2 \) for all \( x \in (2, 5) \). Since \( c \in (2, 5) \), it follows that:

\[ 1 < f'(c) < 2 \]

Multiply the entire inequality by 3:

\[ 3 < 3 f'(c) < 6 \]

Substitute equation (1), \( 3 f'(c) = f(5) + 3 \), into the inequality:

\[ 3 < f(5) + 3 < 6 \]
Step 3: Isolate \( f(5) \)

Subtract 3 from all parts of the inequality:

\[ 3 - 3 < f(5) < 6 - 3 \]
\[ 0 < f(5) < 3 \]
Conclusion:
By the Mean Value Theorem and the given bounds on \( f'(x) \), we have established that: \[ 0 < f(5) < 3 \] \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 24: Mean Value Theorem for a Quadratic Function

For a quadratic function \( f(x) = Ax^2 + Bx + C \), with \( A \ne 0 \), on an interval \( [a, b] \), find the number \( c \in (a, b) \) determined by the Mean Value Theorem.

Hint:
Calculate the derivative \( f'(x) \) and the average rate of change \( \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} \). Set them equal according to the Mean Value Theorem equation \( f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} \) and solve for \( c \).
Solution:

Since \( f(x) = Ax^2 + Bx + C \) is a polynomial, it is continuous on \( [a, b] \) and differentiable on \( (a, b) \). By the Mean Value Theorem (MVT), there exists a number \( c \in (a, b) \) such that:

\[ f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} \quad \text{--- (1)} \]
Step 1: Compute the Derivative

Differentiating \( f(x) \) gives:

\[ f'(x) = 2Ax + B \implies f'(c) = 2Ac + B \]
Step 2: Calculate the Average Rate of Change

Evaluating the difference \( f(b) - f(a) \):

\[ \begin{aligned} f(b) - f(a) &= (Ab^2 + Bb + C) - (Aa^2 + Ba + C) \\ &= A(b^2 - a^2) + B(b - a) \\ &= A(b - a)(b + a) + B(b - a) \\ &= (b - a) \left[ A(b + a) + B \right] \end{aligned} \]

Dividing by \( b - a \) (since \( a \ne b \)):

\[ \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} = A(b + a) + B \]
Step 3: Equate and Solve for \( c \)

Setting the derivative equal to the average rate of change:

\[ 2Ac + B = A(b + a) + B \]

Subtract \( B \) from both sides:

\[ 2Ac = A(b + a) \]

Since \( A \ne 0 \), divide both sides by \( 2A \):

\[ c = \frac{a + b}{2} \]
Conclusion:
For any quadratic function on an interval \( [a, b] \), the value \( c \) guaranteed by the Mean Value Theorem is always the midpoint of the interval: \[ c = \frac{a + b}{2} \] \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 25: Bounds on Constant for Three Distinct Real Roots

If \( f(x) = x^3 + 3x^2 + k \) has three distinct real roots, what are the bounds on \( k \)?

Hint:
1. Find the critical points of \( f(x) \) by solving \( f'(x) = 0 \).
2. For a cubic polynomial to cross the x-axis three times, its local maximum must be strictly positive (\( f(x_{\text{max}}) > 0 \)) and its local minimum must be strictly negative (\( f(x_{\text{min}}) < 0 \)).
Solution:

Let \( f(x) = x^3 + 3x^2 + k \). Since \( f(x) \) is a cubic polynomial with a positive leading coefficient, it has the end behavior \( \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = -\infty \) and \( \lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = \infty \).

Step 1: Find Critical Points

Take the derivative of \( f(x) \) and set it to zero:

\[ f'(x) = 3x^2 + 6x = 3x(x + 2) = 0 \]

The critical points are \( x = -2 \) and \( x = 0 \).

Step 2: Evaluate Local Extrema

Using the Second Derivative Test, \( f''(x) = 6x + 6 \):

  • At \( x = -2 \): \( f''(-2) = -6 < 0 \implies \) Local Maximum at \( (-2, f(-2)) \)
  • At \( x = 0 \): \( f''(0) = 6 > 0 \implies \) Local Minimum at \( (0, f(0)) \)

Evaluating the function at these critical values:

\[ f(-2) = (-2)^3 + 3(-2)^2 + k = -8 + 12 + k = k + 4 \] \[ f(0) = (0)^3 + 3(0)^2 + k = k \]
Step 3: Apply Condition for Three Distinct Roots

A continuous cubic polynomial has 3 distinct real roots if and only if its local maximum is strictly above the x-axis and its local minimum is strictly below the x-axis:

\[ f(-2) > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad f(0) < 0 \]

Substituting the values of local extrema:

  1. \( k + 4 > 0 \implies k > -4 \)
  2. \( k < 0 \)

Combining these two inequalities gives:

\[ -4 < k < 0 \]
Conclusion:
The bounds on \( k \) for \( f(x) = x^3 + 3x^2 + k \) to have three distinct real roots are: \[ -4 < k < 0 \quad \text{or} \quad k \in (-4, 0) \] \(\blacksquare\)
