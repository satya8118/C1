SAQ 1: Inequalities and Real Line Plots

Let \( A = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x^2 < x\} \). Find the set \( A \) and plot it on the real number line.

Hint: Move all terms to one side: \( x^2 - x < 0 \implies x(x - 1) < 0 \). Test the intervals determined by the roots \( x = 0 \) and \( x = 1 \).
Solution:

Step 1: Algebraic Solution

We are given the inequality \( x^2 < x \). Subtracting \( x \) from both sides:

\[ x^2 - x < 0 \implies x(x - 1) < 0 \]

The roots of the expression \( x(x - 1) \) are \( x = 0 \) and \( x = 1 \). These divide the real number line into three intervals: \( (-\infty, 0) \), \( (0, 1) \), and \( (1, \infty) \).

Interval Test Point Sign of \( x(x - 1) \) \( x^2 < x \)?
\( (-\infty, 0) \) \( x = -1 \) \( (-1)(-2) = 2 > 0 \) False
\( (0, 1) \) \( x = 0.5 \) \( (0.5)(-0.5) = -0.25 < 0 \) True
\( (1, \infty) \) \( x = 2 \) \( (2)(1) = 2 > 0 \) False

Thus, the set \( A \) is the open interval:

\[ A = (0, 1) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : 0 < x < 1\} \]

Step 2: Real Number Line Plot

Conclusion:
The set is \( A = (0, 1) \). Open circles at \( x = 0 \) and \( x = 1 \) indicate that boundary points are excluded. \(\blacksquare\)

\[ \{-1, 1\} = \{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x^2 = 1\} \]

Solving $x^2 = 1$ over $\mathbb{R}$ yields exactly $x = 1$ and $x = -1$.

\[ \mathbb{Z} = \{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid \sin(\pi x) = 0\} \]

Conclusion:
All three sets are expressed as explicit sub-collections of real numbers defined by specific equations. \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 4: Injectivity and Monotonicity of Continuous Functions

Let \( I \subseteq \mathbb{R} \) be an interval and \( f: I \to \mathbb{R} \) be a continuous function. Prove that \( f \) is injective if and only if \( f \) is strictly monotone.

Hint:
• (\(\Leftarrow\)) Direction: Strict monotonicity directly implies distinct inputs give distinct outputs.
• (\(\Rightarrow\)) Direction: Assume \( f \) is injective but not strictly monotone. Find points \( a < b < c \) in \( I \) such that \( f(b) \) fails to lie strictly between \( f(a) \) and \( f(c) \). Then apply the Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT) to arrive at a contradiction of injectivity.
Proof (Hijab's Style):

Part 1: If \( f \) is strictly monotone, then \( f \) is injective (\(\Leftarrow\))

Assume \( f \) is strictly increasing (the strictly decreasing case is analogous). Let \( x_1, x_2 \in I \) with \( x_1 \neq x_2 \). Without loss of generality, assume \( x_1 < x_2 \). By definition of strict monotonicity:

\[ x_1 < x_2 \implies f(x_1) < f(x_2) \implies f(x_1) \neq f(x_2) \]

Thus, \( f \) is injective.

Part 2: If \( f \) is continuous and injective, then \( f \) is strictly monotone (\(\Rightarrow\))

Suppose \( f \) is injective but not strictly monotone. Then there exist three points \( a, b, c \in I \) with \( a < b < c \) such that \( f(b) \) does not lie strictly between \( f(a) \) and \( f(c) \).

Since \( f \) is injective, \( f(a) \neq f(b) \neq f(c) \). Thus, exactly one of two non-monotone configurations occurs:

  • Case 1: \( f(b) > f(a) \) and \( f(b) > f(c) \)
  • Case 2: \( f(b) < f(a) \) and \( f(b) < f(c) \)

Consider Case 1 (Case 2 follows by a symmetric argument):
Choose a real number \( y \) such that:

\[ \max\{f(a), f(c)\} < y < f(b) \]

1. Since \( f \) is continuous on \([a, b]\) and \( f(a) < y < f(b) \), by the Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT), there exists \( x_1 \in (a, b) \) such that \( f(x_1) = y \).
2. Similarly, since \( f \) is continuous on \([b, c]\) and \( f(c) < y < f(b) \), by the IVT, there exists \( x_2 \in (b, c) \) such that \( f(x_2) = y \).

Because \( x_1 \in (a, b) \) and \( x_2 \in (b, c) \), we have \( x_1 < b < x_2 \implies x_1 \neq x_2 \). However, \( f(x_1) = y = f(x_2) \), which directly contradicts the injectivity of \( f \).

Conclusion:
The assumption that \( f \) is not strictly monotone leads to a contradiction. Hence, any continuous injective function on an interval must be strictly monotone. \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 5: Interlacing Zeros via Rolle's Theorem / Wronskian

Suppose that \( f, g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} \) both have continuous derivatives and the determinant

\[ \phi(x) = \begin{bmatrix} f(x) & g(x) \\ f'(x) & g'(x) \end{bmatrix} = f(x)g'(x) - f'(x)g(x) \]

is never zero. Show that between any two zeros of \( f \), there must be a zero of \( g \).

Hint:
• Let \( a < b \) be two consecutive zeros of \( f \), so \( f(a) = f(b) = 0 \).
• Assume for contradiction that \( g \) has no zero in \( (a, b) \). Then the quotient \( h(x) = \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \) is well-defined and continuous on \([a, b]\) and differentiable on \((a, b)\).
• Calculate \( h'(x) \) and apply Rolle's Theorem to \( h(x) \).
Proof:

Let \( a, b \in \mathbb{R} \) with \( a < b \) be two distinct zeros of \( f \), so that \( f(a) = 0 \) and \( f(b) = 0 \).

Assume for contradiction that \( g(x) \neq 0 \) for all \( x \in (a, b) \).

Step 1: Verify Endpoints for \( g \)

First, note that \( g(a) \neq 0 \) and \( g(b) \neq 0 \). Indeed, if \( g(a) = 0 \), then:

\[ \phi(a) = f(a)g'(a) - f'(a)g(a) = (0)g'(a) - f'(a)(0) = 0 \]

which contradicts the condition that \( \phi(x) \neq 0 \) everywhere. Thus, \( g(a) \neq 0 \) and similarly \( g(b) \neq 0 \). Hence, \( g(x) \neq 0 \) for all \( x \in [a, b] \).

Step 2: Define Auxiliary Quotient Function

Since \( g(x) \neq 0 \) on \([a, b]\), define the quotient function \( h: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R} \) by:

\[ h(x) = \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \]

1. \( h \) is continuous on \([a, b]\) and differentiable on \((a, b)\).
2. \( h(a) = \frac{f(a)}{g(a)} = 0 \) and \( h(b) = \frac{f(b)}{g(b)} = 0 \).

Step 3: Apply Rolle's Theorem

By Rolle's Theorem, there exists some \( c \in (a, b) \) such that \( h'(c) = 0 \). Differentiating \( h(x) \) using the quotient rule:

\[ h'(x) = \frac{f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)}{[g(x)]^2} = -\frac{\phi(x)}{[g(x)]^2} \]

Setting \( h'(c) = 0 \) implies:

\[ -\frac{\phi(c)}{[g(c)]^2} = 0 \implies \phi(c) = 0 \]

This directly contradicts the given hypothesis that \( \phi(x) \neq 0 \) for all \( x \in \mathbb{R} \).

Conclusion:
Our assumption was false; therefore, \( g \) must have at least one zero in \( (a, b) \) between any two zeros of \( f \). \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 7: Cauchy's Exponential Functional Equation

Let \( f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} \) be a continuous function such that \( f(1) = a \) (where \( a > 0 \)). If \( f(x + y) = f(x)f(y) \) for all \( x, y \in \mathbb{R} \), find \( f(x) \).

Hint:
• Determine \( f(n) \) for positive integers \( n \in \mathbb{N} \) inductively.
• Extend the result to all rational numbers \( q \in \mathbb{Q} \) using \( f\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)^n = f(m) \).
• Use the continuity of \( f \) on \( \mathbb{R} \) to extend the solution from \( \mathbb{Q} \) to all real numbers \( x \in \mathbb{R} \).
Solution:

Step 1: Positive Integers \( n \in \mathbb{N} \)

By repeated application of \( f(x + y) = f(x)f(y) \):

\[ f(n) = f(1 + 1 + \dots + 1) = [f(1)]^n = a^n \]

Step 2: Zero and Negative Integers

• Setting \( x = y = 0 \): \( f(0) = f(0)^2 \implies f(0) = 1 \) (since \( f(1) = a > 0 \implies f \not\equiv 0 \)).
• Setting \( y = -x \): \( f(x)f(-x) = f(0) = 1 \implies f(-x) = \frac{1}{f(x)} \).
Thus, for any integer \( k \in \mathbb{Z} \), \( f(k) = a^k \).

Step 3: Rational Numbers \( q = \frac{m}{n} \in \mathbb{Q} \)

For any positive integer \( n \):

\[ f(1) = f\left(\underbrace{\frac{1}{n} + \dots + \frac{1}{n}}_{n \text{ times}}\right) = \left[f\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right]^n = a \implies f\left(\frac{1}{n}\right) = a^{1/n} \]

Hence, for any rational number \( q = \frac{m}{n} \in \mathbb{Q} \):

\[ f\left(\frac{m}{n}\right) = \left[f\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right]^m = (a^{1/n})^m = a^{m/n} \implies f(q) = a^q \]

Step 4: Extension to Real Numbers \( x \in \mathbb{R} \) by Continuity

Since the rationals \( \mathbb{Q} \) are dense in \( \mathbb{R} \), for any real number \( x \in \mathbb{R} \), there exists a sequence of rational numbers \( (q_n) \subset \mathbb{Q} \) such that \( \lim_{n \to \infty} q_n = x \).

By the continuity of \( f \):

\[ f(x) = f\left(\lim_{n \to \infty} q_n\right) = \lim_{n \to \infty} f(q_n) = \lim_{n \to \infty} a^{q_n} = a^x \]
Conclusion:
The unique continuous solution to the functional equation is: \[ f(x) = a^x \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathbb{R} \quad \blacksquare \]

SAQ 9: Optimization — Maximizing Trapezoid Area

Solution & Derivation:

(a) Diagram & Setup

(b) Formulate Area Function

Let the fourth side be divided into three segments: \( x \), \( 1 \), and \( x \). So the bottom base length is \( b = 1 + 2x \) for \( 0 < x < 1 \).
By the Pythagorean theorem, height \( h = \sqrt{1 - x^2} \).

The area \( A(x) \) of the trapezoid is:

\[ A(x) = \frac{(1 + 2x) + 1}{2} \cdot h = (1 + x)\sqrt{1 - x^2} \]

(c) Optimize Area Function

Taking the derivative with respect to \( x \) using the product rule:

\[ \begin{aligned} A'(x) &= (1)\sqrt{1 - x^2} + (1 + x)\cdot \frac{-x}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} \\ &= \frac{(1 - x^2) - x(1 + x)}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} = \frac{1 - x - 2x^2}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} \end{aligned} \]

\[ 2x^2 + x - 1 = 0 \implies (2x - 1)(x + 1) = 0 \]

SAQ 10: Tangent Line Equation & Linear Approximation

Let \( f(x) = \frac{2x}{x^2 + 3} \).

  1. Write an equation of the tangent line to the curve \( y = f(x) \) at \( x = 1 \).
  2. Use linear approximation to give an approximate value for \( f(1.2) \).
Hint:
• Evaluate \( f(1) \) to find the point of tangency \( (1, f(1)) \).
• Find \( f'(x) \) using the Quotient Rule and evaluate \( f'(1) \) to get the slope \( m \).
• Form the linear approximation equation \( L(x) = f(1) + f'(1)(x - 1) \) and substitute \( x = 1.2 \).
Solution:

(a) Equation of the Tangent Line at \( x = 1 \)

1. Find the point of tangency:

\[ f(1) = \frac{2(1)}{1^2 + 3} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2} \]

So the point of tangency is \( \left(1, \frac{1}{2}\right) \).

2. Find the derivative \( f'(x) \) using the Quotient Rule:

\[ f'(x) = \frac{2(x^2 + 3) - 2x(2x)}{(x^2 + 3)^2} = \frac{2x^2 + 6 - 4x^2}{(x^2 + 3)^2} = \frac{6 - 2x^2}{(x^2 + 3)^2} \]

3. Find the slope at \( x = 1 \):

\[ m = f'(1) = \frac{6 - 2(1)^2}{(1^2 + 3)^2} = \frac{4}{16} = \frac{1}{4} \]

4. Write the point-slope form equation:

\[ y - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}(x - 1) \implies y = \frac{1}{4}x + \frac{1}{4} \]

(b) Linear Approximation for \( f(1.2) \)

The linear approximation formula \( L(x) \) at \( a = 1 \) is given by the tangent line equation:

\[ L(x) = f(1) + f'(1)(x - 1) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4}(x - 1) \]

Substituting \( x = 1.2 \):

\[ f(1.2) \approx L(1.2) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4}(1.2 - 1) = 0.5 + 0.25(0.2) = 0.5 + 0.05 = 0.55 \]
Conclusion:
a) Tangent line equation: \( y = \frac{1}{4}x + \frac{1}{4} \) (or \( x - 4y + 1 = 0 \))
b) Linear approximation: \( f(1.2) \approx 0.55 \) \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 11: Limit Involving Derivatives and Algebraic Conjugates

Suppose that \( f'(2) = 3 \). Find the limit: \[ \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{f(x) - f(2)}{\sqrt{x} - \sqrt{2}} \]

Hint:
• Multiply the top and bottom by the conjugate \( \sqrt{x} + \sqrt{2} \).
• Recognize the definition of the derivative: \( f'(2) = \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{f(x) - f(2)}{x - 2} \).
Solution:

Multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate \( (\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{2}) \):

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{f(x) - f(2)}{\sqrt{x} - \sqrt{2}} &= \lim_{x \to 2} \left[ \frac{f(x) - f(2)}{\sqrt{x} - \sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{2}} \right] \\ &= \lim_{x \to 2} \left[ \frac{f(x) - f(2)}{x - 2} \cdot (\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{2}) \right] \end{aligned} \]

Apply the product rule for limits:

\[ = \left( \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{f(x) - f(2)}{x - 2} \right) \cdot \left( \lim_{x \to 2} (\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{2}) \right) \]

Since \( \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{f(x) - f(2)}{x - 2} = f'(2) = 3 \) and \( \lim_{x \to 2} (\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{2}) = 2\sqrt{2} \):

\[ = 3 \cdot 2\sqrt{2} = 6\sqrt{2} \]
Conclusion:
The value of the limit is \( 6\sqrt{2} \). \(\blacksquare\)

\[ (x - h)^2 + y^2 = r^2 \]

Step 2 (Eliminate \( y \) & Apply Double Root): Substitute the curve's equation \( y = f(x) \) into the circle equation to obtain an algebraic equation in \( x \). Because the circle touches (is tangent to) the curve at \( P(x_0, y_0) \), \( x = x_0 \) must be a double root. Express the polynomial as having factor \( (x - x_0)^2 = 0 \) to determine \( h \) and \( r \).

Step 3 (Construct Tangent Line): The line connecting the center \( (h, 0) \) and point \( P(x_0, y_0) \) is the normal line with slope \( m_{\text{norm}} = \frac{y_0 - 0}{x_0 - h} \). The tangent line is perpendicular to the normal line, with slope:

\[ m_{\text{tan}} = -\frac{1}{m_{\text{norm}}} = -\frac{x_0 - h}{y_0} \]

(b) Worked Example: Tangent to \( y = x^2 \) at \( P(1, 1) \)

1. Circle Equation: For a circle centered at \( (h, 0) \):

\[ (x - h)^2 + y^2 = r^2 \]

Since \( P(1, 1) \) lies on the circle: \( (1 - h)^2 + 1^2 = r^2 \implies r^2 = (1 - h)^2 + 1 \).

2. Substitute Parabola Equation \( y = x^2 \):

\[ \begin{aligned} (x - h)^2 + (x^2)^2 &= (1 - h)^2 + 1 \\ x^2 - 2hx + h^2 + x^4 &= 1 - 2h + h^2 + 1 \\ x^4 + x^2 - 2hx + (2h - 2) &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

3. Enforce Double Root at \( x = 1 \):
Since \( x = 1 \) is an intersection point, \( (x - 1) \) is a factor. Let us factor out \( (x - 1) \):

\[ \begin{aligned} x^4 + x^2 - 2hx + (2h - 2) &= (x - 1)(x^3 + x^2 + 2x + (2h - 2)) = 0 \end{aligned} \]

For \( x = 1 \) to be a double root, the quotient polynomial \( Q(x) = x^3 + x^2 + 2x + (2h - 2) \) must also evaluate to zero at \( x = 1 \):

\[ Q(1) = 1^3 + 1^2 + 2(1) + (2h - 2) = 0 \implies 4 + 2h - 2 = 0 \implies 2h = -2 \implies h = -\frac{5}{2} \text{ (Wait, let's re-verify: } 1 + 1 + 2 + 2h - 2 = 0 \implies 2h + 2 = 0 \implies h = -1) \]
\[ 1 + 1 + 2 + 2h - 2 = 0 \implies 2h + 2 = 0 \implies h = -1 \]

So the center of the normal circle is \( (h, 0) = (-1, 0) \).

4. Determine Slopes and Tangent Line:
• Slope of Normal Line through \( (-1, 0) \) and \( P(1, 1) \):

\[ m_{\text{norm}} = \frac{1 - 0}{1 - (-1)} = \frac{1}{2} \]

• Slope of Tangent Line:

\[ m_{\text{tan}} = -\frac{1}{m_{\text{norm}}} = -2 \quad \text{Wait, checking curve orientation:} \]

Let's re-check \( m = \frac{y_0 - 0}{x_0 - h} = \frac{1 - 0}{1 - (-1)} = \frac{1}{2} \). Since the normal goes down to the left, normal slope is \( \frac{1}{2} \), making tangent slope \( -2 \)? No, for \( y = x^2 \) at \( x = 1 \), \( y' = 2x = 2 \). Let's re-verify the quotient polynomial division:

\[ \begin{aligned} (x^4 + x^2 - 2hx + 2h - 2) \div (x - 1): \\ x^4 - 1 &= (x-1)(x^3 + x^2 + x + 1) \\ (x^2 - 1) &= (x-1)(x+1) \\ -2h(x - 1) &= (x-1)(-2h) \\ \text{Sum} &= (x-1)(x^3 + x^2 + x + 1 + x + 1 - 2h) = (x-1)(x^3 + x^2 + 2x + 2 - 2h) \end{aligned} \]

Evaluating at \( x = 1 \):

\[ 1^3 + 1^2 + 2(1) + 2 - 2h = 0 \implies 6 - 2h = 0 \implies h = 3 \]

So center of circle is \( (h, 0) = (3, 0) \).

• Slope of Normal Line through \( (3, 0) \) and \( P(1, 1) \):

\[ m_{\text{norm}} = \frac{1 - 0}{1 - 3} = -\frac{1}{2} \]

• Slope of Tangent Line:

\[ m_{\text{tan}} = -\frac{1}{m_{\text{norm}}} = -\frac{1}{-1/2} = 2 \]

5. Equation of Tangent Line:

\[ y - 1 = 2(x - 1) \implies y = 2x - 1 \]
Conclusion:
Descartes' Circle Method finds the normal center \( (3, 0) \), giving a normal slope of \( -\frac{1}{2} \). The tangent line perpendicular to it at \( P(1, 1) \) is \( y = 2x - 1 \). \(\blacksquare\)

  • \( f(0) = \sin(0) + \cos(0) = 0 + 1 = 1 \)

\[ L(x) = f(0) + f'(0)x \]

SAQ 23: Riemann Sums & Estimation of \(\pi\)

Let \( f(t) = \frac{1}{1+t^2} \), for \( -1 \le t \le 1 \).

  1. Find \( L(P, f) \) (Lower Riemann sum) and \( U(P, f) \) (Upper Riemann sum) for:
    1. \( P = \{-1, 0, 1\} \)
    2. \( P = \left\{-1, -\frac{1}{2}, 0, \frac{1}{2}, 1\right\} \)
  2. Hence show that \( \frac{13}{5} \le \pi \le \frac{18}{5} \).
Hint:
• \( f(t) \) is symmetric, increases on \( [-1, 0] \), reaches a maximum at \( t = 0 \) (\( f(0) = 1 \)), and decreases on \( [0, 1] \).
• Recall that \( \int_{-1}^1 \frac{1}{1+t^2} \, dt = \left[ \arctan(t) \right]_{-1}^1 = \frac{\pi}{4} - \left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\pi}{2} \).
• Therefore, \( 2 \cdot L(P_2, f) \le \pi \le 2 \cdot U(P_2, f) \).
Solution & Bounds Derivation:

(a) Function Properties

• \( f(-1) = f(1) = \frac{1}{2} \)
• \( f\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = f\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{1 + 1/4} = \frac{4}{5} \)
• \( f(0) = 1 \) (maximum point)

1) Partitions, Lower, and Upper Sums

a) Partition \( P_1 = \{-1, 0, 1\} \):
Subintervals: \( [-1, 0] \) and \( [0, 1] \), each of width \( \Delta t = 1 \).

  • \( L(P_1, f) = f(-1) \cdot 1 + f(1) \cdot 1 = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = 1 \)
  • \( U(P_1, f) = f(0) \cdot 1 + f(0) \cdot 1 = 1 + 1 = 2 \)

b) Partition \( P_2 = \left\{-1, -\frac{1}{2}, 0, \frac{1}{2}, 1\right\} \):
Subintervals: \( \left[-1, -\frac{1}{2}\right] \), \( \left[-\frac{1}{2}, 0\right] \), \( \left[0, \frac{1}{2}\right] \), \( \left[\frac{1}{2}, 1\right] \), each of width \( \Delta t = \frac{1}{2} \).

  • \( L(P_2, f) = \frac{1}{2} \left[ f(-1) + f\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) + f\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) + f(1) \right] \) \[ L(P_2, f) = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{1}{2} + \frac{4}{5} + \frac{4}{5} + \frac{1}{2} \right) = \frac{1}{2} \left( 1 + \frac{8}{5} \right) = \frac{13}{10} \]
  • \( U(P_2, f) = \frac{1}{2} \left[ f\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) + f(0) + f(0) + f\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) \right] \) \[ U(P_2, f) = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{4}{5} + 1 + 1 + \frac{4}{5} \right) = \frac{1}{2} \left( 2 + \frac{8}{5} \right) = \frac{18}{10} = \frac{9}{5} \]

2) Proving \( \frac{13}{5} \le \pi \le \frac{18}{5} \)

By definition of the Riemann integral:

\[ L(P_2, f) \le \int_{-1}^1 \frac{1}{1+t^2} \, dt \le U(P_2, f) \]

Evaluating the exact integral:

\[ \int_{-1}^1 \frac{1}{1+t^2} \, dt = \left[ \arctan(t) \right]_{-1}^1 = \arctan(1) - \arctan(-1) = \frac{\pi}{4} - \left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\pi}{2} \]

Substituting \( L(P_2, f) = \frac{13}{10} \), \( U(P_2, f) = \frac{18}{10} \), and the integral value \( \frac{\pi}{2} \):

\[ \frac{13}{10} \le \frac{\pi}{2} \le \frac{18}{10} \]

Multiplying the inequality by \( 2 \):

\[ \frac{13}{5} \le \pi \le \frac{18}{5} \quad (2.6 \le \pi \le 3.6) \]
Conclusion:
• \( L(P_1, f) = 1 \), \( U(P_1, f) = 2 \)
• \( L(P_2, f) = \frac{13}{10} \), \( U(P_2, f) = \frac{9}{5} \)
• Bounding the integral \( \int_{-1}^1 \frac{1}{1+t^2} dt = \frac{\pi}{2} \) confirms \( \frac{13}{5} \le \pi \le \frac{18}{5} \). \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 24: Curvature, Osculating Circle, and Radius of Curvature

How do we quantify how sharply a curve bends at a given point? Explore the geometric concept of the circle of curvature (osculating circle) and radius of curvature through the following questions.

  1. Q1 (Osculating Circle): Let \( y = f(x) \) be a twice-differentiable function and \( P(a, f(a)) \) be a point on the curve. What three geometric conditions uniquely define the "circle of curvature" at \( P \)?
  2. Q2 (Curvature & Radius Formula): How are the radius of curvature \( R \) and curvature \( \kappa \) mathematically derived from these conditions?
Hint:
• The circle of curvature best fits the curve at \( P \) by matching the function value \( f(a) \), first derivative \( f'(a) \) (slope), and second derivative \( f''(a) \) (concavity).
• Radius of curvature formula: \( R = \frac{(1 + [f'(x)]^2)^{3/2}}{|f''(x)|} \).
• Curvature is the reciprocal of the radius of curvature: \( \kappa = \frac{1}{R} \).
Expository Q&A & Geometric Analysis:

Q1: Three Defining Conditions for the Circle of Curvature

Among all possible circles in the plane, the circle of curvature (or osculating circle) at point \( P(a, f(a)) \) is chosen as the unique circle that satisfies:

  1. Position Match: The circle passes directly through the point \( P(a, f(a)) \).
  2. Slope Match (1st Derivative): At \( P \), the circle has the exact same slope (tangent line) as the curve \( y = f(x) \), meaning \( y_{\text{circle}}'(a) = f'(a) \).
  3. Concavity Match (2nd Derivative): At \( P \), the circle has the exact same concavity (second derivative) as the curve \( y = f(x) \), meaning \( y_{\text{circle}}''(a) = f''(a) \).

Q2: Mathematical Formulas for Radius & Curvature

Matching the first two derivatives yields the explicit formula for the radius of curvature \( R \):

\[ R = \frac{\left(1 + [f'(x)]^2\right)^{3/2}}{|f''(x)|} \]

The curvature \( \kappa \) measures the rate at which the tangent direction turns per unit arc length and is defined as the reciprocal of the radius of curvature:

\[ \kappa = \frac{1}{R} = \frac{|f''(x)|}{\left(1 + [f'(x)]^2\right)^{3/2}} \]
Summary:
The circle of curvature uniquely shares position \( f(a) \), slope \( f'(a) \), and concavity \( f''(a) \) with the curve at point \( P \). Its radius \( R = \frac{(1 + [f']^2)^{3/2}}{|f''|} \) defines the intrinsic radius of curvature. \(\blacksquare\)

SAQ 25: Absolute Maximum of \( f(x) = x^a (1-x)^b \)

If \( a \) and \( b \) are positive real numbers, find the maximum value of \[ f(x) = x^a (1-x)^b \quad \text{for } 0 \le x \le 1 \]

Hint:
• Evaluate \( f(x) \) at the endpoints \( x = 0 \) and \( x = 1 \).
• Differentiate \( f(x) \) using the Product Rule and Chain Rule, set \( f'(x) = 0 \), and solve for the critical point in \( (0, 1) \).
• Alternatively, maximize \( \ln f(x) = a \ln x + b \ln(1-x) \) via logarithmic differentiation.
Solution & Derivation:

Step 1: Endpoint Evaluation

Since \( a, b > 0 \):

\[ \begin{aligned} f(0) &= 0^a (1 - 0)^b = 0 \\ f(1) &= 1^a (1 - 1)^b = 0 \end{aligned} \]

Since \( f(x) > 0 \) for all \( x \in (0, 1) \), the absolute maximum must occur at an interior critical point.

Step 2: Find Critical Points via Differentiation

Differentiate \( f(x) \) with respect to \( x \) using the Product and Chain Rules:

\[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= a x^{a-1} (1-x)^b + x^a \cdot b (1-x)^{b-1} (-1) \\ &= x^{a-1} (1-x)^{b-1} \left[ a(1-x) - bx \right] \\ &= x^{a-1} (1-x)^{b-1} \left[ a - (a+b)x \right] \end{aligned} \]

Setting \( f'(x) = 0 \) for \( x \in (0, 1) \):

\[ a - (a+b)x = 0 \implies x = \frac{a}{a+b} \]

Step 3: Evaluate Maximum Value

Substitute the critical point \( x = \frac{a}{a+b} \) back into \( f(x) \):

\[ \begin{aligned} 1 - x &= 1 - \frac{a}{a+b} = \frac{b}{a+b} \\ f\left(\frac{a}{a+b}\right) &= \left( \frac{a}{a+b} \right)^a \left( \frac{b}{a+b} \right)^b = \frac{a^a b^b}{(a+b)^{a+b}} \end{aligned} \]
Conclusion:
The maximum value occurs at \( x = \frac{a}{a+b} \) and is given by \[ f_{\text{max}} = \frac{a^a b^b}{(a+b)^{a+b}} \quad \blacksquare \]

SAQ 27: Rolle's Theorem & Real Roots of Polynomial Derivatives

Let \( p(x) \in \mathbb{R}[x] \) be a polynomial of degree \( n \) with \( n \) real roots (counting multiplicities). Show that all roots of its derivative \( p'(x) \) are also real.

Hint:
• Express \( p(x) = c(x - r_1)^{e_1}(x - r_2)^{e_2} \cdots (x - r_k)^{e_k} \), where \( e_1 + e_2 + \dots + e_k = n \).
• Each root \( r_i \) with multiplicity \( e_i > 1 \) gives a root of \( p'(x) \) with multiplicity \( e_i - 1 \).
• Apply Rolle's Theorem between adjacent distinct roots \( r_i \) and \( r_{i+1} \) to find additional distinct real roots of \( p'(x) \).
Proof & Complete Derivation:

Step 1: Factorization and Multiplicity Analysis

Let \( r_1 < r_2 < \dots < r_k \) be the distinct real roots of \( p(x) \) with multiplicities \( e_1, e_2, \dots, e_k \) respectively, such that:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^k e_i = n \]

For each root \( r_i \), we can factor \( p(x) \) as \( p(x) = (x - r_i)^{e_i} q_i(x) \), where \( q_i(r_i) \neq 0 \). Differentiating gives:

\[ \begin{aligned} p'(x) &= e_i (x - r_i)^{e_i - 1} q_i(x) + (x - r_i)^{e_i} q_i'(x) \\ &= (x - r_i)^{e_i - 1} \Big[ e_i q_i(x) + (x - r_i) q_i'(x) \Big] \end{aligned} \]

Thus, each \( r_i \) is a root of \( p'(x) \) with multiplicity \( e_i - 1 \). Summing these multiplicities across all \( k \) distinct roots yields:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^k (e_i - 1) = \left(\sum_{i=1}^k e_i\right) - k = n - k \quad \text{roots of } p'(x) \]

Step 2: Applying Rolle's Theorem Between Distinct Roots

Consider the \( k \) distinct roots ordered as \( r_1 < r_2 < \dots < r_k \). Since \( p(x) \) is a polynomial, it is continuous on \([r_i, r_{i+1}]\) and differentiable on \((r_i, r_{i+1})\) with \( p(r_i) = p(r_{i+1}) = 0 \).

By Rolle's Theorem, for each of the \( k - 1 \) open intervals \( (r_i, r_{i+1}) \), there exists at least one point \( c_i \in (r_i, r_{i+1}) \) such that:

\[ p'(c_i) = 0 \quad \text{for } i = 1, 2, \dots, k - 1 \]

These \( k - 1 \) roots \( c_1, c_2, \dots, c_{k-1} \) are distinct from each other and distinct from the original roots \( r_i \).

Step 3: Total Root Count Verification

The derivative \( p'(x) \) is a polynomial of degree \( n - 1 \). The total number of real roots found (counting multiplicity) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Total Real Roots} &= \underbrace{(n - k)}_{\text{from repeated roots } r_i} + \underbrace{(k - 1)}_{\text{from Rolle's Theorem in } (r_i, r_{i+1})} \\ &= n - 1 \end{aligned} \]

Since \( \deg(p') = n - 1 \) and we have accounted for exactly \( n - 1 \) real roots (with multiplicity), all roots of \( p'(x) \) must be real.

Conclusion:
By combining the multiplicity reduction at repeated roots with Rolle's Theorem applied between adjacent distinct roots, we account for all \( n - 1 \) roots of \( p'(x) \) in \( \mathbb{R} \). \(\blacksquare\)
