Rigorous Analysis: Continuity of $e^{x}$ via $\varepsilon$–$\delta$

Every step of the standard proof — plus a self-contained proof of the key lemma using only Bernoulli’s inequality, with no Taylor series in sight.

0 What we must prove

$f(x)=e^{x}$ is continuous at $c\in\mathbb{R}$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a $\delta>0$ such that

\[ |x-c| \lt \delta \;\Longrightarrow\; |e^{x}-e^{c}| \lt \varepsilon . \]

Since $c$ is arbitrary, proving this for a general $c$ proves continuity on all of $\mathbb{R}$. The whole difficulty is packed into one question: how big can $|e^{x}-e^{c}|$ be when $x$ is close to $c$?

1 Factor out the constant

Apply the exponent law $e^{a+b}=e^{a}e^{b}$ to separate the fixed point $c$ from the moving variable $x$:

\[ |e^{x}-e^{c}| \;=\; |e^{c}e^{x-c}-e^{c}| \;=\; e^{c}\,|e^{x-c}-1| . \]

Writing $h=x-c$, the problem collapses to a single quantity:

\[ \boxed{\;|e^{x}-e^{c}| = e^{c}\,|e^{h}-1|,\qquad h=x-c.\;} \]

The factor $e^{c}$ is a harmless constant. Everything now hinges on how $|e^{h}-1|$ behaves for small $h$.

2 The key lemma: a local linear bound

Because $e^{h}$ grows faster than any line, no single linear bound $|e^{h}-1|\le K|h|$ can hold for all $h$. But we do not need one — we only need a local window around $0$. Restricting to $|h|\lt 1$ is enough.

Crucial Lemma

If $|h|\lt 1$, then

\[ |e^{h}-1| \;\le\; e\,|h| . \]
Inside the unit window the exponential curve stays underneath a straight ceiling of slope $e$.
$|e^{h}-1|$ $e\,|h|$ shaded: the unit window $|h|\lt 1$

Substituting $h=x-c$ into the lemma gives the working inequality:

\[ |e^{x}-e^{c}| \;=\; e^{c}\,|e^{x-c}-1| \;\le\; e^{c}\cdot e\,|x-c| \;=\; e^{\,c+1}\,|x-c| . \]

3 Choosing $\delta$

We want the bound to land strictly below the error tolerance $\varepsilon$:

\[ e^{\,c+1}|x-c| \lt \varepsilon \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |x-c| \lt \frac{\varepsilon}{e^{\,c+1}} = \varepsilon\,e^{-(c+1)} . \]

But that is only legal if we also respect the unit window $|x-c|\lt 1$ required by the lemma. Imposing both constraints at once:

\[ \boxed{\;\delta \;=\; \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{\varepsilon}{e^{\,c+1}}\right) \;=\; \min\!\left(1,\; \varepsilon\,e^{-(c+1)}\right).\;} \]

4 Formal synthesis

Let $\varepsilon>0$ be arbitrary and set $\delta=\min\!\left(1,\ \varepsilon e^{-(c+1)}\right)$. Suppose $|x-c|\lt\delta$. Then both constraints hold simultaneously:

(i) Geometric constraint

$|x-c| \lt \delta \le 1$, so the lemma applies with $h=x-c$.

(ii) Metric constraint

$|x-c| \lt \delta \le \dfrac{\varepsilon}{e^{\,c+1}}$.

Chaining everything together:

\[ \begin{aligned} |e^{x}-e^{c}| &= e^{c}\,|e^{x-c}-1| \\[2pt] &\le e^{c}\cdot e\,|x-c| && \text{(Lemma, valid by (i))} \\[2pt] &\lt e^{\,c+1}\cdot \frac{\varepsilon}{e^{\,c+1}} = \varepsilon && \text{(by (ii))}. \end{aligned} \]
Conclusion

For every $\varepsilon>0$ the choice $\delta=\min\!\left(1,\ \varepsilon e^{-(c+1)}\right)$ satisfies the $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ condition at $c$. Hence $f(x)=e^{x}$ is continuous at $c$; since $c$ was arbitrary, $e^{x}$ is continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

A Appendix — the lemma without Taylor series

The usual derivation of $|e^{h}-1|\le e|h|$ quotes the series $e^{h}=\sum h^{n}/n!$. That is convenient but not necessary. Everything the lemma needs follows from Bernoulli’s inequality together with the limit definition of the exponential.

Tool: Bernoulli

For $u\ge -1$ and every integer $n\ge 1$,

\[ (1+u)^{n} \;\ge\; 1+nu . \]

A1 A consequence: $e^{t}\ge 1+t$

Fix $t\gt -1$ and put $u=t/n$ in Bernoulli’s inequality. Since $t\gt -1$ we have $u\ge -1$ for every $n\ge 1$, so

\[ \left(1+\frac{t}{n}\right)^{n} \;\ge\; 1+t \qquad\text{for all } n\ge 1 . \]

Using the standard limit definition $e^{t}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{t}{n}\right)^{n}$ and passing to the limit (the left side converges to $e^{t}$, the right side is constant):

\[ \boxed{\;e^{t}\;\ge\;1+t \qquad \text{for every } t\gt -1.\;} \tag{B} \]

This single inequality is the whole engine of the argument. Notice it is free of derivatives and of infinite series.

A2 The negative half: $-1\lt h\le 0$

Apply (B) directly with $t=h$ (legal because $h\gt -1$):

\[ e^{h}\;\ge\;1+h \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 0 \;\le\; 1-e^{h} \;\le\; -h \;=\; |h| \;\le\; e\,|h| . \]

Since $h\le 0$ we have $|e^{h}-1| = 1-e^{h}$, so $|e^{h}-1|\le e|h|$ in this case. Done.

A3 The positive half: $0\le h\lt 1$

Here is the trick — factor the difference by pulling out $e^{h}$:

\[ e^{h}-1 \;=\; e^{h}\left(1-e^{-h}\right). \]

Now bound the two factors separately.

Factor 1: $e^{h}\le e$

Because $h\le 1$, we have $1-h\ge 0$, so by (B) $e^{\,1-h}\ge 1+(1-h)=2-h\ge 1$. Hence $e - e^{h} = e^{h}\left(e^{\,1-h}-1\right)\ge 0$, i.e. $e^{h}\le e$. (Equivalently: $t\mapsto e^{t}$ is increasing.)

Factor 2: $1-e^{-h}\le h$

Apply (B) with $t=-h$. This is legal precisely because $h\lt 1$ gives $-h\gt -1$. We get $e^{-h}\ge 1-h$, i.e. $1-e^{-h}\le h$.

Multiplying the two bounds (both sides are non-negative):

\[ e^{h}-1 \;=\; e^{h}\left(1-e^{-h}\right) \;\le\; e\cdot h \;=\; e\,|h| . \]

So $|e^{h}-1|\le e|h|$ in this case too.

A4 Wrap-up

Combining the two cases:

\[ |h|\lt 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; |e^{h}-1|\;\le\;e\,|h| . \qquad \blacksquare \]
What was used, and what was not.
Used: Bernoulli’s inequality $(1+u)^{n}\ge 1+nu$ for $u\ge -1$, the limit definition $e^{t}=\lim_{n\to\infty}(1+t/n)^{n}$, and monotonicity of $t\mapsto e^{t}$ (itself a consequence of Bernoulli).
Not used: Taylor expansions, power series, derivatives, L’Hôpital’s rule, or the mean value theorem. The proof is elementary in the strict sense.
Why the case split is unavoidable. A tempting one-line bound is $e^{h}\le \dfrac{1}{1-h}$, obtained from Bernoulli applied to $e^{-h}$. That gives $e^{h}-1\le \dfrac{h}{1-h}$, which is only $\le eh$ while $h\le 1-\tfrac1e\approx0.632$ — it blows up as $h\to1^{-}$. The factorization $e^{h}-1=e^{h}(1-e^{-h})$ avoids this entirely by recycling the small quantity $h$ instead of $1/(1-h)$.
