Carathéodory's Criterion for Differentiation

While the standard definition of the derivative relies on the limit of a difference quotient, Constantin Carathéodory introduced an elegant equivalent condition. It shifts the focus from limits of fractions to the existence of a continuous "multiplier" function.

Carathéodory's Criterion

Let \( f: I \to \mathbb{R} \) be a function defined on an open interval \( I \), and let \( a \in I \). Then \( f \) is differentiable at \( a \) if and only if there exists a function \( h: I \to \mathbb{R} \) such that:

  • \( h \) is continuous at \( a \).
  • \( f(x) - f(a) = h(x)(x - a) \) for all \( x \in I \).

Moreover, in this case, the derivative is simply \( f'(a) = h(a) \).

Why does this work? (The Intuition)

If \( f \) is differentiable at \( a \), we can define \( h(x) = \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} \) for \( x \neq a \), and \( h(a) = f'(a) \). Since \( f \) is differentiable, \( \lim_{x \to a} h(x) = f'(a) = h(a) \), so \( h \) is continuous at \( a \).

Conversely, if such a continuous \( h \) exists, then \( \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} = h(x) \) for \( x \neq a \). Taking the limit as \( x \to a \), we get \( f'(a) = \lim_{x \to a} h(x) = h(a) \) by continuity.

Figure 1: As \( x \to a \), the secant line slope \( h(x) \) approaches the tangent line slope \( f'(a) \).

Examples

Example 1: Polynomial (Success)

Let \( f(x) = x^3 \) and \( a = 2 \). Find \( h(x) \) and \( f'(2) \).

Step 1: Compute \( f(x) - f(a) \).

\[ f(x) - f(2) = x^3 - 8 = (x - 2)(x^2 + 2x + 4) \]

Step 2: Extract \( h(x) \). We need \( f(x) - f(2) = h(x)(x - 2) \). Therefore, \( h(x) = x^2 + 2x + 4 \) for \( x \neq 2 \).

Step 3: Check continuity at \( a = 2 \). Since \( h(x) \) is a polynomial, it is continuous everywhere.

Step 4: Compute \( f'(2) \).

\[ f'(2) = h(2) = (2)^2 + 2(2) + 4 = 12 \]
Observation: Because \( f \) is a polynomial, the multiplier \( h(x) \) is also a polynomial. Continuity is automatic, so the criterion is easily satisfied.

Example 2: Absolute Value (Failure)

Let \( f(x) = |x| \) and \( a = 0 \). Try to find \( h(x) \) and \( f'(0) \).

Step 1: Compute \( f(x) - f(0) \).

\[ f(x) - f(0) = |x| \]

Step 2: Extract \( h(x) \). We need \( |x| = h(x) \cdot x \). For \( x \neq 0 \), \( h(x) = \frac{|x|}{x} \).

\[ h(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x > 0 \\ -1 & \text{if } x < 0 \end{cases} \]

Step 3: Check continuity at \( a = 0 \). As \( x \to 0^+ \), \( h(x) \to 1 \). As \( x \to 0^- \), \( h(x) \to -1 \). The limit does not exist.

Step 4: Compute \( f'(0) \). Since no continuous \( h \) exists, \( f'(0) \) does not exist.

Observation: The failure of differentiability is exactly the failure of the secant slope \( h(x) \) to have a continuous extension at \( a \). The "jump" in \( h(x) \) corresponds to the sharp corner in \( f(x) \).

Figure 2: The absolute value function has a sharp corner. The multiplier \( h(x) \) jumps from -1 to 1 at \( x=0 \).

Example 3: Oscillatory Success (Subtle)

Let \( f(x) = x^2 \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \) for \( x \neq 0 \), and \( f(0) = 0 \). Find \( h(x) \) and \( f'(0) \).

Step 1: Compute \( f(x) - f(0) \).

\[ f(x) - f(0) = x^2 \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \]

Step 2: Extract \( h(x) \). Dividing by \( x \):

\[ h(x) = x \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \]

Step 3: Check continuity at \( a = 0 \). Since \( -|x| \leq x \sin(1/x) \leq |x| \), the Squeeze Theorem gives:

\[ \lim_{x \to 0} h(x) = 0 \]

We define \( h(0) = 0 \). Thus, \( h \) is continuous at \( 0 \).

Step 4: Compute \( f'(0) \).

\[ f'(0) = h(0) = 0 \]

Figure 3: The multiplier \( h(x) = x \sin(1/x) \) oscillates infinitely but is squeezed to 0 by the envelope lines \( y = \pm x \), so \( h \) is continuous at 0.

Observation: This is a famous example. The derivative \( f'(x) \) for \( x \neq 0 \) is \( 2x \sin(1/x) - \cos(1/x) \), which has no limit as \( x \to 0 \) (it oscillates wildly). However, the Carathéodory multiplier \( h(x) \) is continuous at \( 0 \). This highlights that the criterion is about the existence of a continuous secant slope, not the continuity of the derivative itself.

Example 4: Vertical Tangent (Failure)

Let \( f(x) = x^{2/3} \) and \( a = 0 \). Try to find \( h(x) \) and \( f'(0) \).

Step 1: Compute \( f(x) - f(0) \).

\[ f(x) - f(0) = x^{2/3} \]

Step 2: Extract \( h(x) \). Dividing by \( x \):

\[ h(x) = \frac{x^{2/3}}{x} = x^{-1/3} = \frac{1}{\sqrt[3]{x}} \]

Step 3: Check continuity at \( a = 0 \). As \( x \to 0 \), \( h(x) \to \infty \). The function is unbounded near 0, so it cannot be continuous at 0.

Step 4: Compute \( f'(0) \). Since no continuous \( h \) exists, \( f'(0) \) does not exist.

Observation: The function \( f(x) = x^{2/3} \) has a vertical tangent at \( x=0 \). This corresponds to the multiplier \( h(x) \) blowing up to infinity. Carathéodory's criterion elegantly captures this failure mode.

Figure 4: A vertical tangent corresponds to the multiplier \( h(x) \) becoming unbounded as \( x \to a \).

Example 5: Oscillatory Failure

Let \( f(x) = x \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \) for \( x \neq 0 \), and \( f(0) = 0 \). Evaluate differentiability at \( a = 0 \).

Step 1: Compute \( f(x) - f(0) \).

\[ f(x) - f(0) = x \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \]

Step 2: Extract \( h(x) \). Dividing by \( x \):

\[ h(x) = \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \]

Step 3: Check continuity at \( a = 0 \). As \( x \to 0 \), \( \sin(1/x) \) oscillates infinitely often between \( -1 \) and \( 1 \). The limit does not exist, so no matter what value we assign to \( h(0) \), \( h \) cannot be continuous at \( 0 \).

Step 4: Compute \( f'(0) \). Since no continuous \( h \) exists, \( f'(0) \) does not exist.

Figure 5: The multiplier \( h(x) = \sin(1/x) \) oscillates infinitely often between \( -1 \) and \( 1 \) as \( x \to 0 \). No limit exists, so \( h \) cannot be continuous at \( 0 \).

Observation: Comparing this to Example 3 is crucial. In Example 3, multiplying by an extra factor of \( x \) "dampened" the oscillation enough to make the secant slope continuous at 0. Here, without that extra \( x \), the secant slope oscillates too wildly, and differentiability fails.

Key Takeaways

  • Algebraic Equivalence: Carathéodory's criterion provides an algebraic equivalent to the analytic limit definition of the derivative.
  • The Role of \( h(x) \): The function \( h(x) \) represents the slope of the secant line between \( a \) and \( x \). Differentiability is equivalent to this secant slope having a continuous extension at \( a \).
  • Failure Modes: Differentiability fails when \( h(x) \) cannot be made continuous at \( a \). This happens in three common ways:
    • Jump Discontinuity: (e.g., \( |x| \)) The left and right slopes differ.
    • Infinite Discontinuity: (e.g., \( x^{2/3} \)) The slope becomes unbounded (vertical tangent).
    • Oscillatory Discontinuity: (e.g., \( \sin(1/x) \)) The slope oscillates without settling on a value.
  • Chain Rule: This criterion simplifies the proof of the Chain Rule. If \( f(x) - f(a) = h(x)(x-a) \) and \( g(y) - g(b) = k(y)(y-b) \), then \( g(f(x)) - g(f(a)) = k(f(x))h(x)(x-a) \), making the multiplier for the composition simply \( k(f(x)) \cdot h(x) \).
