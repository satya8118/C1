Rigorous Mathematical Proofs

Part A: Proof of Intermediate Value Property Darboux Function

Let $f(x) = \sin(1/x)$ for $x \neq 0$ and $f(0) = C \in [-1, 1]$. We prove that $f$ has the IVP on any interval $[a, b] \subset \mathbb{R}$.

  • Case 1 ($0 < a < b$ or $a < b < 0$): $f(x)$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ as a composition of continuous functions ($x \mapsto 1/x$ and $\sin$). By classical IVT, $f$ satisfies IVP.
  • Case 2 ($a \le 0 \le b$): Assume $b > 0$. By Archimedean property, choose $n \in \mathbb{N}$ large enough such that: $$x_n^+ = \frac{1}{\frac{\pi}{2} + 2n\pi} \in (0, b), \quad x_n^- = \frac{1}{\frac{3\pi}{2} + 2n\pi} \in (0, b)$$
  • Notice $f(x_n^+) = 1$ and $f(x_n^-) = -1$. On the closed subinterval $J = [\min(x_n^+, x_n^-), \max(x_n^+, x_n^-)] \subset (0, b)$, $f$ is continuous and range is $[-1, 1]$.
  • Since any intermediate value $y^* \in [\min(f(a), f(b)), \max(f(a), f(b))] \subseteq [-1, 1]$, IVT on $J$ guarantees $c \in J \subset (a, b)$ with $f(c) = y^*$. $\blacksquare$
Part B: $\epsilon$-$\delta$ Proof of Discontinuity at $x=0$ Discontinuous

To show $f$ is NOT continuous at $x=0$, we must negate the definition: $$\exists \epsilon > 0, \, \forall \delta > 0, \, \exists x \in (-\delta, \delta) \text{ s.t. } |f(x) - f(0)| \ge \epsilon$$

  • Set $\epsilon_0 = 1$. Let $\delta > 0$ be arbitrary.
  • Choose $n \in \mathbb{N}$ large such that $x_1 = \frac{1}{\frac{\pi}{2} + 2n\pi} < \delta$ and $x_2 = \frac{1}{\frac{3\pi}{2} + 2n\pi} < \delta$.
  • Evaluating $f$: $f(x_1) = 1$ and $f(x_2) = -1$.
  • By triangle inequality: $$2 = |f(x_1) - f(x_2)| \le |f(x_1) - f(0)| + |f(x_2) - f(0)|$$
  • Hence at least one of $|f(x_1) - f(0)| \ge 1$ or $|f(x_2) - f(0)| \ge 1$ MUST hold! Thus $f$ cannot be continuous at $x=0$. $\blacksquare$

Non-Additivity of IVP: $f + g$ Counterexample

The sum of two functions possessing the Intermediate Value Property may fail drastically to satisfy IVP.

Evaluated on interval [-1, 1]

Function Definitions

$f(x)$ Definition:

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} \sin(1/x) & x \neq 0 \\ 1 & x = 0 \end{cases}$$

Satisfies IVP everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$.

$g(x)$ Definition:

$$g(x) = \begin{cases} -\sin(1/x) & x \neq 0 \\ 1 & x = 0 \end{cases}$$

Satisfies IVP everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$.

Sum $(f+g)(x)$ Behavior:

$$(f+g)(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & x \neq 0 \\ 2 & x = 0 \end{cases}$$
Why IVP Fails for $f+g$: On interval $[-1, 1]$, $(f+g)(-1) = 0$ and $(f+g)(0) = 2$. However, $(f+g)([-1, 1]) = \{0, 2\}$. All intermediate values in $(0, 2)$ (like $y = 1$) are completely missed!

Proof Rigorous Analysis: Non-Closure of Darboux Functions under Addition

1. Individual Functions Have IVP: For any interval $I = [a, b]$ containing $0$, $\sin(1/x)$ oscillates infinitely between $-1$ and $1$ on $I \setminus \{0\}$. Thus $f(I) = [-1, 1]$ and $g(I) = [-1, 1]$. Since $f(0) = 1 \in [-1, 1]$ and $g(0) = 1 \in [-1, 1]$, adding $x=0$ does not introduce any values outside $[-1, 1]$ nor create gaps. Both $f$ and $g$ map every subinterval containing $0$ onto $[-1, 1]$.

2. Sum Function Calculation: For $x \neq 0$, $f(x) + g(x) = \sin(1/x) + (-\sin(1/x)) = 0$. At $x = 0$, $f(0) + g(0) = 1 + 1 = 2$. Thus, $(f+g)(x)$ is identical to $0$ everywhere except at the origin where it jumps to $2$.

3. Breakdown of IVP: Choose interval $I = [-1, 1]$. The endpoints map to $(f+g)(-1) = 0$ and $(f+g)(0) = 2$. Pick intermediate value $y^* = 1 \in (0, 2)$. For $y^* = 1$ to satisfy IVP, there must exist $c \in [-1, 1]$ with $(f+g)(c) = 1$. But $(f+g)(x)$ only takes values $0$ and $2$. No such $c$ exists.

Conclusion: The set of Darboux (IVP) functions is NOT a vector space / module closed under addition.

Theorem & Proof Monotone + IVP $\implies$ Continuity

Theorem: Let $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be an interval and let $f: I \to \mathbb{R}$ be a monotonic function. If $f$ satisfies the Intermediate Value Property on $I$, then $f$ is continuous on $I$.

Step 1: One-Sided Limits Exist

Without loss of generality, assume $f$ is non-decreasing on $I$. At any interior point $x_0 \in I$, the left-hand limit $L = \lim_{x \to x_0^-} f(x) = \sup_{x < x_0} f(x)$ and right-hand limit $R = \lim_{x \to x_0^+} f(x) = \inf_{x > x_0} f(x)$ exist and satisfy: $$L \le f(x_0) \le R$$

Step 2: Proof by Contradiction (Assume Discontinuity)

Suppose $f$ is discontinuous at $x_0$. Then $L < R$. The open gap $(L, R) \setminus \{f(x_0)\}$ is non-empty. Choose $y^*$ in this open gap such that $y^* \neq f(x_0)$.

Step 3: Missing Value Contradiction

Choose points $a < x_0 < b$ in $I$. Because $f$ is non-decreasing:
• For all $x < x_0$: $f(x) \le L < y^*$
• For $x = x_0$: $f(x_0) \neq y^*$
• For all $x > x_0$: $f(x) \ge R > y^*$
Therefore, there exists no $c \in [a, b]$ such that $f(c) = y^*$.

Step 4: Conclusion

Since $y^*$ lies strictly between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, IVP demands the existence of $c \in (a, b)$ with $f(c) = y^*$. Contradiction! Hence $L = f(x_0) = R$, proving $f$ is continuous at $x_0$. $\blacksquare$
