1. The object of study

For a function $f$ and two distinct points $a \neq b$, the differential quotient is

$$ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. $$

It is the slope of the secant line through the two points $\bigl(a,f(a)\bigr)$ and $\bigl(b,f(b)\bigr)$. On the picture, the horizontal leg is $b-a$ and the vertical leg is $f(b)-f(a)$.

The central idea of differential calculus is the passage

$$ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} \;\xrightarrow[\;b\to a\;]{}\; f'(a). $$

The secant rotates into the tangent. The quotient is a two-point, algebraic object; its limit is the local, infinitesimal object we call the derivative.

The quotient is a symmetric linear function of $a$ and $b$. Symmetric because swapping $a$ and $b$ gives the same $a+b$; linear because it is of first degree in each variable. Geometrically, the secant slope on a parabola grows as you move the interval to the right. Only when the two points coincide do we get the tangent slope: $$ \lim_{b\to a}(a+b) = 2a = f'(a). $$

$$ \frac{b^{3}-a^{3}}{b-a} = a^{2}+ab+b^{2}. $$

The quotient is a symmetric homogeneous polynomial of degree $2$ in $a,b$. Homogeneous because if you scale both $a$ and $b$ by a factor $t$, the quotient scales by $t^{2}$ — matching the fact that $x^{3}$ itself scales by $t^{3}$ and the quotient has one fewer power. As $b\to a$, all three terms tend to $a^{2}$, and $$ \lim_{b\to a}\bigl(a^{2}+ab+b^{2}\bigr) = 3a^{2} = f'(a). $$

5. Quartic $f(x) = x^{4}$

$$ \frac{b^{4}-a^{4}}{b-a} = a^{3}+a^{2}b+ab^{2}+b^{3}. $$

As $b\to a$, the four terms each tend to $a^{3}$, so $$ \lim_{b\to a}\bigl(a^{3}+a^{2}b+ab^{2}+b^{3}\bigr) = 4a^{3} = f'(a). $$

The pattern is now unmistakable: the quotient has exactly as many terms as the degree, and in the limit each term contributes the same amount $a^{\,n-1}$.

6. General power $f(x) = x^{n}$

$$ \frac{b^{n}-a^{n}}{b-a} = a^{n-1}+a^{n-2}b+a^{n-3}b^{2}+\cdots+ab^{n-2}+b^{n-1}. $$

There are exactly $n$ terms, each homogeneous of degree $n-1$. As $b\to a$, every term tends to $a^{n-1}$, so

$$ \lim_{b\to a}\frac{b^{n}-a^{n}}{b-a} = n\,a^{n-1}. $$

This single computation gives the derivative of every monomial, and by linearity, of every polynomial. It is the algebraic heart of the power rule.

7. Reciprocal $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$

For $a,b \neq 0$,

$$ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} = \frac{\dfrac{1}{b}-\dfrac{1}{a}}{b-a} = \frac{\dfrac{a-b}{ab}}{b-a} = \frac{-(b-a)}{ab\,(b-a)} = -\frac{1}{ab}. $$

$2$$5$$3$$-\tfrac{3}{10}$$-\tfrac{1}{10}$

slope = —
Why is the slope always negative? Look at the algebraic formula: $-1/(ab)$. On the positive side, both $a,b > 0$, so $ab > 0$ and the quotient is negative. On the negative side, both $a,b < 0$, so again $ab > 0$ and the quotient is negative. Only if $a$ and $b$ straddle zero does $ab$ become negative — but then $1/x$ has a vertical asymptote between them, and the secant becomes meaningless.

Geometrically: on each branch of the hyperbola, as $x$ increases, $y$ decreases. The graph goes down, so the secant slope is always negative. The algebra $-1/(ab)$ encodes this fact perfectly.

As $b\to a$, the quotient tends to $$ \lim_{b\to a}\left(-\frac{1}{ab}\right) = -\frac{1}{a^{2}}, $$ so $(1/x)' = -1/x^{2}$.

8. Reciprocal square $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^{2}}$

$$ \frac{1/b^{2}-1/a^{2}}{b-a} = \frac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{a^{2}b^{2}(b-a)} = \frac{-(b-a)(a+b)}{a^{2}b^{2}(b-a)} = -\frac{a+b}{a^{2}b^{2}}. $$

As $b\to a$,

$$ \lim_{b\to a}\left(-\frac{a+b}{a^{2}b^{2}}\right) = -\frac{2a}{a^{4}} = -\frac{2}{a^{3}}. $$

So $(1/x^{2})' = -2/x^{3}$.

Again the quotient is negative whenever $a$ and $b$ have the same sign — the function is decreasing on each branch. The exponent rule $(x^{p})' = p\,x^{p-1}$ extends from integers to $p = -2$.

$$ \frac{\sqrt b-\sqrt a}{b-a} = \frac{\sqrt b-\sqrt a}{(\sqrt b-\sqrt a)(\sqrt b+\sqrt a)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt a+\sqrt b}. $$

The quotient is symmetric in $a,b$ and homogeneous of degree $-1/2$ — matching the exponent $1/2$. As $b\to a$, $$ \lim_{b\to a}\frac{1}{\sqrt a+\sqrt b} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt a}, $$ so $(\sqrt{x})' = 1/(2\sqrt{x})$.

10. Exponential $f(x) = e^{x}$

$$ \frac{e^{b}-e^{a}}{b-a} = e^{a}\cdot\frac{e^{\,b-a}-1}{b-a}. $$ Put $h = b-a$. Then $$ \frac{e^{b}-e^{a}}{b-a} = e^{a}\cdot\frac{e^{h}-1}{h}. $$

Using the standard limit

$$ \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{e^{h}-1}{h} = 1, $$

we obtain

$$ \lim_{b\to a}\frac{e^{b}-e^{a}}{b-a} = e^{a}. $$
slope = —
Thus $(e^{x})' = e^{x}$. The function is its own derivative — a remarkable property that characterises the exponential among all continuous functions. In the quotient, the factor $e^{a}$ carries the “location,” and the factor $(e^{h}-1)/h$ is a universal function of the gap $h$ that happens to tend to $1$.

11. Logarithm $f(x) = \ln x$, $a > 0$

$$ \frac{\ln b-\ln a}{b-a} = \frac{\ln(b/a)}{b-a}. $$ Write $b = a+h$: $$ = \frac{\ln\!\bigl(1+\tfrac{h}{a}\bigr)}{h} = \frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{\ln(1+u)}{u}, \qquad u = \frac{h}{a}. $$

Using the standard limit

$$ \lim_{u\to 0}\frac{\ln(1+u)}{u} = 1, $$

we get

$$ \lim_{b\to a}\frac{\ln b-\ln a}{b-a} = \frac{1}{a}. $$
slope = —
So $(\ln x)' = 1/x$. The reciprocal of $x$ appears again — the same expression we found in Example 7, but now as a derivative. This is a beautiful duality: the derivative of the logarithm is the reciprocal.

12. Sine $f(x) = \sin x$

Use the sum-to-product identity

$$ \sin b-\sin a = 2\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{b-a}{2}\Bigr) \cos\!\Bigl(\frac{a+b}{2}\Bigr). $$ Then $$ \frac{\sin b-\sin a}{b-a} = \cos\!\Bigl(\frac{a+b}{2}\Bigr)\cdot \frac{\sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{b-a}{2}\bigr)}{\tfrac{b-a}{2}}. $$

As $b\to a$, the fraction tends to $1$ (standard limit $\sin u/u\to 1$) and $\cos\!\bigl(\tfrac{a+b}{2}\bigr)\to\cos a$. Hence

$$ (\sin x)' = \cos x. $$
slope = —
The quotient is symmetric in $a$ and $b$ — because swapping them does not change the secant slope. The expression $\cos\!\bigl(\tfrac{a+b}{2}\bigr)$ shows that the secant slope depends only on the midpoint of the interval, not on the interval's width. As the width shrinks to zero, the midpoint tends to $a$ and the secant slope becomes $\cos a$.

13. Cosine $f(x) = \cos x$

Similarly,

$$ \cos b-\cos a = -2\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{b-a}{2}\Bigr) \sin\!\Bigl(\frac{a+b}{2}\Bigr), $$ so $$ \frac{\cos b-\cos a}{b-a} = -\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{a+b}{2}\Bigr)\cdot \frac{\sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{b-a}{2}\bigr)}{\tfrac{b-a}{2}} \;\longrightarrow\; -\sin a. $$
$$ (\cos x)' = -\sin x. $$
The minus sign is not an accident: cosine is decreasing wherever sine is positive, and vice versa. The algebra captures this through the factor $-\sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{a+b}{2}\bigr)$.

14. A cautionary example: $f(x) = |x|$

At $a > 0$: for $b$ close to $a$, $b > 0$, so

$$ \frac{|b|-|a|}{b-a} = \frac{b-a}{b-a} = 1. $$

The one-sided limit as $b\to a^{+}$ is $1$.

At $a < 0$: for $b$ close to $a$, $b < 0$, so

$$ \frac{|b|-|a|}{b-a} = \frac{-b-(-a)}{b-a} = \frac{-(b-a)}{b-a} = -1. $$

The limit is $-1$.

At $a = 0$: the quotient is

$$ \frac{|b|-|0|}{b-0} = \frac{|b|}{b} = \operatorname{sign}(b), $$

which is $1$ for $b > 0$ and $-1$ for $b < 0$. It has no limit as $b\to 0$.

slope = —

Drag the sliders to put $a$ and $b$ on either side of the corner at $x = 0$. The secant slope jumps discontinuously.

Conclusion: $|x|$ is differentiable for every $x\neq 0$, with derivative $$ (|x|)' = \begin{cases} 1, & x > 0,\\[2pt] -1, & x < 0,\end{cases} $$ but it is not differentiable at $0$. This is where the differential quotient refuses to settle down — the graph has a corner, and no tangent line exists. The differential quotient is thus a faithful detector of differentiability.

16. Why this is beautiful

A few patterns stand out.

Symmetry

For polynomials, $1/x$, $1/x^{2}$, $\sqrt{x}$, and the trigonometric functions, the differential quotient is symmetric in $a$ and $b$. That is, swapping the two points does not change the quotient. This is exactly what we should expect, because the slope of the secant is the same whether we traverse it from $a$ to $b$ or from $b$ to $a$.

Homogeneity

For $f(x) = x^{p}$, the quotient is homogeneous of degree $p-1$ in $(a,b)$. This is a fingerprint of the power function, and it passes to the limit: $f'(x) = p\,x^{p-1}$ is homogeneous of degree $p-1$.

Elementary functions give elementary quotients

For each of the classical functions, the quotient has a closed form involving the same building blocks as the function itself: polynomials, powers, exponentials, logarithms, trigonometric functions. This is not an accident — it reflects the fact that the derivative of an elementary function is again elementary.

The limit reveals the derivative

For every function above except $|x|$, the quotient has a limit as $b\to a$, and that limit is precisely the derivative. For $|x|$, the limit fails at $0$, and the failure is exactly the corner in the graph. The differential quotient is thus a faithful detector of differentiability.

Two universal limits

All the transcendental examples reduce to two standard limits:

$$ \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{e^{h}-1}{h} = 1, \qquad \lim_{u\to 0}\frac{\ln(1+u)}{u} = 1, $$

together with the trigonometric limit $\sin u/u\to 1$. These three constants are the seeds from which the derivatives of $e^{x}$, $\ln x$, $\sin x$, and $\cos x$ grow.

The differential quotient is where differentiation begins: it is a two-point object, algebraic and concrete, and its limit is the local, infinitesimal object we call the derivative. Seen through the table above, differentiation is not a collection of isolated rules but a single coherent phenomenon.

17. Self-assessment

Try each question first, then click to reveal the answer.

Q1 For $f(x) = 5x - 7$, compute the differential quotient $\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ and simplify.

$\dfrac{(5b-7)-(5a-7)}{b-a} = \dfrac{5(b-a)}{b-a} = 5$. The slope is constant, as expected for a line.

Q2 For $f(x) = x^{2} + 3x$, compute the differential quotient.

$\dfrac{(b^{2}+3b)-(a^{2}+3a)}{b-a} = \dfrac{(b^{2}-a^{2}) + 3(b-a)}{b-a} = (a+b) + 3$.

Q3 Using Q2, find $f'(a)$.

$\displaystyle\lim_{b\to a}\bigl((a+b)+3\bigr) = 2a + 3$. So $f'(x) = 2x + 3$.

Q4 For $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$, explain in words why the quotient is always negative when $a$ and $b$ have the same sign.

The quotient equals $-1/(ab)$. If $a$ and $b$ have the same sign, then $ab > 0$, so $-1/(ab) < 0$. Geometrically, on each branch of the hyperbola the function is decreasing: as $x$ increases, $y$ decreases, hence the secant slope is negative.

Q5 Compute $\displaystyle\lim_{b\to a}\frac{\sqrt b - \sqrt a}{b-a}$ and state the derivative of $\sqrt x$.

$\dfrac{\sqrt b - \sqrt a}{b-a} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt a + \sqrt b}$, so the limit is $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt a}$. Hence $(\sqrt x)' = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}$.

Q6 For $f(x) = e^{x}$, verify the quotient formula and compute the limit as $b\to a$.

$\dfrac{e^{b}-e^{a}}{b-a} = e^{a}\cdot\dfrac{e^{b-a}-1}{b-a}$. With $h = b-a$, the second factor tends to $1$, so the limit is $e^{a}$. Hence $(e^{x})' = e^{x}$.

Q7 For $f(x) = \ln x$, show that the quotient equals $\dfrac{1}{a}\cdot\dfrac{\ln(1+u)}{u}$ with $u = (b-a)/a$, and compute the limit.

$\dfrac{\ln b - \ln a}{b-a} = \dfrac{\ln(1+h/a)}{h}$ with $h = b-a$. Putting $u = h/a$, this becomes $\dfrac{1}{a}\cdot\dfrac{\ln(1+u)}{u}$. As $u\to 0$, the fraction tends to $1$, so the limit is $1/a$. Hence $(\ln x)' = 1/x$.

Q8 For $f(x) = |x|$ at $a = 0$, compute the one-sided quotients as $b\to 0^{+}$ and $b\to 0^{-}$.

For $b > 0$: $\dfrac{|b|-0}{b-0} = \dfrac{b}{b} = 1$. For $b < 0$: $\dfrac{|b|-0}{b-0} = \dfrac{-b}{b} = -1$. The two one-sided limits differ, so the limit does not exist and $|x|$ is not differentiable at $0$.

Q9 State the general power rule for $x^{n}$, and prove it using the factorisation $b^{n}-a^{n} = (b-a)(b^{n-1}+b^{n-2}a+\cdots+a^{n-1})$.

$\displaystyle\lim_{b\to a}\frac{b^{n}-a^{n}}{b-a} = n\,a^{n-1}$. Proof: factor $b^{n}-a^{n} = (b-a)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a^{\,n-1-k}b^{\,k}$, cancel $b-a$, then let $b\to a$: each of the $n$ terms becomes $a^{n-1}$, giving $n\,a^{n-1}$.

Q10 For $f(x) = \sin x$, explain why the secant slope depends only on the midpoint of the interval.

The sum-to-product identity gives $\dfrac{\sin b - \sin a}{b-a} = \cos\!\bigl(\tfrac{a+b}{2}\bigr)\cdot \dfrac{\sin((b-a)/2)}{(b-a)/2}$. The first factor depends only on the midpoint $(a+b)/2$, and the second only on the half-width $(b-a)/2$. So the midpoint controls the leading behaviour, and the half-width controls the correction.

Q11 Sketch (in words) the graphs of $f(x) = x^{3}$ and its secant line through $a = 1$, $b = 2$. Is the secant above or below the graph between the two points?

The secant has slope $7$ and passes through $(1,1)$ and $(2,8)$. Since $x^{3}$ is convex on $x > 0$ (its second derivative is $6x > 0$), the graph lies below the secant between the two points. The secant is above the graph on $(1,2)$.

Q12 Suppose $f$ is differentiable at $a$ and $f'(a) > 0$. What can you say about the sign of $\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ for $b$ close to $a$?

Since the quotient tends to $f'(a) > 0$ as $b\to a$, for $b$ close enough to $a$ the quotient must also be positive. In particular, $f(b) > f(a)$ for $b$ close to $a$ on either side — the function is locally increasing at $a$.

Q13 A student claims: “The differential quotient of $1/x$ is $1/(ab)$.” Where is the error?

The correct formula is $-1/(ab)$. The minus sign comes from $a - b = -(b-a)$ in the numerator. Forgetting the sign would give a positive slope everywhere, which contradicts the fact that $1/x$ is decreasing on each branch.

Q14 Compute the differential quotient of $f(x) = x^{2} - 4x + 1$ and take the limit $b\to a$.

$\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} = \dfrac{(b^{2}-a^{2}) - 4(b-a)}{b-a} = (a+b) - 4$. Limit as $b\to a$: $2a - 4$. So $f'(x) = 2x - 4$.

Q15 Why does the differential quotient of $\cos x$ have a minus sign, while the differential quotient of $\sin x$ does not?

From the identities $\cos b - \cos a = -2\sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{b-a}{2}\bigr) \sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{a+b}{2}\bigr)$ and $\sin b - \sin a = 2\sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{b-a}{2}\bigr) \cos\!\bigl(\tfrac{a+b}{2}\bigr)$, the extra minus in the cosine case produces $-\sin a$ instead of $\cos a$. Geometrically, the graph of cosine is decreasing wherever sine is positive, and vice versa; the sign flip encodes this relation.
