Local Properties of Continuous Functions

Direct $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ proofs for Sign Preservation and Local Boundedness—exploring local function behavior near a point of continuity[cite: 4].

📌 Overview

We provide direct $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ proofs of two essential local properties of continuous functions[cite: 4]:

  • Sign Preservation: Continuous functions non-zero at $c$ retain their sign in a neighborhood around $c$[cite: 4].
  • Local Boundedness: Continuous functions at $c$ remain bounded in a neighborhood around $c$[cite: 4].

Seeing these alongside sequential proofs demonstrates how the two continuity frameworks operate in practice[cite: 4].

⚡ Theorem 1 — Sign Preservation

Let $f$ be continuous at $c$ with $f(c) \neq 0$[cite: 4]. Then there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $f(x)$ has the same sign as $f(c)$ for all $x \in (c-\delta, c+\delta)$[cite: 4]:

Positive Case:
If $f(c) > 0$, then $f(x) > 0$ for all $x \in (c-\delta, c+\delta)$[cite: 4].
Negative Case:
If $f(c) < 0$, then $f(x) < 0$ for all $x \in (c-\delta, c+\delta)$[cite: 4].
💡 Intuition for Choosing $\varepsilon$: We want $f(x)$ to remain on the same side of $0$ as $f(c)$[cite: 4]. The distance from $f(c)$ to $0$ is $|f(c)|$[cite: 4]. By forcing $|f(x) - f(c)| < |f(c)|$, $f(x)$ cannot cross $0$ or switch signs[cite: 4]. Thus, setting $\varepsilon = |f(c)|$ is the natural geometric choice[cite: 4].
Choosing $\varepsilon = |f(c)|$ creates an $\varepsilon$-band $(f(c)-\varepsilon, f(c)+\varepsilon)$ whose lower endpoint touches $0$ when $f(c) > 0$[cite: 4]. Consequently, any $f(x)$ lying inside this band remains strictly positive[cite: 4].
Formal Proof We treat the case $f(c) > 0$; the case $f(c) < 0$ follows analogously[cite: 4].

Step 1: Choose $\varepsilon$.
Since $f(c) \neq 0$, we have $|f(c)| > 0$[cite: 4]. Set $\varepsilon := |f(c)| = f(c) > 0$[cite: 4].

Step 2: Apply Continuity.
Since $f$ is continuous at $c$, for this $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that[cite: 4]: $$|x-c| < \delta \implies |f(x)-f(c)| < \varepsilon = |f(c)|$$[cite: 4] Step 3: Expand Absolute Value Inequality.
The inequality $|f(x)-f(c)| < |f(c)|$ is equivalent to[cite: 4]: $$-|f(c)| < f(x)-f(c) < |f(c)|$$[cite: 4] Adding $f(c)$ throughout[cite: 4]: $$f(c)-|f(c)| < f(x) < f(c)+|f(c)|$$[cite: 4] Step 4: Case $f(c) > 0$.
Here $|f(c)| = f(c)$, so the left-hand inequality simplifies to[cite: 4]: $$f(c)-f(c) = 0 < f(x)$$[cite: 4] Therefore, $f(x) > 0$ for all $x$ satisfying $|x-c| < \delta$[cite: 4].

Step 4': Case $f(c) < 0$.
Here $|f(c)| = -f(c)$, so the right-hand inequality simplifies to[cite: 4]: $$f(x) < f(c) + (-f(c)) = 0$$[cite: 4] Therefore, $f(x) < 0$ for all $x$ satisfying $|x-c| < \delta$[cite: 4].

In both cases, the $\delta$-neighborhood $(c-\delta, c+\delta)$ preserves the sign of $f(c)$[cite: 4]. $\blacksquare$
🔒 Theorem 2 — Local Boundedness

Let $f$ be continuous at $c$[cite: 4]. Then there exist $\delta > 0$ and $M > 0$ such that[cite: 4]:

$$|x-c| < \delta \implies |f(x)| \le M$$[cite: 4]

That is, $f$ is bounded on the neighborhood $(c-\delta, c+\delta)$[cite: 4].

💡 Triangle Inequality Strategy: We decompose $|f(x)|$ as $|(f(x)-f(c)) + f(c)| \le |f(x)-f(c)| + |f(c)|$[cite: 4]. By choosing a convenient fixed value $\varepsilon = 1$, continuity bounds $|f(x)-f(c)| < 1$[cite: 4]. Substituting yields $|f(x)| < 1 + |f(c)|$[cite: 4]. Setting $M = 1 + |f(c)|$ provides an explicit upper bound[cite: 4].
Choosing $\varepsilon = 1$ bounds $f(x)$ within distance $1$ of $f(c)$[cite: 4]. Applying the triangle inequality yields $|f(x)| \le |f(x)-f(c)| + |f(c)| < 1 + |f(c)| = M$[cite: 4].
Formal Proof Step 1: Choose $\varepsilon = 1$.
The definition of continuity applies for any positive $\varepsilon$[cite: 4]. We choose $\varepsilon := 1$[cite: 4].

Step 2: Find $\delta$.
Since $f$ is continuous at $c$, for $\varepsilon = 1$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that[cite: 4]: $$|x-c| < \delta \implies |f(x)-f(c)| < 1$$[cite: 4] Step 3: Apply the Triangle Inequality.
For any $x \in (c-\delta, c+\delta)$, write $f(x) = (f(x)-f(c)) + f(c)$[cite: 4]. By the triangle inequality[cite: 4]: $$|f(x)| = |(f(x)-f(c)) + f(c)| \le |f(x)-f(c)| + |f(c)|$$[cite: 4] Step 4: Substitute Bound.
Since $|x-c| < \delta$, we substitute $|f(x)-f(c)| < 1$[cite: 4]: $$|f(x)| \le |f(x)-f(c)| + |f(c)| < 1 + |f(c)|$$[cite: 4] Step 5: Define $M$.
Set $M := 1 + |f(c)|$[cite: 4]. This constant depends only on $f(c)$, independent of $x$[cite: 4]. Thus, for all $x \in (c-\delta, c+\delta)$[cite: 4]: $$|f(x)| < M$$[cite: 4] Hence, $f$ is bounded by $M$ on $(c-\delta, c+\delta)$[cite: 4]. $\blacksquare$

🎓 Technical Remarks on $\varepsilon$ Flexibility
  • Sign Preservation Rigidity: Setting $\varepsilon = |f(c)|$ is dictated by geometry—it marks the exact distance from $f(c)$ to $0$[cite: 4]. Choosing a larger $\varepsilon$ risks overshooting $0$, while any smaller $\varepsilon$ remains valid[cite: 4].
  • Local Boundedness Flexibility: Setting $\varepsilon = 1$ is arbitrary[cite: 4]. Selecting any constant $k > 0$ yields a valid bound $M = k + |f(c)|$[cite: 4]. Choosing $1$ offers an intuitive, clean representation[cite: 4].
