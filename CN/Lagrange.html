Formal Statement & Geometric Intuition

Theorem Statement (Lagrange’s Mean Value Theorem)

Let $f(x)$ be a real-valued function defined on a closed interval $[a, b]$. If $f$ satisfies the following two hypotheses:

H1:

Continuity: $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$.

H2:

Differentiability: $f$ is differentiable on the open interval $(a, b)$.

Then there exists at least one point $c \in (a, b)$ such that $$f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$$

The Average Speed Analogy

Suppose you drive 180 km in exactly 2 hours. Your average speed is 90 km/h. Common sense says that at some instant during the trip, your speedometer must have read exactly 90 km/h — you cannot average 90 without touching 90.

Here $f(t)$ is position, $\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ is average velocity, and $f'(c)$ is instantaneous velocity. As long as you did not teleport (Continuity) or undergo an instantaneous velocity jump (Differentiability), the instantaneous rate must equal the average rate somewhere.

Geometric Tangent Parallelism

Geometrically, the secant line connecting $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$ has slope $m = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$. Lagrange's MVT guarantees that somewhere in the interior $(a, b)$, the tangent line is parallel to this secant line.

Rolle’s Theorem is precisely the special case where the secant is horizontal ($m = 0$).

Strict Necessity of Each Hypothesis

Why removing even one single hypothesis causes the entire theorem guarantee to collapse

Lagrange’s MVT is a precision instrument. Each condition is strictly required to guarantee a tangent parallel to the secant. If a single condition is violated, counterexamples exist where no point $c$ exists with $f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$.

Failure Mode 1

Endpoint Jump on $[a, b]$

$f(x) = x$ for $0 \le x < 1$
$f(1) = 3$ on $[0, 1]$
  • Differentiable on $(0, 1)$: $f'(x) = 1$. H2 holds!
  • Jump discontinuity at the endpoint $x = 1$: $\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = 1$ but $f(1) = 3$.
  • Secant slope $= \frac{3 - 0}{1 - 0} = 3$, but $f'(c) = 1$ for every $c \in (0, 1)$.
$f'(x) = 1$ everywhere on $(0,1)$. Slope NEVER equals 3!
Failure Mode 2

Corner (Non-Diff on $(a,b)$)

$f(x) = |x|$ on $[-1, 1]$
  • Continuous on $[-1, 1]$
  • Secant slope $= \frac{1 - 1}{1 - (-1)} = 0$
  • Corner at $x=0$ ($f'(0)$ undefined).
Slopes are $-1$ or $+1$. Slope NEVER equals $0$!
Failure Mode 3

Vertical Cusp (Infinite Slope)

$f(x) = x^{2/3}$ on $[-1, 1]$
  • Continuous on $[-1, 1]$
  • Secant slope $= \frac{1 - 1}{1 - (-1)} = 0$
  • Vertical cusp at $x=0$; $f'(0)$ is infinite.
$f'(x) = \frac{2}{3}x^{-1/3}$ is never $0$ — it blows up!

Sufficiency vs. Necessity Mechanics

Logical breakdown: Why MVT's hypotheses are Sufficient but NOT Necessary

Sufficient Conditions

If both hypotheses hold ($H1 \land H2$), then the conclusion ($\exists c \in (a,b),\; f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$) is 100% guaranteed to occur.

NOT Necessary Conditions

Failing one or more conditions does NOT mean the conclusion is impossible! It only means MVT cannot be used to guarantee it.

Concrete Proofs of Non-Necessity

Example 1: Non-Differentiable yet MVT Holds

$f(x) = x^2 + |x|$ on $[-1, 2]$

  • • H1: Continuous on $[-1, 2]$.
  • • H2 Fails: Sharp corner at $x = 0$ ($f'(0)$ undefined).
  • • Secant slope $= \frac{f(2) - f(-1)}{2 - (-1)} = \frac{6 - 2}{3} = \frac{4}{3}$.
Outcome: On $x > 0$, $f(x) = x^2 + x$, so $f'(x) = 2x + 1 = \frac{4}{3}$ yields $c = \frac{1}{6} \in (-1, 2)$. A parallel tangent exists despite the corner!
Example 2: Discontinuous yet MVT Holds

$f(x) = x^2$ on $[-1, 1)$, $\;f(x) = x^2 - 1$ on $[1, 2]$

  • • H1 Fails: Jump discontinuity at $x = 1$.
  • • H2 Also Fails: Differentiability implies continuity, so $f$ is also non-differentiable at $x=1$.
  • • Secant slope $= \frac{f(2) - f(-1)}{3} = \frac{3 - 1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$.
  • • On $(-1, 1)$: $f'(x) = 2x = \frac{2}{3} \Rightarrow x = \frac{1}{3}$.
Outcome: $c = \frac{1}{3} \in (-1, 2)$ works perfectly. Even a total failure of hypotheses does not forbid the MVT conclusion from holding by coincidence!

Special Deep Dive: Tilt the World Until Rolle Applies

How subtracting the secant line transforms MVT into Rolle’s Theorem — and why differentiability is required only on the open interval

Students often ask: "Rolle’s Theorem only handles horizontal secants. How does MVT handle slanted ones?"

The Auxiliary Gap Function:

Define the vertical gap between the curve and the secant line:

$$h(x) = f(x) - \left[ f(a) + \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(x - a) \right]$$
  1. Endpoints vanish: $h(a) = 0$ and $h(b) = 0$, so $h(a) = h(b)$ automatically. The equal-endpoint condition is manufactured!
  2. Rolle applies: $h$ inherits continuity on $[a,b]$ and differentiability on $(a,b)$ from $f$.
  3. Conclusion: $\exists c$ with $h'(c) = 0$, i.e. $f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$.

Why $c$ is Strictly Interior

The guaranteed point $c$ lies in the open interval $(a,b)$, never at the boundary. Fermat’s Theorem on stationary points requires a two-sided neighbourhood — a requirement that only makes sense in the interior. This is exactly why differentiability is demanded only on $(a,b)$: vertical tangents or corners at the endpoints cannot obstruct an interior parallel tangent.

Formal Rigorous Proof of Lagrange’s MVT

Step-by-step mathematical proof by reduction to Rolle’s Theorem

Proof Logic Architecture Flowchart

Step 1: Construct the Auxiliary Function

Define a new function $h$ on $[a, b]$ that measures the vertical distance between the graph of $f$ and the secant line joining $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$:

$$h(x) = f(x) - f(a) - \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}\,(x - a)$$

Step 2: Verify Rolle’s Hypotheses for $h$

  • Continuity on $[a, b]$: $h$ is a sum of $f$ (continuous by H1) and a linear polynomial (always continuous). Thus $h$ is continuous on $[a, b]$.
  • Differentiability on $(a, b)$: $h$ is a sum of $f$ (differentiable by H2) and a linear polynomial. Thus $h$ is differentiable on $(a, b)$ with $h'(x) = f'(x) - \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$.
  • Equal endpoints: $h(a) = 0$ and $h(b) = 0$, hence $h(a) = h(b)$.

Step 3: Apply Rolle’s Theorem to $h$

Since all three hypotheses of Rolle’s Theorem are satisfied by $h$ on $[a, b]$, there exists at least one point $c \in (a, b)$ such that:

$$h'(c) = 0$$

Step 4: Unwind the Derivative and Conclude

Substituting the expression for $h'(c)$ obtained in Step 2:

$$h'(c) = f'(c) - \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} = 0$$ $$\Downarrow$$ $$f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$$
$$\therefore \;\exists\, c \in (a,b) \text{ with } f'(c) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} \quad \blacksquare \text{ (Q.E.D.)}$$

Rolle’s Theorem is the special case of MVT where the secant line happens to be horizontal ($f(a) = f(b)$). MVT is proved by reducing it to Rolle’s Theorem via the auxiliary gap function $h(x) = f(x) - f(a) - \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(x-a)$. Conversely, MVT is the generalisation that removes the equal-endpoint restriction.

MVT guarantees a minimum of one such point but places no upper bound. In the degenerate case of a linear function, every point works.
