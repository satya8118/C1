Higher Order Differentiation,
The Dynamics of $\xi(x)$ & Taylor's Theorem

Deconstructing the crucial insight behind Taylor's remainder term: why the intermediate point $\xi(x)$ is a dynamic composite function, why it is non-polynomial, and how it behaves asymptotically as $x \to c$.

Inspiration Note: The continuous difference quotient framework $h_1(x), h_2(x), \dots, h_{n+1}(x)$ and remainder analysis presented here is inspired by Omar Hijab's textbook, Introduction to Analysis.

1. The Core Paradox & Its Resolution

Why $f(x) = P_n(x) + R_{n+1}(x)$ Looks Like a Polynomial, Yet $f(x)$ is NOT

Consider Taylor's theorem in Lagrange form centered at $c$:

\[ f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n \frac{f^{(k)}(c)}{k!}(x-c)^k \;+\; \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi(x))}{(n+1)!}(x-c)^{n+1} \]

At first glance, both terms on the right side appear to involve powers of $(x-c)$, leading to a common misconception: "If both parts look like algebraic power terms, why isn't $f(x)$ just a polynomial of degree $n+1$?"

1. Polynomial Part $P_n(x)$

In $P_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n a_k (x-c)^k$, the coefficients $a_k = \frac{f^{(k)}(c)}{k!}$ are fixed scalar constants computed strictly at the expansion center $c$. $P_n(x)$ is a true polynomial.

2. Remainder Part $R_{n+1}(x)$

The coefficient of $(x-c)^{n+1}$ is $A(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi(x))}{(n+1)!}$. This coefficient is NOT constant! It depends dynamically on $x$ because $\xi = \xi(x)$ shifts whenever $x$ changes.

Resolution of the Paradox

Transcendental functions such as $e^x$, $\sin x$, and $\ln(1+x)$ have infinitely many non-zero derivatives. If $R_{n+1}(x)$ were a polynomial of degree $n+1$, then $f(x)$ would be a finite polynomial, implying $f^{(N)}(x) \equiv 0$ for large $N$—a complete contradiction! The non-polynomial nature of $f(x)$ is entirely absorbed by the dynamic term $f^{(n+1)}(\xi(x))$.

2. Deconstructing $f^{(n+1)}(\xi(x))$ as a Function Composition

To rigorously understand the expression $f^{(n+1)}(\xi(x))$, we must dissect it as a composite function $(g \circ \xi)(x) = g(\xi(x))$.

FUNCTION COMPOSITION PIPELINE
DOMAIN INPUT $x \in I \setminus \{c\}$
→
INNER SELECTION FUNCTION $\xi(x)$ $\xi : x \mapsto \xi(x) \in (c, x)$
→
OUTER DERIVATIVE $g(u)$ $g(u) = f^{(n+1)}(u)$
→
COMPOSITE VALUE $(g \circ \xi)(x)$

Key Mathematical Properties of $\xi(x)$:

  • 1. The Mean Value Map: $\xi(x)$ is a function that maps every evaluation point $x \neq c$ to the specific intermediate location guaranteed by the Mean Value Theorem (or Rolle's Theorem) strictly inside the interval $(c, x)$ (or $(x, c)$ if $x < c$).
  • 2. Existence vs Uniqueness & Selection Dynamics: MVT guarantees that at least one such $\xi(x)$ exists. If $f^{(n+1)}$ is not strictly monotonic, there might be multiple points satisfying the equation. In such cases, $\xi(x)$ represents a well-defined selection rule (e.g., choosing the smallest or continuous branch of intermediate points).
  • 3. Continuity of the Composite: When $f^{(n+1)}$ is continuous on $(a,b)$, as $x \to c$, the squeezed interval $(c, x)$ forces $\xi(x) \to c$. Thus, the composite function satisfies $\lim_{x \to c} f^{(n+1)}(\xi(x)) = f^{(n+1)}(c)$.

3. Asymptotic Behavior & The Rule $\lim_{x \to c} \frac{\xi(x)-c}{x-c} = \frac{1}{n+2}$

Where exactly does $\xi(x)$ sit relative to $c$ and $x$ as $x$ approaches $c$? By the Squeeze Theorem, since $c < \xi(x) < x$, we immediately have $\lim_{x \to c} \xi(x) = c$. But how fast does $\xi(x)$ move toward $c$?

Asymptotic Location Theorem
\[ \lim_{x \to c} \frac{\xi(x) - c}{x - c} = \frac{1}{n + 2} \qquad \text{(assuming } f^{(n+2)}(c) \neq 0 \text{)} \]

As $x \to c$, the intermediate point $\xi(x)$ approaches a fixed fractional distance $\frac{1}{n+2}$ along the segment from $c$ to $x$!

Proof Sketch for $n=0$ (Ordinary Mean Value Theorem)

By MVT, $f(x) - f(c) = f'(\xi(x))(x-c)$. Expand both sides in Taylor series around $c$ up to second order (assuming $f''(c) \neq 0$):

\[ f(x) - f(c) = f'(c)(x-c) + \frac{f''(c)}{2}(x-c)^2 + o((x-c)^2) \] \[ f'(\xi(x)) = f'(c) + f''(c)(\xi(x)-c) + o(\xi(x)-c) \]

Substitute $f'(\xi(x))$ into MVT:

\[ f'(c)(x-c) + \frac{f''(c)}{2}(x-c)^2 + o((x-c)^2) = \left[ f'(c) + f''(c)(\xi(x)-c) + o(\xi(x)-c) \right] (x-c) \]

Subtract $f'(c)(x-c)$ from both sides and divide by $f''(c)(x-c)^2$:

\[ \frac{1}{2} + o(1) = \frac{\xi(x)-c}{x-c} + o(1) \implies \lim_{x \to c} \frac{\xi(x)-c}{x-c} = \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{0+2} \]

4. Concrete Explicit Example: $f(x) = e^x$ at Center $c = 0$

For $f(x) = e^x$ centered at $c = 0$, we can solve for $\xi(x)$ explicitly in closed form!

The Taylor decomposition with Lagrange remainder states:

\[ e^x = \sum_{k=0}^n \frac{x^k}{k!} + \frac{e^{\xi(x)}}{(n+1)!}x^{n+1} \]

Isolating $e^{\xi(x)}$ and taking the natural logarithm yields the explicit formula for $\xi(x)$:

\[ \xi(x) = \ln \left( \frac{(n+1)!}{x^{n+1}} \left( e^x - \sum_{k=0}^n \frac{x^k}{k!} \right) \right) \]
Evaluating $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\xi(x)}{x}$ for $e^x$

Using the higher Taylor series expansion $e^x - \sum_{k=0}^n \frac{x^k}{k!} = \frac{x^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} + \frac{x^{n+2}}{(n+2)!} + O(x^{n+3})$, we substitute into $\xi(x)$:

\[ \xi(x) = \ln \left( 1 + \frac{x}{n+2} + O(x^2) \right) = \frac{x}{n+2} + O(x^2) \]

Dividing by $x$ gives $\frac{\xi(x)}{x} \to \frac{1}{n+2}$, confirming the general theorem directly!

5. Hijab's Continuous Quotient Perspective vs Lagrange Form

In Introduction to Analysis, Omar Hijab avoids non-constructive intermediate values $\xi(x)$ at the outset by defining the difference quotient directly as a continuous function $h_{n+1}(x)$:

\[ h_{n+1}(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{R_{n+1}(x,c)}{(x-c)^{n+1}/(n+1)!}, & x \neq c, \\[0.8em] f^{(n+1)}(c), & x = c. \end{cases} \]
Bridging Both Views

Equating Hijab's continuous quotient $h_{n+1}(x)$ with the Lagrange remainder reveals:

\[ h_{n+1}(x) = f^{(n+1)}(\xi(x)) = (f^{(n+1)} \circ \xi)(x) \]

This identity unites both perspectives: Hijab's $h_{n+1}(x)$ is continuous at $c$ precisely because $\lim_{x \to c} f^{(n+1)}(\xi(x)) = f^{(n+1)}(c) = h_{n+1}(c)$.
