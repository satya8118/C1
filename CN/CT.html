Mathematical Nugget

Curve Tracing

A short, practical checklist for sketching the graph of a function by hand — domain, intercepts, symmetry, asymptotes, critical points, and concavity — followed by two worked examples and four practice problems.

01Why bother sketching by hand

What curve tracing gives you

Curve tracing (or curve sketching) is the skill of building an accurate picture of a function's graph without plotting hundreds of points — by reading off a handful of structural facts about the function instead. Done well, it tells you where the graph rises and falls, where it bends, where it heads off to infinity, and where it's undefined, all before you ever touch a plotting tool.

Every curve-tracing problem is really the same six-step routine, applied to a different function. Learn the routine once, and every "sketch this graph" problem becomes an exercise in bookkeeping rather than a fresh puzzle.
02The routine

A six-step checklist

Step 1 Domain

Where is the function undefined or discontinuous? Division by zero and logarithms of non-positive numbers are the usual culprits.

Step 2 Intercepts

Set \(y=0\) and solve for \(x\) to get the \(x\)-intercept(s); set \(x=0\) to get the \(y\)-intercept.

Step 3 Symmetry

Check \(f(-x)\): if it equals \(f(x)\), the graph is symmetric about the \(y\)-axis (even); if it equals \(-f(x)\), symmetric about the origin (odd).

Step 4 Asymptotes

Vertical asymptotes occur where the denominator vanishes; horizontal asymptotes come from the limit as \(x\to\pm\infty\).

Step 5 Critical points & monotonicity

Solve \(f'(x)=0\) (or find where \(f'\) is undefined). The sign of \(f'\) on either side tells you increasing vs. decreasing, and classifies each critical point.

Step 6 Concavity & inflection points

Solve \(f''(x)=0\). Where \(f''>0\) the graph is concave up; where \(f''<0\), concave down. A sign change marks an inflection point.

Steps 5 and 6 lean on exactly the derivative tests you've already seen — a critical point is a local minimum when \(f''>0\) there, a local maximum when \(f''<0\), and inconclusive (possibly an inflection point) when \(f''=0\).

03Step 3, in detail

The symmetry test

Checking symmetry costs almost nothing and can cut your work in half — sketch one side, then reflect.

TestConclusionExample
\(f(-x)=f(x)\) Even function — graph symmetric about the \(y\)-axis \(f(x)=x^2+4\): \(f(-x)=(-x)^2+4=x^2+4=f(x)\)
\(f(-x)=-f(x)\) Odd function — graph symmetric about the origin \(f(x)=x^3-3x\): \(f(-x)=-x^3+3x=-(x^3-3x)=-f(x)\)
Neither identity holds No symmetry — must sketch the full domain directly \(f(x)=x^2-6x+8\): \(f(-x)=x^2+6x+8\ne\pm f(x)\)
Replacing \(y\) by \(-y\) leaves the equation unchanged Symmetric about the \(x\)-axis (typically a relation, not a function) \(y^2=x\): \((-y)^2=x\) is the same equation
04Step 4, in detail

Types of asymptotes

An asymptote is a line the graph gets arbitrarily close to but never actually reaches. There are three flavors:

TypeHow to find itExample
Vertical Values of \(x\) where the function is undefined because the denominator vanishes (and the numerator doesn't vanish there too) \(f(x)=\dfrac{2x+1}{x-3}\) → vertical asymptote at \(x=3\)
Horizontal \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)\) — divide top and bottom by the highest power of \(x\) Same \(f(x)\) above → horizontal asymptote \(y=2\)
Slant (oblique) Occurs when the numerator's degree is exactly one more than the denominator's. Do polynomial long division; the quotient (ignoring the remainder) is the slant asymptote \(f(x)=\dfrac{x^2+1}{x}=x+\dfrac1x\) → slant asymptote \(y=x\)

A function can have a horizontal asymptote or a slant asymptote, but never both at the same "end" of the graph — a slant asymptote only shows up precisely when there's no horizontal one to have.

05Worked example — a rational function

Intercepts and asymptotes of \(f(x)=\dfrac{2x+1}{x-3}\)

\(x\)-intercept: set \(f(x)=0\), i.e. \(2x+1=0\) (with \(x\ne3\)), giving \(x=-\tfrac12\). Intercept at \(\left(-\tfrac12,0\right)\).

\(y\)-intercept: \(f(0)=\dfrac{1}{-3}=-\tfrac13\). Intercept at \(\left(0,-\tfrac13\right)\).

Vertical asymptote: the denominator vanishes at \(x=3\), so there's a vertical asymptote there.

Horizontal asymptote: divide numerator and denominator by \(x\): \(f(x)=\dfrac{2+1/x}{1-3/x}\). As \(x\to\pm\infty\), the \(1/x\) terms vanish, leaving \(f(x)\to2\). So \(y=2\) is a horizontal asymptote.

Graph of f(x) = (2x + 1) / (x − 3)

Dashed lines mark the asymptotes \(x=3\) and \(y=2\); the two branches approach both but never touch them. The marked points are the intercepts computed above.

06Worked example — a cubic polynomial

Slope and concavity of \(f(x)=x^3-3x^2+2x\)

\(f'(x)=3x^2-6x+2\), so the slope at \(x=-1\) is \(f'(-1)=3+6+2=11\) — steeply increasing there.

\(f''(x)=6x-6\), which vanishes at \(x=1\). For \(x<1\), \(f''(x)<0\) (concave down); for \(x>1\), \(f''(x)>0\) (concave up). So the concavity flips at \(x=1\), and \((1,f(1))=(1,0)\) is an inflection point.

Interactive — the graph of f(x) = x³ − 3x² + 2x

Toggle each layer on or off. Notice the two critical points (where the tangent is momentarily flat) sit on either side of the single inflection point — exactly where the checklist above predicts them.

07Four families you'll meet constantly

Tracing common function families

Most functions you'll be asked to sketch belong to one of four families, each with its own shortcut.

Linear — f(x) = 2x + 3

A straight line needs just two points — no derivatives required. Here, \(x\)-intercept \(-\tfrac32\), \(y\)-intercept \(3\).

Quadratic — f(x) = x² + 4

Even: \(f(-x)=(-x)^2+4=f(x)\), so symmetric about the \(y\)-axis. \(f'(x)=2x=0\) at \(x=0\); \(f''(x)=2>0\), a minimum, value \(f(0)=4\).

Exponential — f(x) = 2ˣ − 1

Base \(2>1\), so strictly increasing. \(f'(x)=2^x\ln2\) is never zero — no critical points. \(f''(x)=2^x\ln^22>0\) always — convex everywhere, with horizontal asymptote \(y=-1\) as \(x\to-\infty\).

Logarithmic — f(x) = log₁₀x + 5

Domain is \(x>0\), with a vertical asymptote at \(x=0\). \(\log_{10}x+5\) is the ordinary log curve shifted up \(5\) units — logarithmic functions are, in fact, the mirror image of exponential functions across the line \(y=x\), since one is the inverse of the other.

08Cheat sheet

Even-degree polynomial \(\mathbb{R}\) Both ends point the same way (up if leading coeff. \(>0\)) None
Odd-degree polynomial \(\mathbb{R}\) Ends point opposite ways; at least one inflection point None
Rational \(\mathbb{R}\) minus the denominator's zeros Broken into separate branches by vertical asymptotes Vertical always; horizontal or slant, depending on degree

09Practice

Eight problems to try yourself

Problem 1

Sketch \(f(x)=x+8\).

Domain is all of \(\mathbb{R}\); as \(x\to\pm\infty\), \(f(x)\to\pm\infty\). \(f'(x)=1\) everywhere, so there are no critical points — the function is increasing at a constant rate throughout, with no bends to trace. The \(x\)-intercept is at \(x=-8\) (setting \(x+8=0\)), the \(y\)-intercept is at \(f(0)=8\), and the graph is simply a straight line through those two points.

Problem 2

Sketch \(f(x)=x^2-6x+8\).

Domain is \(\mathbb{R}\); \(f(x)\to\infty\) as \(x\to\pm\infty\). Critical points: \(f'(x)=2x-6=0\implies x=3\). Checking the sign of \(f'\): negative for \(x<3\) (decreasing), positive for \(x>3\) (increasing) — so \(x=3\) is a minimum.

Factoring for \(x\)-intercepts: \(x^2-6x+8=(x-2)(x-4)=0\), giving \(x=2\) and \(x=4\). The parabola dips to a minimum at \(x=3\) between these two roots.

Problem 3

Sketch \(f(x)=x^3-3x+4\).

Domain is \(\mathbb{R}\); \(f(x)\to\pm\infty\) as \(x\to\pm\infty\) respectively. Critical points: \(f'(x)=3x^2-3=0\implies x^2=1\implies x=\pm1\).

Sign of \(f'\): positive on \((-\infty,-1)\) (increasing), negative on \((-1,1)\) (decreasing), positive on \((1,\infty)\) (increasing) — so \(x=-1\) is a local maximum and \(x=1\) is a local minimum. A convenient extra point: \(f(0)=4\), the \(y\)-intercept.

Problem 4

Sketch \(f(x)=e^x+2\).

\(f'(x)=e^x\), which is never zero — so there are no critical points, and the function increases everywhere. \(f''(x)=e^x>0\) always, so the graph is concave up throughout, with no inflection points. This is just the ordinary exponential curve \(e^x\), shifted up by \(2\) units — it approaches the horizontal asymptote \(y=2\) as \(x\to-\infty\), and rises without bound as \(x\to\infty\).

Problem 5

Sketch \(f(x)=x^3-3x\), using symmetry to simplify your work.

Symmetry: \(f(-x)=-x^3+3x=-(x^3-3x)=-f(x)\), so \(f\) is odd — symmetric about the origin. Sketch the right half and reflect.

Critical points: \(f'(x)=3x^2-3=0\implies x=\pm1\). \(f''(x)=6x\): at \(x=1\), \(f''=6>0\) (local min, value \(f(1)=-2\)); at \(x=-1\), \(f''=-6<0\) (local max, value \(f(-1)=2\)) — consistent with the odd symmetry.

Inflection point: \(f''(x)=6x=0\implies x=0\), and \(f(0)=0\) — the origin itself, exactly where an odd function's graph must pass.

\(x\)-intercepts: \(x^3-3x=x(x^2-3)=0\implies x=0,\pm\sqrt3\).

Problem 6

Sketch \(f(x)=\dfrac{1}{x-2}+1\).

Domain: all reals except \(x=2\), where the denominator vanishes — vertical asymptote at \(x=2\).

Horizontal asymptote: as \(x\to\pm\infty\), \(\dfrac{1}{x-2}\to0\), so \(f(x)\to1\) — horizontal asymptote \(y=1\).

Intercepts: \(x\)-intercept from \(\dfrac{1}{x-2}=-1\implies x=1\); \(y\)-intercept \(f(0)=\dfrac{1}{-2}+1=\tfrac12\).

Monotonicity: \(f'(x)=-\dfrac{1}{(x-2)^2}<0\) everywhere it's defined — the function is decreasing on each branch, with no critical points. This is just \(y=\tfrac1x\) shifted \(2\) right and \(1\) up.

Problem 7

Sketch \(f(x)=\ln(x)-2\).

Domain: \(x>0\) — logarithms are only defined for positive inputs. Vertical asymptote at \(x=0\), and no \(y\)-intercept, since \(x=0\) is excluded.

\(x\)-intercept: \(\ln x=2\implies x=e^2\approx7.39\).

Monotonicity and concavity: \(f'(x)=\dfrac1x>0\) for every \(x\) in the domain — always increasing, no critical points. \(f''(x)=-\dfrac{1}{x^2}<0\) always — concave down throughout.

Problem 8

Sketch \(f(x)=\dfrac{x^2+1}{x}\). (Hint: rewrite it first.)

Rewrite: \(f(x)=\dfrac{x^2+1}{x}=x+\dfrac1x\). Domain is \(x\ne0\), giving a vertical asymptote at \(x=0\). Since the numerator's degree \((2)\) is exactly one more than the denominator's \((1)\), there's a slant asymptote — reading it straight off the rewritten form, \(y=x\), as \(\tfrac1x\to0\) at both ends.

Symmetry: \(f(-x)=-x-\tfrac1x=-f(x)\) — odd, symmetric about the origin.

Critical points: \(f'(x)=1-\dfrac{1}{x^2}=0\implies x=\pm1\). \(f''(x)=\dfrac{2}{x^3}\): at \(x=1\), \(f''=2>0\) (local min, \(f(1)=2\)); at \(x=-1\), \(f''=-2<0\) (local max, \(f(-1)=-2\)).

Intercepts: none — \(x^2+1=0\) has no real solution, and \(x=0\) isn't in the domain.

The takeaway. Almost every curve-tracing problem reduces to the same short script: pin down the domain, find the intercepts, differentiate once for critical points and monotonicity, differentiate again for concavity and inflection points, and check the behavior at the edges of the domain (asymptotes, or \(x\to\pm\infty\)). Run that script and the sketch draws itself.
