The Nature of Mathematical Breaks

An Interactive Guide to the Classification of Discontinuities

🌱 Welcome to University Calculus

In senior school (10+1, 10+2), you learned that a function $f(x)$ is continuous if you can trace its graph without lifting your pencil. While useful, real analysis demands a rigorous definition built on limits!

A function $f: I \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous at $x = a$ if and only if three conditions hold:

  1. $f(a)$ is defined.
  2. The limit $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists in $\mathbb{R}$.
  3. $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$.

When any of these conditions fail, $x = a$ is a point of discontinuity. Let $D$ denote the set of all points where $f$ is discontinuous:

$$D = \{ x \in \mathbb{R} \mid f \text{ is discontinuous at } x \}$$

In this guide, we explore how breaks are classified, why every discontinuity fits neatly into three categories, and how large the discontinuity set $D$ can become.

⚡ Why Every Discontinuity is One of the Three Types

Why do mathematicians divide discontinuities into Removable, Jump, and Essential? Is it possible for a break to fall outside these categories?

The answer lies in examining the two one-sided limits at $x = a$:

$$\text{Left-hand limit: } L_L = \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) \quad \text{and} \quad \text{Right-hand limit: } L_R = \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$$

Exhaustive Decision Tree of One-Sided Limits in $\mathbb{R}$:

1. Do BOTH $L_L$ and $L_R$ exist as FINITE real numbers?

👉 Case 1A: $L_L = L_R = L$
The two-sided limit $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ exists! If $f(a) \neq L$ or $f(a)$ is undefined, this is a Removable Discontinuity.

👉 Case 1B: $L_L \neq L_R$
The two one-sided limits exist but disagree. The function steps from $L_L$ to $L_R$. This is a Jump Discontinuity.

2. Does AT LEAST ONE of $L_L$ or $L_R$ FAIL to exist as a finite real number?

👉 Case 2: Essential Discontinuity
If $L_L$ or $L_R$ equals $\pm \infty$ or oscillates without approaching any single value, this is an Essential Discontinuity.

Because any pair of one-sided limits either both exist and are equal, both exist and are unequal, or at least one fails to exist as a finite number, this breakdown covers all logical possibilities. No discontinuity can ever escape this trichotomy!

🔍 Deep-Dive: The Three Primary Classes

Click through the tabs below to explore each discontinuity type with visual graphs and concrete mathematical examples:

Removable Discontinuity ($R$) — "The Hole"

Intuition: As you approach $x = a$ from either side, the function travels towards the exact same height $L$. However, right at $x = a$, there is a missing point or a point displaced above or below the curve.

Condition: $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ exists in $\mathbb{R}$, but $f(a) \neq L$ (or $f(a)$ is undefined).

Why "Removable"? We can remove the discontinuity entirely by defining (or redefining) $f(a) = L$.

Examples:

  • Example 1: $f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}$ at $x = 1$. The function is undefined at $x=1$, but $\lim_{x \to 1} f(x) = 2$. Defining $f(1) = 2$ removes the break.
  • Example 2: $g(x) = \frac{\sin x}{x}$ at $x = 0$. Here $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$, but $g(0)$ is undefined. Setting $g(0) = 1$ makes $g$ continuous everywhere.
  • Example 3: $h(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 & \text{if } x \neq 2 \\ 1 & \text{if } x = 2 \end{cases}$. The limit is $4$, but $h(2) = 1$.

Jump Discontinuity ($J$) — "The Step"

Intuition: Approaching $x = a$ from the left lands you at height $L_L$, but approaching from the right lands you at a different height $L_R$. The graph steps vertically across a gap.

Condition: Both $L_L = \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x)$ and $L_R = \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$ exist as finite real numbers, but $L_L \neq L_R$.

The positive value $|L_R - L_L|$ is called the jump magnitude.

Can we fix it? No! Changing $f(a)$ at a single point cannot bridge the vertical gap between $L_L$ and $L_R$.

Examples:

  • Example 1 (Signum Function): $\text{sgn}(x) = \frac{x}{|x|}$ for $x \neq 0$. Here $\lim_{x \to 0^-} \text{sgn}(x) = -1$ and $\lim_{x \to 0^+} \text{sgn}(x) = +1$. The jump size is $|1 - (-1)| = 2$.
  • Example 2 (Floor Function): $f(x) = \lfloor x \rfloor$ at any integer $n \in \mathbb{Z}$. As $x \to n^-$, $f(x) \to n-1$, whereas as $x \to n^+$, $f(x) \to n$. The jump size is $1$.
  • Example 3 (Piecewise Model): $p(x) = \begin{cases} x + 1 & \text{if } x < 0 \\ \cos(x) - 2 & \text{if } x \ge 0 \end{cases}$. At $x = 0$, $L_L = 1$ and $L_R = -1$.

Essential Discontinuity ($E$) — "Wild Behavior"

Intuition: Near $x = a$, the function displays wild behavior: it blows up toward positive or negative infinity, or oscillates back and forth at infinite frequency.

Condition: At least one of the one-sided limits $\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x)$ or $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$ fails to exist as a finite real number.

Essential discontinuities are partitioned into three subtypes ($E = E_1 \cup E_2 \cup E_3$):

$E_1$: Oscillatory (Bounded)

The limit fails to exist because the function oscillates infinitely fast, yet stays bounded in a finite range.

Example: $f(x) = \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$ at $x = 0$. As $x \to 0$, $1/x \to \infty$, causing $\sin(1/x)$ to bounce between $-1$ and $+1$ endlessly.

$E_2$: Infinite (Unbounded)

At least one one-sided limit blows up to $+\infty$ or $-\infty$ (a vertical asymptote).

Examples: $f(x) = \frac{1}{x}$ or $g(x) = \frac{1}{x^2}$ at $x = 0$.

$E_3$: Mixed / Asymmetric Wild

One side behaves smoothly while the other side goes to infinity or oscillates wildly.

Example: $f(x) = e^{1/x}$ at $x = 0$. As $x \to 0^-$, $e^{1/x} \to 0$ (well-behaved). As $x \to 0^+$, $e^{1/x} \to +\infty$.

🔬 Detailed Anatomical Study: $E_1$, $E_2$, and $E_3$

Essential discontinuities are not all identical. To analyze them in rigor, real analysis decomposes $E$ into three distinct mathematical regions based on boundedness and asymmetry.

🌊 Type $E_1$: Bounded Oscillatory Discontinuity

Mathematical Characterization: Near $x = a$, the function $f(x)$ remains strictly bounded within some finite interval $[M_1, M_2]$, but neither $\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x)$ nor $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$ exists because the values oscillate endlessly between upper and lower bounds.

SVG Visual: $f(x) = \sin(1/x)$ near $x=0$ (Infinite frequency oscillation within $[-1, 1]$)

Prototypical Examples of $E_1$:

  • $f(x) = \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$ at $x = 0$. As $x \to 0$, $1/x$ passes through infinitely many multiples of $2\pi$, causing $f(x)$ to cycle between $-1$ and $+1$ infinitely many times in any neighborhood around $0$.
  • $g(x) = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{x}\right)$ at $x = 0$. Takes value $1$ at $x = \frac{1}{2k}$ and $-1$ at $x = \frac{1}{2k+1}$.
  • $h(x) = \text{sgn}(x) \cdot \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$ at $x = 0$.

🚀 Type $E_2$: Unbounded Infinite Discontinuity (Vertical Asymptote)

Mathematical Characterization: Near $x = a$, the function grows without bound. At least one of the one-sided limits is $+\infty$ or $-\infty$:

$$\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \pm \infty \quad \text{or} \quad \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \pm \infty$$

SVG Visual: Symmetric & Asymmetric Vertical Asymptotes ($1/x^2$ vs $1/x$ near $x=0$)

Prototypical Examples of $E_2$:

  • $f(x) = \frac{1}{x^2}$ at $x = 0$. Here $\lim_{x \to 0^-} f(x) = +\infty$ and $\lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x) = +\infty$. Both sides shoot to $+\infty$.
  • $g(x) = \frac{1}{x}$ at $x = 0$. Here $\lim_{x \to 0^-} g(x) = -\infty$ and $\lim_{x \to 0^+} g(x) = +\infty$.
  • $h(x) = \tan(x)$ at $x = \frac{\pi}{2}$. Left-hand limit is $+\infty$, right-hand limit is $-\infty$.

🔀 Type $E_3$: Mixed / Asymmetric Wild Discontinuity

Mathematical Characterization: One-sided behaviors are radically asymmetric. For instance, the function may approach a well-behaved finite limit from the left, but blow up to $\infty$ or oscillate endlessly from the right.

SVG Visual: $f(x) = e^{1/x}$ near $x=0$ (Left limit $= 0$, Right limit $= +\infty$)

Prototypical Examples of $E_3$:

  • Exponential Singularity: $f(x) = e^{1/x}$ at $x = 0$. $$\lim_{x \to 0^-} e^{1/x} = e^{-\infty} = 0 \quad (\text{finite!}), \qquad \lim_{x \to 0^+} e^{1/x} = e^{+\infty} = +\infty \quad (\text{infinite!})$$
  • Oscillatory-Piecewise Hybrid: $$g(x) = \begin{cases} \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) & \text{if } x < 0 \\ x + 1 & \text{if } x \ge 0 \end{cases}$$ At $x = 0$, the left limit does not exist ($E_1$ oscillation), but the right limit is a well-behaved finite number ($L_R = 1$).
  • Oscillatory-Unbounded Hybrid: $h(x) = \frac{1}{x} \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$ at $x = 0$. Oscillates with unbounded amplitude as $x \to 0$.

Summary Comparison Matrix: $E_1$, $E_2$, $E_3$

Subtype Left Limit $L_L$ Right Limit $L_R$ Bounded near $a$? Prototypical Example
E1 (Oscillatory) Does not exist (oscillates) Does not exist (oscillates) Yes (Bounded) $\sin(1/x)$ at $x=0$
E2 (Infinite) $\pm \infty$ $\pm \infty$ No (Unbounded) $1/x^2$ or $1/x$ at $x=0$

🧮 The Complete Decomposition of $D$

Because every point of discontinuity fits into one and only one category, the set $D$ can be written as a disjoint union:

$$D = R \cup J \cup E = R \cup J \cup (E_1 \cup E_2 \cup E_3)$$

🏛️ The Discontinuity Zoo: How Large Can $D$ Be?

Now let's examine the structure and size of the set $D$. How many points of discontinuity can a single function have, and how close together can they be?

Case 1

$D$ is Finite

The set of breaks consists of just a few isolated points.

Function: $f(x) = \frac{x}{x^2 - 4}$

$D = \{-2, 2\}$ (2 essential points)

💡 Click to reveal deeper fact...
When $D$ is finite, the function remains continuous on almost the entire real line!
Case 2

$D$ Infinite, NOT Dense

There are infinitely many breaks, but they are separated by intervals of continuity.

Function: Floor Function $f(x) = \lfloor x \rfloor$

$D = \mathbb{Z}$ (Countably infinite, discrete)

💡 Click to reveal deeper fact...
Although there are infinitely many jump points, inside every open interval $(n, n+1)$, $f(x)$ is continuous!
Case 3

$D$ Infinite & Everywhere Dense

Every open interval, no matter how small, contains a discontinuity!

Dirichlet Function:

$$D(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x \in \mathbb{Q} \\ 0 & \text{if } x \notin \mathbb{Q} \end{cases}$$

$D = \mathbb{R}$ (Discontinuous everywhere!)

💡 Click to reveal deeper fact...
Because both rationals and irrationals are dense in $\mathbb{R}$, $D(x)$ jumps between 0 and 1 infinitely often in every interval!
Case 4

$D$ Dense, Continuous at Irrationals

Can $f$ be discontinuous on a dense set, yet continuous at uncountably many points?

Thomae's (Popcorn) Function:

$$T(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{q} & \text{if } x = \frac{p}{q} \text{ in lowest terms} \\ 0 & \text{if } x \text{ is irrational} \end{cases}$$

$D = \mathbb{Q}$ (Discontinuous on rationals, continuous on irrationals!)

💡 Click to reveal deeper fact...
$T(x)$ is continuous at EVERY irrational point and discontinuous at EVERY rational point! All its discontinuities are removable.

🔥 The Big Question: Can ANY Subset $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ Be a Discontinuity Set?

🔥 You may skip to read. Read at a later stage. Provided just to know the fact.

Given an arbitrary subset $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, can we construct a function $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ such that $D(f) = S$?


Answer: NO! Not every subset of $\mathbb{R}$ can be a discontinuity set!

To understand why, we look at real analysis and topology. The set of discontinuities $D(f)$ of any real-valued function must satisfy a strict structural property: it must be an $F_\sigma$ set.

Theorem (Young / Froda):

For any function $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, the set $D(f)$ of points of discontinuity is an $F_\sigma$ set (a countable union of closed sets).

Proof Outline using Oscillation:

Define the oscillation of $f$ at a point $x$ as:

$$\omega_f(x) = \lim_{\delta \to 0^+} \sup \{ |f(u) - f(v)| : u, v \in (x-\delta, x+\delta) \}$$
  • A function $f$ is continuous at $x$ if and only if $\omega_f(x) = 0$.
  • Therefore, $f$ is discontinuous at $x$ if and only if $\omega_f(x) > 0$.
  • For each $n \in \mathbb{N}$, define the set $E_n = \left\{ x \in \mathbb{R} \;\middle|\; \omega_f(x) \ge \frac{1}{n} \right\}$.
  • One can prove that each $E_n$ is a closed set in $\mathbb{R}$.
  • The discontinuity set is then the countable union of these closed sets:
$$D(f) = \bigcup_{n=1}^\infty E_n \quad \implies \quad D(f) \text{ is an } F_\sigma \text{ set!}$$

The Mind-Bending Asymmetry:

  • Rationals $\mathbb{Q}$: The set $\mathbb{Q}$ is a countable union of singletons $\bigcup_{q \in \mathbb{Q}} \{q\}$. Since singletons are closed, $\mathbb{Q}$ is an $F_\sigma$ set! That is why Thomae's function can be discontinuous on $\mathbb{Q}$ and continuous on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$.
  • Irrationals $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$: By the Baire Category Theorem, the set of irrational numbers is a $G_\delta$ set (a countable intersection of open sets) that is NOT an $F_\sigma$ set!

🤯 Fundamental Theorem:

There exists NO function $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ that is continuous at every rational number and discontinuous at every irrational number!
